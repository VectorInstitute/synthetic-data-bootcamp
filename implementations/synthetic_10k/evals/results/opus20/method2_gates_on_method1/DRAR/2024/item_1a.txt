Item 1A. Risk Factors

An investment in our securities involves a high degree of risk. You should carefully consider the risks described below, together with the other information in this report, including Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations and our Consolidated Financial Statements and related notes. Any of these risks could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows and the trading price of our common stock. The risks described below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks not presently known to us, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also impair our business.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Changes in the prices of crude oil, natural gas, NGLs and refined products can significantly affect our revenue, margins and cash flows.

A substantial portion of our reported revenue reflects the gross value of commodities that we purchase and resell through our marketing activities. As a result, our revenue and cost of revenue move closely with commodity prices, and the spread between them is narrow relative to the size of either line. In fiscal 2024, cost of revenue grew faster than revenue, and gross profit and gross margin declined compared with fiscal 2023 despite higher reported revenue. Because the spread is narrow, small changes in purchase costs, sales prices, transportation costs or product quality differentials can have a disproportionate effect on our gross profit and operating income.

Commodity prices are volatile and are determined by factors outside our control. These factors include domestic and global supply and demand, the production decisions of major oil-producing nations, the level of domestic drilling and completion activity, weather, storage inventories, the availability of pipeline and export capacity, government policies affecting production or trade, armed conflicts and political instability, the pace of adoption of alternative energy sources, and general economic conditions. Sustained low or volatile prices could reduce the volumes delivered to our systems, compress our marketing spreads, lower the value of commodities we retain under certain processing contracts and increase the risk that our customers fail to perform.

Our results depend on the drilling and production activity of producers in the basins we serve, which we do not control.

Our gathering, processing, fractionation and liquids transportation businesses rely on producers continuing to drill and complete wells connected to our systems. Production from any individual well declines over time, so maintaining or increasing throughput requires a continuing program of new well connections. Producers' decisions to drill depend on commodity prices, their access to capital, their cost structures, the productivity of their acreage, regulatory requirements and their own strategic priorities, including pressure from investors to return capital rather than reinvest in growth.

Acreage dedications and minimum volume commitments mitigate, but do not eliminate, this exposure. Dedications do not obligate producers to drill, and minimum volume commitments expire, may be renegotiated, or may be uncollectible if the counterparty experiences financial distress. A sustained reduction in drilling activity in one or more of our core basins could reduce throughput across several of our segments at the same time, because volumes that enter our gathering systems also feed our processing plants, fractionation complexes and downstream pipelines.

Our percentage-of-proceeds, keep-whole and marketing arrangements expose us to direct commodity price and spread risk.

A portion of our Gathering and Processing segment revenue is earned under percentage-of-proceeds and keep-whole arrangements, under which our compensation varies with the value of natural gas and NGLs. Under keep-whole arrangements, we may bear the cost of replacing the heating value of NGLs extracted from the gas stream, which can be unprofitable when NGL prices are low relative to natural gas prices. Our Liquids Logistics and Marketing segment captures location, quality and time spreads that can narrow or disappear because of new pipeline capacity, changes in regional supply and demand, or market dislocations.

Our hedging activities may not be effective in offsetting these exposures. Hedges may cover only a portion of our volumes, may not correlate precisely with the prices we realize, may require us to post collateral that reduces our liquidity, and may prevent us from benefiting fully from favorable price movements. Our risk management policies may not detect or prevent unauthorized trading or positions that exceed approved limits, which could result in significant losses.

We may be unable to renew or replace expiring contracts on favorable terms, or at all.

Many of our transportation, storage, gathering and processing agreements have fixed terms. When these contracts expire, customers may elect not to renew, may seek to reduce contracted capacity, or may demand lower rates, shorter terms or fewer volume commitments. Our ability to recontract depends on the availability of competing infrastructure, the supply and demand outlook in the relevant markets, the creditworthiness and strategic plans of our customers, and regulatory constraints on our rates. If we recontract at lower rates or with less firm capacity, or if capacity remains unsubscribed, our revenue and cash flows could decline even if our operating costs do not.

We face significant competition, which could reduce our volumes, rates and margins.

We compete with other midstream companies, integrated energy companies, producer-owned gathering and processing systems, private equity-backed developers and marketing and trading firms. Some competitors have greater financial resources, lower costs of capital, newer assets or access to supply sources that we lack. Competition is based on rates, service quality, system reliability, interconnectivity, available capacity and the ability to offer integrated services. New pipelines and processing plants built by competitors in our core basins, or alternative transportation methods such as rail and truck, could divert volumes from our systems and pressure our rates and marketing margins.

We are exposed to the credit risk of our customers and other counterparties.

Our customers include producers, marketers, refiners, petrochemical manufacturers, utilities, power generators and exporters, many of whom are themselves exposed to commodity price volatility. Some of our customers are highly leveraged or have below-investment-grade credit ratings. During periods of low prices or tight credit markets, customers may be unable to pay amounts owed, may seek to reject or renegotiate contracts in bankruptcy proceedings, or may be unable to provide adequate credit support. Our accounts receivable and other current assets are significant, and losses from counterparty nonperformance, including counterparties to our hedging and marketing transactions, could adversely affect our results and liquidity.

Long-term changes in energy demand, including the transition to lower-carbon energy sources, could reduce demand for our services and the useful lives of our assets.

Government policies, technological developments, investor preferences and consumer behavior are encouraging greater energy efficiency and the use of renewable energy, electrification and other alternatives to hydrocarbons. Over time, these trends could reduce demand for crude oil, natural gas, NGLs and refined products, lower the volumes moving through our systems, shorten the economic lives of our pipelines, plants and terminals, and increase the risk of asset impairments. Our property, plant and equipment represents the large majority of our total assets, and much of it has long expected service lives. Our efforts to develop infrastructure related to carbon capture, renewable natural gas and hydrogen are at an early stage. They may not generate returns comparable to our existing businesses or offset declines in our traditional services.

Our growth and expansion projects may not be completed on schedule or within budget, and may not generate expected returns.

We invest significant capital in new pipelines, processing plants, fractionators, storage and export facilities and in expansions of existing systems. In fiscal 2024, our capital expenditures and other investing outflows significantly exceeded our cash provided by operating activities. These projects are subject to risks that include permitting delays, legal challenges, landowner opposition, cost inflation for steel, equipment and labor, contractor performance, supply chain disruptions, adverse weather and changes in customer demand after we commit capital. Projects often require years to complete, and revenue generally does not begin until they are placed in service. If we experience cost overruns or delays, or if contracted volumes fail to materialize, we may not recover our investment, and our leverage could increase without a corresponding increase in cash flow.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Our operations are subject to hazards that could result in personal injury, loss of life, environmental damage and significant financial loss, and our insurance may not be adequate.

Our pipelines, processing plants, fractionators, storage caverns, terminals and marine facilities handle flammable, explosive and toxic substances under high pressure. These operations involve risks of fires, explosions, ruptures, leaks, releases of hydrogen sulfide and other hazardous materials, cavern failures, equipment malfunctions, vehicle and vessel accidents and other incidents. Such events could result in injury or death, damage to property and natural resources, evacuation of nearby communities, regulatory penalties, litigation, suspension of operations and lasting damage to our reputation and relationships with regulators and communities.

We do not insure against every risk, and our insurance is subject to deductibles, retentions, sublimits and exclusions. Insurance coverage for certain risks, including pollution, named windstorms and cyber incidents, has become more costly and more limited, and we may choose to self-insure a larger portion of these risks. An uninsured or underinsured event could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition and results of operations.

The age and condition of portions of our infrastructure may require substantial unplanned maintenance and integrity expenditures.

Our asset base is extensive and includes pipeline segments, compressor stations and processing facilities that have been in service for many years. Aging assets may be more susceptible to corrosion, mechanical failure and other integrity problems. Our depreciation and amortization expense is large relative to our operating income, reflecting the capital intensity of our business, and maintaining and replacing assets requires continuing investment. Integrity assessments, new regulatory requirements or incidents on our systems or those of other operators could require us to repair, replace or reduce the operating pressure of facilities. Such actions could raise costs, reduce throughput and lower revenue.

Cyberattacks and other disruptions of our information and operational technology systems could disrupt operations and harm our business.

We rely on information technology and industrial control systems, including supervisory control and data acquisition systems, to operate our pipelines and facilities, schedule and measure volumes, process transactions and maintain financial records. These systems are targets of increasingly sophisticated attacks by criminal organizations, nation-state actors and others, including ransomware and attacks directed at critical energy infrastructure. A successful attack, or a failure of our systems or those of our vendors and interconnecting parties, could disrupt the flow of commodities, compromise safety systems, expose confidential data, result in regulatory penalties under federal security directives and cause significant remediation costs and liability. Our safeguards may not prevent or detect every intrusion.

Severe weather, natural disasters and the physical effects of climate change could damage our facilities and disrupt operations.

Our assets are located in regions exposed to hurricanes, flooding, extreme cold, extreme heat, wildfires, drought and earthquakes. Severe weather can damage facilities, interrupt power supplies needed for compression and processing, curtail producer volumes, disrupt marine and rail logistics and impair customers' ability to receive deliveries. Extreme cold events have in the past caused widespread freeze-offs of production and failures of power and gas infrastructure across the industry. Changes in the frequency and severity of such events could increase our operating and capital costs, raise insurance premiums and reduce the reliability of our systems.

Our large workforce exposes us to labor cost pressures, labor relations risks and difficulties in attracting and retaining qualified personnel.

We employ about 202,000 people, and labor is a significant component of both our cost of revenue and our selling, general and administrative expense. Wage inflation, rising benefit costs, competition for skilled technicians, engineers and operators, and changes in labor laws could increase our costs. Portions of our workforce may be represented by labor unions, and work stoppages, strikes or difficult negotiations could disrupt operations. The loss of key management or experienced operating personnel, or our inability to transfer knowledge as experienced employees retire, could impair our ability to operate safely and execute our strategy.

We depend on third-party pipelines, facilities, suppliers and service providers.

Our systems interconnect with pipelines, processing plants, fractionators, refineries, export terminals, railroads and power suppliers that we do not own or control. Outages, capacity constraints, quality specification changes or curtailments at these third-party facilities could limit our ability to receive or deliver volumes. We also rely on a limited number of suppliers for specialized equipment such as compressors, turbines, line pipe and processing components. Supply chain disruptions, tariffs and trade restrictions could increase costs or delay maintenance and growth projects.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

Our substantial indebtedness and the level of our interest expense have resulted in net losses and could limit our flexibility and our ability to meet our obligations.

We have a substantial amount of long-term debt, and our total liabilities represent the large majority of our total assets. In each of fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023, our interest expense exceeded our operating income, and as a result we reported a loss before income taxes and a net loss in both years. Although operating income improved in fiscal 2024 and our net loss narrowed, we cannot assure you that we will achieve or sustain profitability. If operating income does not grow faster than our interest expense, we will continue to report net losses, and our stockholders' equity could erode over time.

Our level of indebtedness could, among other things:

require us to dedicate a substantial portion of our cash flow from operations to interest and principal payments, reducing funds available for capital expenditures, acquisitions and other purposes; increase our vulnerability to adverse economic, industry and commodity price conditions; limit our flexibility to plan for or react to changes in our business and competitive environment; place us at a disadvantage relative to competitors that are less leveraged; and limit our ability to obtain additional financing on acceptable terms.

Rising interest rates and refinancing risk could increase our borrowing costs.

Our interest expense increased in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023, and our long-term debt also increased. Portions of our debt bear interest at variable rates, and our fixed-rate debt must be refinanced as it matures. If interest rates remain elevated or rise further, or if credit spreads for midstream issuers widen, the cost of refinancing maturing debt and funding new projects will increase. Because our interest expense already exceeds our operating income, further increases in borrowing costs would directly increase our net losses unless offset by higher operating income. Disruptions in the credit markets could also make refinancing difficult or impossible at the times we need it.

Our cash flow from operations has not been sufficient to fund our investing activities, and we depend on access to the debt and equity capital markets.

In fiscal 2024, our net cash used in investing activities substantially exceeded our net cash provided by operating activities. We funded the difference through additional borrowings and net issuances of equity securities. Our ability to continue funding growth capital and other investments depends on our continued access to the capital markets on acceptable terms. Market conditions, our financial performance, our credit ratings, investor sentiment toward the energy sector and investor preferences concerning fossil fuel infrastructure could restrict that access. If we cannot raise capital when needed, we may be required to defer or cancel growth projects, sell assets on unfavorable terms or take other actions that could harm our business. Further equity issuances would dilute the ownership interests of existing stockholders.

A downgrade in our credit ratings could increase our financing costs and collateral requirements.

Our credit ratings reflect the rating agencies' assessment of our leverage, interest coverage, business risk and other factors. Our net losses, high leverage and the coverage of our interest expense by operating income are factors the agencies consider. A downgrade could increase the interest rates on new borrowings and certain existing facilities, restrict our access to the commercial paper and capital markets, and trigger requirements to post letters of credit or cash collateral under commercial, hedging and regulatory arrangements. Any of these outcomes could reduce our liquidity at a time when we may need it most.

The agreements governing our indebtedness contain restrictive covenants.

Our credit facilities and indentures contain covenants that limit our ability to incur additional debt, grant liens, sell assets, make certain investments and enter into mergers or other fundamental transactions. Certain facilities require us to maintain specified leverage or interest coverage ratios. Our ability to comply with these covenants depends on our operating performance and may be affected by events beyond our control. A breach could result in an event of default, which could permit lenders to accelerate repayment and could trigger cross-defaults under other debt agreements. Any of these consequences could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition.

Our significant non-current liabilities could require cash outlays greater than we anticipate.

In addition to long-term debt, we carry a large balance of other non-current liabilities, which increased in fiscal 2024. These liabilities include, among other items, asset retirement obligations for pipelines, plants and storage facilities, environmental remediation liabilities, lease obligations, deferred credits and employee benefit obligations. Estimates of these liabilities depend on assumptions about future costs, timing, discount rates, regulatory requirements and actuarial factors. Changes in these assumptions or in applicable law could materially increase the amounts we must recognize and ultimately pay, which could reduce the cash available to service debt and fund operations.

We may be required to record impairments of our long-lived assets or goodwill.

We have substantial property, plant and equipment, goodwill and other non-current assets. We test these assets for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable, and we test goodwill at least annually. Declines in commodity prices or producer activity, the loss of significant customers, adverse regulatory decisions, increases in our cost of capital, changes in long-term demand expectations or sustained declines in our market capitalization could require us to record impairment charges. Impairment charges would not directly affect cash, but they would reduce our reported earnings and stockholders' equity and could affect covenant compliance and credit ratings.

We may not be able to realize the benefit of our tax attributes, and changes in tax laws could adversely affect us.

We did not record a provision for income taxes in fiscal 2024 or fiscal 2023, reflecting our pre-tax losses and our assessment of the realizability of deferred tax assets. Our ability to use net operating loss carryforwards and other tax attributes depends on our generating sufficient future taxable income. It may also be limited if we undergo an ownership change as defined under applicable tax rules, and equity issuances could contribute to such a change. Changes in federal or state tax laws, including changes to corporate tax rates, limits on interest deductibility, the treatment of capital expenditures and minimum taxes based on financial statement income, could increase our tax liabilities. Such changes could also reduce the value of our tax attributes.

We do not currently pay dividends, and we cannot assure you that we will do so in the future.

We did not pay dividends on our common stock in fiscal 2024. Any future decision to pay dividends will depend on our results of operations, cash flows, capital requirements, debt covenants, leverage objectives and other factors our board of directors considers relevant. Given our net losses, our investment program and our level of indebtedness, investors should not expect to receive dividends in the near term.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

Rate regulation by FERC and state agencies could limit our revenue and our ability to recover costs.

The rates, terms and conditions of service on our interstate natural gas pipelines and storage facilities, and on our interstate liquids pipelines, are subject to FERC regulation, and many of our intrastate assets are subject to state regulation. Regulators may initiate rate proceedings, customers may file complaints challenging our rates, and changes in regulatory policy, including on allowed returns, income tax allowances, index-based rate adjustments and depreciation, could reduce the rates we are permitted to charge. Regulatory lag may prevent us from recovering cost increases promptly. Gathering facilities that are reclassified as transmission could become subject to additional regulation.

We are subject to extensive pipeline safety regulation, and new requirements could increase our costs.

Our pipelines and storage facilities are subject to federal pipeline safety regulations and to state programs. These rules govern design, construction, testing, operation, maintenance and integrity management. The scope of these regulations has expanded in recent years to include additional gathering lines, more stringent integrity verification and maximum operating pressure reconfirmation requirements, leak detection and repair standards, and underground storage safety rules. Compliance may require significant capital expenditures, pressure reductions or operational changes. Violations can result in substantial civil penalties and orders requiring corrective action or shutdown.

Environmental laws and regulations impose significant costs and potential liabilities.

Our operations are subject to stringent federal, state and local laws governing air emissions, water discharges, waste management, the remediation of contaminated properties, the protection of wildlife and habitat, and the handling of hazardous substances. These laws may impose strict, joint and several liability for remediation costs and natural resource damages, regardless of fault, including for contamination caused by prior owners or operators of our facilities. Compliance requires permits that may be difficult to obtain or renew and that may contain onerous conditions. Changes in these laws, or more stringent enforcement, could increase our operating and capital costs and subject us to fines, penalties and third-party claims.

Climate change laws and regulations, including methane emission requirements, could increase our costs and reduce demand for our services.

Federal and state authorities have adopted and continue to consider measures to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. These include methane emission standards for oil and natural gas facilities, charges on methane emissions above specified thresholds, enhanced monitoring and reporting obligations, and state cap-and-trade and clean fuel programs. Compliance could require us to replace or retrofit equipment, expand leak detection programs, electrify compression and pay emissions charges. Measures that discourage hydrocarbon production or consumption could also reduce volumes on our systems. In addition, new climate-related disclosure requirements could increase compliance costs and expose us to claims regarding the accuracy of our disclosures.

Permitting challenges, opposition to infrastructure development and difficulty obtaining rights-of-way could delay or prevent our projects.

Construction and expansion of our facilities require federal, state and local permits and approvals, as well as rights-of-way from private landowners, government agencies and, in some cases, tribal authorities. Environmental organizations, landowners, local governments and others have increasingly challenged midstream projects through litigation, administrative proceedings and public opposition. These challenges include disputes over environmental reviews and the use of eminent domain. Such challenges have delayed, increased the cost of, or caused the cancellation of projects across the industry. Our existing rights-of-way may also be subject to expiration, renegotiation or legal challenge, which could increase our costs or disrupt existing operations.

We are subject to litigation and regulatory proceedings that could result in significant liabilities.

In the ordinary course of business, we are party to lawsuits, arbitrations, regulatory investigations and enforcement actions involving contract disputes, personal injury, property damage, environmental matters, royalty and pricing claims, employment matters and other issues. Given the size of our workforce and the scope of our operations, the number and variety of such matters is significant. The outcome of these proceedings is inherently uncertain. Adverse judgments or settlements could exceed our reserves and insurance coverage and could require changes in our business practices.

General Risks

Economic downturns and inflation could adversely affect our business.

A recession or slowdown in the United States or global economy could reduce demand for energy and petrochemical products, depress commodity prices, curtail producer activity and impair the financial condition of our customers. Inflation in labor, materials, equipment and services increases our operating and capital costs. Some of our contracts contain escalation provisions, and some of our regulated rates are indexed to inflation measures. However, these mechanisms may not fully or promptly offset cost increases, particularly because our cost of revenue is large relative to our gross profit.

Geopolitical events, terrorism and armed conflict could disrupt energy markets and our operations.

Wars, sanctions, trade disputes and political instability in energy-producing regions can cause sharp swings in commodity prices, alter global trade flows and affect demand for exports from our facilities. Energy infrastructure is a potential target for terrorist attacks and sabotage, both physical and cyber. Such events could damage our assets, disrupt operations, increase security and insurance costs and adversely affect financial markets and our access to capital.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to changes in commodity prices, our operating results, our ability to return to profitability, changes in our credit ratings, our capital-raising activities, announcements by competitors or customers, regulatory developments, and general market and economic conditions. Investor and lender policies that restrict investment in fossil fuel companies could reduce demand for our securities. Future issuances of common stock or securities convertible into common stock could depress the market price and dilute existing holders.

Our financial statements depend on significant estimates and judgments, and material weaknesses in internal control could affect investor confidence.

The preparation of our financial statements requires estimates related to asset useful lives, asset retirement obligations, impairment assessments, revenue recognition, derivative valuation, environmental liabilities and deferred tax asset realizability, among others. Actual results could differ materially from these estimates. If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, or if we identify a material weakness, we may be unable to report our results accurately or on time. This could harm investor confidence and the market price of our common stock and expose us to regulatory action.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could discourage a takeover that stockholders may consider favorable.

As a Delaware corporation, we are subject to provisions of Delaware law that may restrict certain business combinations with interested stockholders. Our certificate of incorporation and bylaws contain provisions that could delay or prevent a change in control, including advance notice requirements for stockholder proposals and the ability of our board of directors to issue preferred stock without stockholder approval. These provisions could limit the price that investors may be willing to pay for our common stock in the future.
