Item 1A. Risk Factors

Investing in our securities involves risk. Before making an investment decision, readers should carefully consider the risks and uncertainties described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 7, Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations, and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8. Any of the following risks, individually or together, could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows or the trading price of our common stock. The risks described below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks that we do not currently know of, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also impair our business. The order in which the risks are presented does not indicate their relative likelihood or potential magnitude.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Demand for our products is cyclical, and a downturn in the end markets we serve could significantly reduce our revenue and profitability. Purchases of construction and mining machinery, engines, power generation equipment and automation systems are capital investments. Customers often defer them when economic conditions weaken, credit tightens or their own revenue declines. Our sales depend on residential and nonresidential construction activity, public infrastructure investment, mining and quarrying capital programs, energy production and transmission, data center and commercial construction, and manufacturing and logistics capital spending. Each of these is sensitive to interest rates, business confidence, commodity prices and overall economic growth.

During a downturn, customers and dealers may postpone new equipment purchases, extend the operating lives of existing fleets, reduce rental fleet investment and draw down existing inventories before placing new orders. These responses can amplify the decline in our shipments relative to the decline in end-user activity. Because a meaningful portion of our cost base consists of manufacturing capacity, engineering resources and selling and administrative functions that cannot be reduced quickly, lower volumes would likely reduce our gross and operating margins more than proportionately. Our recent revenue and operating income growth should not be taken as an indication of future results.

Volatility in commodity prices could reduce demand from our mining, quarrying and energy customers. A significant portion of the demand for our large mining equipment, off-highway trucks, compression packages and engines comes from customers whose investment decisions depend on the prices of metals, minerals, coal, aggregates, crude oil and natural gas. When commodity prices fall or are expected to remain depressed, mining companies and energy producers typically reduce exploration, delay or cancel new projects, idle equipment and cut maintenance budgets. Those actions reduce demand for both new equipment and aftermarket parts and services. Commodity price movements can be abrupt and are driven by global supply and demand, geopolitical developments, currency movements and investor sentiment, none of which we control. Over the longer term, shifts in energy policy and in the global energy mix could also structurally reduce demand for equipment used in the extraction and transport of certain fossil fuels.

Changes in government infrastructure spending and public policy could adversely affect demand for our construction equipment. Governmental bodies at the federal, state, local and foreign levels fund a substantial share of road, bridge, water, transit and utility projects, and therefore drive a substantial share of demand in our Construction and Resource Equipment segment. Infrastructure funding is subject to legislative appropriation, budget constraints, political priorities and administrative delays. Funds authorized under multi-year programs may be disbursed more slowly than expected, redirected to other purposes or reduced in future budget cycles. Delays in project approvals, environmental permitting or procurement can also defer equipment purchases by the contractors who perform public works. A reduction in public infrastructure investment, or a slower pace of spending than our customers and dealers anticipate, could reduce our sales and lead to excess inventory in our distribution channel.

Growth in our Power and Energy Systems segment depends in part on continued investment in data centers and distributed power, which may not be sustained. In recent periods, demand for our generator sets, gas turbines, energy storage integration and related controls has benefited from the construction of data centers and from customers' increased focus on reliable backup and prime power. This demand is concentrated among a relatively small number of large developers and operators, and their investment plans can change quickly because of technological developments, financing conditions, power grid availability, permitting constraints or shifts in their own business strategies. If data center construction slows, if customers adopt alternative power architectures that rely less on our products, or if competitors expand capacity faster than demand grows, we could face order cancellations, pricing pressure and underutilized manufacturing capacity in this segment. Capacity investments we make in anticipation of continued demand may not produce the returns we expect.

We operate in highly competitive markets, and competitive pressures could reduce our market share, pricing and margins. We compete with large multinational equipment manufacturers, regional and specialized producers, and a growing number of competitors based in lower-cost countries. Some of these competitors may have greater resources in particular product lines or geographies, lower manufacturing costs, more favorable access to government support or financing, or more aggressive pricing strategies. In industrial automation, we also compete with technology companies and software providers that may move more quickly in robotics, artificial intelligence and controls. Competitors may introduce products with better performance, lower total cost of ownership or more advanced digital features, or may use pricing and financing incentives to gain share during weak demand periods. Because cost of revenue represents the large majority of our revenue, even modest price reductions can have a significant effect on our profitability. If we cannot differentiate our products, maintain our dealer network's service advantage and manage our cost structure, our results could be adversely affected.

If we fail to keep pace with technological change, including electrification, autonomy and digital connectivity, our products could become less competitive. Our industry is undergoing significant technological change. Customers increasingly expect alternative and lower-emission powertrains, battery-electric and hybrid machines, autonomous and semi-autonomous operation, advanced operator assistance, and integrated data platforms that support predictive maintenance and fleet optimization. Developing these technologies requires sustained investment in research and development, software talent and testing infrastructure. The outcome of those investments is uncertain, and they may take longer, cost more or achieve less market acceptance than we anticipate. Competing technologies, industry standards or customer preferences may develop in ways that make our chosen approaches less attractive. New entrants, including well-funded technology companies, may disrupt traditional equipment business models. If we underinvest, invest in the wrong technologies or are slow to commercialize new offerings, we could lose market share. If we invest heavily in technologies that do not achieve broad adoption, we may be unable to recover our costs.

We depend on our independent dealers and distributors, and their performance and financial condition significantly affect our results. A substantial portion of our sales, and most of our aftermarket parts and service activity, flows through independently owned dealers and distributors. We do not control their operations, and their decisions regarding inventory levels, rental fleet investment, pricing, staffing and capital expenditures directly affect our sales. Dealers may increase or reduce inventory based on their expectations of end-user demand, which can cause our shipments to diverge from retail activity and make our results more volatile. A dealer's financial difficulty, change of ownership, loss of key personnel or failure to meet our service standards could harm customer relationships and our reputation in that territory. Replacing a dealer can be costly, time-consuming and disruptive. Consolidation among dealers could also increase their bargaining power. In addition, competitors may seek to attract our dealers or to build competing distribution in our dealers' territories.

Our international operations subject us to economic, political, currency and trade risks. We manufacture, source and sell products in many countries across North America, Latin America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Asia-Pacific region. Our international business is subject to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates, which affect the translated value of our foreign revenue and costs and the relative competitiveness of products manufactured in different countries. It is also subject to changes in tariffs, quotas, export controls, sanctions and other trade restrictions; to local content and investment requirements; to restrictions on the repatriation of earnings; to differing labor regulations; to political instability, armed conflict and terrorism; and to the difficulty of enforcing contracts and protecting intellectual property in certain jurisdictions. Escalating trade tensions between major economies could raise our input costs, disrupt supply chains that cross national borders, provoke retaliatory measures against our products or reduce demand in affected markets. Our efforts to hedge currency exposures or to mitigate trade-related costs may not be effective.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Increases in the cost or reduced availability of raw materials, components and energy could reduce our margins. Our products require large quantities of steel, cast iron, aluminum, copper and other metals, as well as engines and drivetrain components, hydraulics, electronic controls, semiconductors, batteries, tires and other purchased parts. Because cost of revenue accounts for most of our revenue, our profitability is highly sensitive to changes in material, component, freight and energy costs. Prices for these inputs can rise sharply because of global demand, supply disruptions, trade measures, inflation and geopolitical events. We may be unable to raise our prices quickly enough, or by enough, to offset higher costs, particularly where competitive conditions are intense or where we have committed to fixed prices under existing orders and dealer programs. Some components, including certain electronic and engineered parts, come from a limited number of suppliers or from single sources. Shortages of these components could halt production of finished equipment even when other inputs are available.

Disruptions in our supply chain or manufacturing operations could prevent us from meeting customer demand. Our manufacturing network relies on a complex, multi-tier supply base and on the timely movement of materials, components and finished goods across regions. Supplier financial distress, quality failures, capacity constraints, labor disputes, natural disasters, pandemics, transportation bottlenecks, cyberattacks or geopolitical events could interrupt the flow of parts to our factories. Many of our products are built at a limited number of facilities that have specialized tooling and skilled workforces, so a significant disruption at any one of them, whether caused by fire, severe weather, equipment failure, a utility outage or another event, could be difficult to remedy quickly. Production interruptions can lead to late deliveries, contractual penalties, lost sales, higher expedited freight and overtime costs, and damage to our relationships with dealers and customers. Our insurance may not fully cover the resulting losses.

Our significant investment in inventories exposes us to the risk of obsolescence, write-downs and reduced cash flow. Inventories represent the largest component of our current assets. We carry substantial inventories of raw materials, work in process, finished machines and replacement parts to support long production cycles, global distribution and the aftermarket needs of our installed base. If demand weakens unexpectedly, if product transitions or regulatory changes make existing models less marketable, or if we misjudge the mix of products customers want, we could be left with excess or obsolete inventory. In that case we may need to reduce production, offer discounts or record write-downs, any of which would reduce our margins. Building inventory in anticipation of demand also consumes operating cash, which could constrain other uses of capital. Conversely, holding too little inventory could cause us to miss sales opportunities when demand strengthens.

Engineered-to-order projects and long-term service commitments expose us to cost overruns and execution risk. Certain products in our Power and Energy Systems and Industrial Automation segments, as well as large mining equipment and autonomy deployments, are designed to customer specifications. They often involve long lead times, multi-year delivery schedules, integration with customer facilities, and performance or availability guarantees. Pricing for these arrangements is generally established before all costs are known. If our estimates of material, labor, engineering or commissioning costs prove inaccurate, if inflation rises faster than anticipated, or if technical difficulties or customer-caused delays occur, our margins on these projects could fall or the projects could become loss-making. Failure to meet contractual delivery dates or performance specifications may result in liquidated damages, warranty claims, contract termination and reputational harm. Long-term service agreements likewise depend on assumptions about equipment reliability and maintenance costs that may prove incorrect.

Product quality issues, warranty claims and product liability claims could result in significant costs and damage our reputation. Our products are complex, operate in demanding environments and are often critical to our customers' operations and safety. Design or manufacturing defects, failures of purchased components or software errors in connected and autonomous systems could cause equipment failures, property damage, personal injury or death. Such events could lead to warranty costs, product recalls, field retrofit campaigns, regulatory investigations and product liability litigation. Our warranty reserves are based on historical experience and engineering estimates, and actual costs may exceed them, particularly for new technologies with limited field history. Claims involving autonomous operation or safety-critical automation may raise novel liability questions. Significant quality problems could also damage our brand reputation for durability and reliability, which is central to our competitive position.

Cybersecurity incidents affecting our information systems or connected products could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability. We rely on information technology systems to design and manufacture products, manage our supply chain, process orders and transactions, and communicate with dealers, suppliers and customers. Most of our new machines and power systems also transmit operating data to our cloud-based platforms, and some of our products can be monitored, updated or controlled remotely. These systems and products are potential targets for cyberattacks, ransomware, malicious insiders and other threats, and the threats continue to grow in sophistication. A successful attack could interrupt our manufacturing operations, compromise confidential and proprietary information or personal data, impair the functioning or safety of customer equipment, or allow unauthorized access to customer sites. Any of these could result in significant remediation costs, legal claims, regulatory penalties and loss of customer trust. Our security measures, and those of the third-party service providers on which we depend, may not prevent every incident, and our insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

Our success depends on attracting and retaining skilled employees and on maintaining constructive relations with our workforce. We employ approximately 126,100 people worldwide. Our operations depend on skilled production workers, welders, machinists, service technicians, engineers and software developers, many of whom are in high demand across the industrial and technology sectors. Labor shortages, wage inflation and competition for talent, particularly in software, controls, electrification and data science, could increase our costs and slow our product development. A portion of our workforce is covered by collective bargaining agreements or is represented by works councils, and many of our dealers face similar labor dynamics. Strikes, work stoppages or difficult contract negotiations at our facilities, our suppliers or our dealers could disrupt production and service and increase our costs. The loss of key members of senior management or technical leadership, or failure to plan effectively for their succession, could also adversely affect our business.

Acquisitions, divestitures and strategic investments may not achieve their intended benefits, and we may be required to record impairment charges. As part of our strategy, we pursue selective acquisitions, joint ventures and investments that complement our capabilities, particularly in technology, automation and services. These transactions carry risks that include overpaying for assets, failing to integrate operations, systems and personnel, failing to retain key employees and customers of acquired businesses, failing to realize projected revenue or cost synergies, and assuming unknown liabilities. Goodwill and other intangible and long-lived assets represent a significant portion of our total assets. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually, and more often when events indicate its value may not be recoverable. A sustained decline in the performance or outlook of a reporting unit, adverse changes in market conditions or an increase in discount rates could require us to record significant non-cash impairment charges, which would reduce our reported earnings and stockholders' equity.

Risks Related to Financing and Capital Structure

Our current liabilities exceed our current assets and our cash balances are modest relative to the size of our operations, so we depend on continued strong operating cash flow to meet our obligations. As of the end of fiscal 2024, our total current liabilities exceeded our total current assets, and our cash and cash equivalents were small relative to our revenue, our current liabilities and our annual capital deployment. We manage liquidity with the expectation that cash generated from operations, together with access to short-term borrowing markets and credit facilities, will be sufficient to meet our working capital, capital expenditure, debt service and capital return needs. If operating cash flow declined materially because of lower demand, margin pressure, inventory build-up or slower collection of receivables, we could be required to borrow more, reduce capital investment, curtail share repurchases or dividends, or seek financing on less favorable terms. A working capital deficit also leaves us less able to absorb unexpected cash demands, such as large legal settlements, tax payments or customer defaults, without external funding.

Our capital return program reduces the cash available to absorb adverse developments, and we may reduce or suspend it. In fiscal 2024 we returned a substantial amount of capital to stockholders through share repurchases and dividends. Together these exceeded the cash we retained after funding capital expenditures and other investing activities. Repurchases have reduced our share count and supported earnings per share, but they have also limited the growth of our cash balances and stockholders' equity. Our Board of Directors may change, reduce or suspend repurchases or dividends at any time based on our financial condition, cash flow, capital requirements, market conditions and other factors. A reduction in capital returns could adversely affect the trading price of our common stock. Conversely, if we continue to return capital at high levels during a period of weakening performance, our financial flexibility could be reduced at a time when we need it most.

Changes in interest rates and in the availability of credit could increase our financing costs and reduce demand for our products. Although our long-term debt is modest relative to our earnings and total capitalization, we rely on access to commercial paper, bank credit and other short-term financing to manage seasonal and cyclical working capital needs. Disruptions in credit markets, a downgrade of our credit ratings or rising interest rates could increase our borrowing costs or limit our access to funding when we need it. Interest rates and credit conditions also strongly influence our customers and dealers, who frequently finance equipment purchases, rental fleets and inventory. Higher rates or tighter lending standards could cause customers to defer purchases, reduce the willingness of dealers to carry inventory, and reduce the availability of the retail financing and leasing programs that we support through third-party financial institutions. Because we do not operate a captive finance company, we have limited ability to offset tighter third-party credit with our own financing.

We are exposed to the credit risk of our dealers, distributors and customers. We extend credit to dealers, distributors and direct customers in the ordinary course of business and carry significant accounts receivable. Some of our customers operate in volatile industries such as mining, oil and gas, and construction contracting, where financial distress can arise quickly during commodity or construction downturns. In certain cases we also provide guarantees, residual value commitments or other support in connection with financing programs arranged with third parties. If a significant dealer or customer became insolvent or was unable to pay amounts owed to us on a timely basis, we could incur credit losses, higher collection costs and losses on financing support arrangements. Economic weakness in particular regions or industries could increase defaults across multiple counterparties at the same time.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

Increasingly stringent emissions, efficiency and environmental regulations could increase our costs and affect demand for our products. Our engines, machines and power generation equipment are subject to extensive and evolving regulation of exhaust emissions, greenhouse gases, noise, fuel efficiency and product safety in the United States, the European Union, China and other jurisdictions. Meeting new emissions standards typically requires significant engineering investment, product redesign, certification testing and the introduction of more complex and costly aftertreatment systems. Regulations differ across jurisdictions and change over time, which increases compliance costs and complicates global product planning. Customers sometimes accelerate purchases ahead of new standards and then reduce purchases afterward, creating volatility in demand. A failure to certify products on schedule, or a determination that our products do not comply with applicable requirements, could result in fines, recalls, sales restrictions and reputational harm. Policies that restrict or phase out fossil-fuel-powered equipment could also reduce demand for certain of our existing product lines faster than we can transition to alternatives.

We are subject to environmental, health and safety laws, and compliance costs and liabilities could be significant. Our manufacturing, remanufacturing and testing operations are subject to laws and regulations governing air emissions, wastewater discharges, the handling and disposal of hazardous materials, workplace safety and the remediation of contaminated sites. We have been, and may in the future be, identified as a potentially responsible party for the investigation and cleanup of sites we currently or formerly owned or operated, or to which we sent waste. Under some laws such liability can be imposed without regard to fault. The discovery of previously unknown contamination, stricter enforcement, changes in regulations or new climate-related disclosure and reporting requirements could require significant expenditures. Violations could result in fines, penalties, operating restrictions or third-party claims.

We are subject to anti-corruption, export control, sanctions and trade compliance laws, and violations could result in significant penalties. Our global operations require us to comply with anti-bribery laws in the United States and other countries, export control and economic sanctions regulations, customs requirements, and laws governing data privacy and competition. Certain of our products and technologies, including engines, turbines, autonomy systems and industrial controls, may be subject to export licensing requirements or to restrictions on sales to particular countries, entities or end uses. Sales through independent dealers, distributors and agents in higher-risk jurisdictions increase the risk of improper conduct by third parties for which we could be held responsible. Rapid changes in sanctions programs and export restrictions may require us to cease or restructure business in affected markets on short notice. Violations could result in substantial fines, criminal penalties, loss of export privileges, debarment from government contracts and reputational damage.

Changes in tax laws or adverse outcomes of tax examinations could increase our effective tax rate and reduce our earnings. We are subject to income and other taxes in the United States and in numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate increased in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023 and could continue to fluctuate because of changes in the geographic mix of our earnings, changes in the valuation of deferred tax assets and liabilities, and changes in tax laws, regulations and interpretations. The implementation of global minimum tax rules, changes to United States international tax provisions, and new digital services or other taxes could increase our tax burden and compliance costs. We are regularly examined by tax authorities, and the outcome of those examinations may differ from the amounts we have provided for uncertain tax positions. Higher tariffs and customs duties, while not income taxes, would also increase our costs.

We are involved in legal proceedings, and adverse outcomes could have a material effect on our results. In the ordinary course of business, we are party to lawsuits, claims and governmental proceedings involving product liability, warranty, intellectual property, commercial and dealer disputes, employment matters, environmental issues and other subjects. Litigation is inherently unpredictable, and the costs of defending claims, along with any judgments or settlements, could be significant and may not be covered by insurance. Some proceedings may seek injunctive relief that could restrict the sale of particular products or require changes to our business practices. Regulatory investigations can also divert management attention and harm our reputation regardless of their outcome.

Our failure to protect our intellectual property, or claims that we infringe the intellectual property of others, could harm our competitive position. We rely on patents, trademarks, trade secrets, copyrights and contractual protections to safeguard our designs, manufacturing processes, software and brand. These protections may be challenged, invalidated or circumvented, and the laws of some countries in which we operate offer weaker protection than those of the United States. Competitors and counterfeiters may copy our products or sell imitation replacement parts, which can reduce our aftermarket revenue and expose customers to safety and performance risks that may be wrongly attributed to us. As our products incorporate more software, connectivity and autonomy technology, we face a greater risk of infringement claims from third parties, including entities whose primary business is asserting patents. Defending such claims could be costly and could require us to obtain licenses, redesign products or stop selling affected offerings.

General Risks

Global economic, geopolitical and public health events could adversely affect our business. Our results are influenced by global economic conditions and by events beyond our control. These include recessions, inflation, financial market disruptions, armed conflicts, terrorism, political instability, pandemics and other public health emergencies. Such events can reduce customer demand, disrupt supply chains and logistics, cause sudden changes in commodity and energy prices, affect currency exchange rates and restrict our ability to operate in certain regions. The effects of any of these events on our business are difficult to predict and could persist for extended periods.

Climate change and severe weather events could disrupt our operations and those of our suppliers, dealers and customers. Many of our manufacturing facilities, suppliers and customer operations are located in areas exposed to hurricanes, flooding, wildfires, extreme heat, earthquakes and other natural hazards, some of which may become more frequent or severe because of climate change. Such events could damage facilities, interrupt production and logistics, and reduce customer activity in construction and mining. Evolving stakeholder expectations regarding climate-related matters, including demands for disclosure, emissions reductions and product transitions, may increase our costs. If we fail to meet those expectations, our reputation and access to capital could suffer.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile. The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to actual or anticipated changes in our results or outlook, conditions in the industrial, construction, mining and energy sectors, changes in securities analysts' estimates, announcements by competitors, changes in our capital return policies, and general market and economic conditions. Many of these factors are outside our control and may be unrelated to our operating performance. Because our results are cyclical, investor perceptions of where we are in the economic cycle can also cause significant movements in our stock price.
