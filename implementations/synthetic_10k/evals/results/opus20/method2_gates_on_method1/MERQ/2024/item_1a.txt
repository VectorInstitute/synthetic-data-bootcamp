Item 1A. Risk Factors

An investment in our common stock involves a high degree of risk. You should consider carefully the risks and uncertainties described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report, including our Consolidated Financial Statements and the related notes and "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations." If any of these risks occurs, alone or in combination, our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows and liquidity could be materially and adversely affected. In that event, the market price of our common stock could decline, and you could lose some or all of your investment.

This section contains forward-looking statements, which are subject to the cautionary language under "Cautionary Note Regarding Forward-Looking Statements" elsewhere in this Annual Report. The risks below are organized by category for convenience. The order in which they appear does not indicate their relative likelihood or the magnitude of their potential effect. Other risks and uncertainties that we have not identified, or that we currently believe are not material, could also harm us.

Summary of Risk Factors

The following summarizes the principal risks that could adversely affect our business, financial condition and results of operations:

Demand for office space continues to be weakened by hybrid and remote work arrangements, which could reduce occupancy and rental rates in our office portfolio. Our results depend on economic and real estate conditions in the metropolitan markets where our properties are located. We face intense competition for tenants, residents, services clients and investment opportunities. Our residential leases are short-term, and new apartment supply in our submarkets could pressure rents and occupancy. The financial difficulties or bankruptcy of commercial tenants could reduce our revenue. Our third-party services revenue depends on agreements that clients can generally terminate on short notice. Our cost structure is largely fixed, our margins are narrow, and increases in operating costs could significantly reduce our profitability. Re-leasing space and keeping our properties competitive require substantial capital expenditures. Development and redevelopment activities expose us to construction, entitlement, financing and lease-up risks. Real estate is relatively illiquid, and our capital recycling strategy may not succeed. We may be required to record impairment charges on our real estate, goodwill or other long-lived assets. Our operating income has recently been only modestly greater than our interest expense, which leaves our earnings highly sensitive to changes in operating results or borrowing costs. We may be unable to refinance our debt on favorable terms, and our financing agreements contain covenants that restrict our operations. We have substantial long-term obligations in addition to our indebtedness. Our share repurchase program reduces our liquidity and may not enhance long-term stockholder value. As a taxable corporation, we are subject to income taxes, and changes in tax laws or property tax assessments could increase our costs. We are subject to extensive environmental, accessibility, building performance and housing regulations. Cybersecurity incidents or failures of our information or building technology systems could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Continued weakness in office demand resulting from hybrid and remote work could reduce occupancy, rental rates and the value of our office properties.

Office buildings represent a meaningful portion of our Commercial Properties segment. Many employers have adopted hybrid or remote work policies, and tenants are reassessing the amount, configuration and location of the space they lease. As leases expire, tenants may renew for less space, relocate to smaller or newer buildings, sublease excess space or vacate entirely. Sublease space marketed by other tenants competes directly with our vacant space and can depress effective rents in a submarket. During fiscal 2024, office market conditions in several of our markets remained challenging. If they do not improve, or deteriorate further, we may experience lower occupancy, reduced rental rates, larger tenant concession packages and longer downtime between leases.

Sustained weakness in office demand could also reduce the values of our office assets, impair our ability to sell them at acceptable prices, and lead to impairment charges. In addition, lenders and investors have reduced their exposure to the office sector, which could limit the availability and increase the cost of financing for office properties and depress the prices buyers are willing to pay.

Our results depend on economic and real estate conditions in the metropolitan markets where our properties are concentrated.

Our properties are located in metropolitan markets across the United States, and our revenue depends on local employment levels, population trends, business formation and the supply of competing space in those markets. A regional economic slowdown, the relocation or downsizing of major employers, changes in local tax or regulatory policy, rising crime or perceptions of reduced public safety, or declining use of public transportation in urban cores could reduce demand for our properties. To the extent a significant portion of our revenue is derived from a limited number of markets or submarkets, adverse developments in those areas could affect us disproportionately. A broader recession, prolonged high interest rates or reduced consumer spending could reduce demand across all of our property types and our services business at the same time.

We face significant competition for tenants, residents, services clients and investment opportunities.

We compete with numerous owners, operators and developers of office, industrial, retail and multifamily properties, many of which own properties near ours. Some of these competitors have greater financial resources, lower costs of capital or newer buildings. Competitors that own buildings with lower occupancy or that face financial distress may offer rental rates, free rent or tenant improvement allowances that we are unwilling or unable to match. In our services business, we compete with national, regional and local property management, leasing and development firms, some of which have broader geographic coverage or more established relationships with institutional owners. In acquiring and developing properties, we compete with public and private real estate companies, institutional investors and private equity funds, which may increase prices and reduce our expected returns. Any of these competitive pressures could reduce our occupancy, revenue, fee income and growth.

The short-term nature of our residential leases and increases in new apartment supply could pressure rental rates and occupancy in our Residential Communities segment.

Our residential leases generally have terms of approximately one year or less. As a result, our residential rental revenue is exposed to declines in market rents relatively quickly. Several of our markets have experienced elevated levels of new multifamily construction, and newly delivered communities frequently offer significant concessions during lease-up. That competing supply could limit our ability to raise rents, increase resident turnover, and require us to offer concessions or accept lower occupancy. Demand for our apartments could also decline if job growth slows, if residents shift toward single-family rentals or homeownership, or if household formation weakens. Higher turnover increases our costs to prepare, market and lease units.

The financial difficulties, bankruptcy or insolvency of commercial tenants could reduce our revenue and increase our costs.

We depend on commercial tenants to pay rent and to reimburse us for their share of property operating expenses. A tenant experiencing financial difficulty may delay or stop paying rent, seek rent concessions, decline to renew, or file for bankruptcy protection. In a bankruptcy proceeding, a tenant may reject its lease, and our claim for unpaid future rent may be limited and paid only in part, if at all. The loss of an anchor tenant at a retail center could reduce traffic to the center and allow other tenants to terminate their leases or reduce their rent under co-tenancy provisions. Retail tenants also continue to face competition from online commerce, and some industrial tenants are exposed to changes in supply chain strategy, trade policy and consumer demand. Re-leasing vacated space may take considerable time and require significant capital.

Our third-party services business depends on management and service agreements that clients can often terminate on short notice.

Our Real Estate Services and Development segment earns fees under property management, leasing, development and asset management agreements, many of which are cancelable by either party on relatively short notice. Clients may terminate or decline to renew these agreements if they sell the underlying properties, consolidate service providers, bring functions in-house or become dissatisfied with our performance. Fees based on property revenue or transaction value decline when rents, occupancy or transaction volumes fall. Our services business also exposes us to potential liability for errors in property operations, construction oversight or financial reporting performed on behalf of clients, and to conflicts of interest when we serve clients whose properties compete with our own.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Our cost structure is largely fixed, our margins are narrow, and increases in operating costs could significantly reduce our profitability.

A substantial majority of our revenue is consumed by property operating costs, the direct costs of providing services, and depreciation and amortization. Many of these costs, including real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs and maintenance, on-site labor and depreciation, do not decline proportionately when occupancy or revenue falls. As a result, a relatively small decline in revenue can produce a disproportionately larger decline in operating income. During fiscal 2024, our revenue declined modestly while our operating income declined at a significantly faster rate, and our operating margin remained narrow.

Operating costs have risen in recent years because of wage inflation, higher property insurance premiums, increased real estate tax assessments, and higher energy and materials prices. Our ability to recover these increases from tenants is limited. Residential leases generally do not provide for expense recovery. Some commercial leases cap recoverable expenses or use base years. Vacant space generates costs without corresponding reimbursements. Our selling, general and administrative expenses also make up a significant share of our revenue, and our efforts to reduce discretionary corporate spending may not offset increases elsewhere. If we cannot control costs or pass them through to tenants, our results of operations and cash flows could be materially and adversely affected.

Re-leasing space and keeping our properties competitive require substantial capital expenditures.

When leases expire or tenants vacate, we typically incur significant costs for tenant improvements, leasing commissions, free rent and other concessions, and these costs have increased in competitive markets, particularly for office space. Our properties also require ongoing capital investment to replace building systems, update amenities, improve energy efficiency and comply with evolving codes and tenant expectations. Older properties may become functionally obsolete or require substantial renovation to compete with newer buildings. If we do not have sufficient capital, or choose to defer these investments, our properties could become less attractive to tenants and residents, leading to lower occupancy and rental rates. Our capital expenditure requirements also reduce the cash available for debt reduction, share repurchases and new investment.

Our development and redevelopment activities expose us to construction, entitlement, financing and lease-up risks.

We develop multifamily and industrial properties, redevelop and reposition commercial assets, and entitle land for future use or sale. These activities involve risks that include construction cost overruns caused by inflation in labor and materials, contractor defaults, supply chain delays, failure to obtain zoning, permits or other governmental approvals on a timely basis or at all, and community opposition. Financing may not be available on acceptable terms, and completed projects may not lease up at expected rents or within expected time frames. Guaranteed maximum price contracts and pre-leasing do not eliminate these risks. We may abandon projects after incurring significant predevelopment costs, which we would then be required to expense. Development projects generally produce little or no revenue while under construction, and capitalized interest and other carrying costs may exceed our estimates.

Real estate investments are relatively illiquid, and our strategy of recycling capital through dispositions may not succeed.

Our strategy contemplates selling mature, non-core or lower-growth assets and redeploying the proceeds. Real estate is relatively illiquid, and our ability to sell properties quickly, or at prices we consider acceptable, depends on buyer demand, financing availability and conditions in the transaction markets, all of which are outside our control. Transaction volumes for certain property types, particularly office, have been depressed, and buyers have demanded significant price reductions. Contractual restrictions in leases, joint venture agreements, financing agreements or ground leases may limit our ability to sell certain properties. Because we are a taxable corporation, gains on sales may be subject to income tax, which reduces the net proceeds available for redeployment. If we cannot complete dispositions on favorable terms, we may be unable to fund our capital allocation priorities, reduce our exposure to weaker sectors or improve our portfolio quality.

We may be required to record impairment charges on our real estate, goodwill or other long-lived assets.

Property and equipment, goodwill and other non-current assets represent substantially all of our total assets. We evaluate real estate and other long-lived assets for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually. Declines in occupancy, rental rates or market values, increases in capitalization or discount rates, changes in our expected holding periods, or decisions to sell assets could require us to record impairment charges. The carrying amount of our goodwill declined during fiscal 2024. Further deterioration in office market conditions, a decline in the performance of the reporting units to which goodwill is assigned, or a sustained decline in the market price of our common stock could result in additional charges. Impairment charges do not affect our cash, but they could materially reduce our reported earnings and stockholders' equity and could affect compliance with financial covenants.

Our joint ventures subject us to risks associated with shared decision-making and reliance on our partners.

We own certain properties and conduct certain development activities through joint ventures with institutional and other partners. Our partners may have economic or business interests that differ from ours, and they may be able to block or delay decisions about financing, leasing, capital spending or sale of the venture's assets. A partner may experience financial distress and fail to fund its share of required capital, and we may choose or be required to fund those amounts ourselves. Disputes with partners could result in litigation or arbitration that increases our costs and distracts management. Buy-sell and other provisions in our joint venture agreements could require us to purchase our partner's interest, or sell our own, at a time or price that is not favorable to us.

Uninsured losses, natural disasters and the physical effects of climate change could damage our properties and increase our costs.

Our properties are exposed to damage from fires, severe storms, flooding, earthquakes, extreme heat and other natural or man-made events. Some of our markets are subject to a heightened risk of these events. Insurance for certain risks, including windstorm, flood, earthquake and terrorism, may be unavailable, available only at significantly higher cost, or subject to high deductibles and coverage limits. Property insurance premiums have increased materially in recent years. If we experience a loss that is uninsured or exceeds our policy limits, we could lose our investment in, and the anticipated revenue from, the affected property while remaining obligated for related debt and other obligations. Over time, the physical effects of climate change could also increase operating and capital costs, reduce demand for properties in affected areas, and lower property values.

Cybersecurity incidents or failures of our information or building technology systems could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.

We rely on information technology systems to manage leasing, rent collection, revenue management, accounting, payroll and our services business. We also use networked operational technology to control building systems such as heating, cooling, access control, elevators and life safety equipment. These systems, and those of our third-party vendors, are vulnerable to cyberattacks, ransomware, employee error, malfeasance and system failures. We collect and store personal information about residents, tenants, employees and clients, including financial information. A security breach could disrupt property operations, compromise building safety, result in the theft of funds or data, trigger notification obligations and regulatory penalties, and expose us to litigation and reputational harm. Our insurance may not fully cover these losses, and the costs of protecting our systems continue to increase.

Our success depends on our ability to attract and retain key personnel and a skilled workforce.

We depend on our senior management team and on experienced personnel in leasing, property operations, development, asset management and finance. We employ approximately 16,800 people, many of whom work in on-site property management, maintenance and engineering roles. Competition for these employees is intense, and labor shortages and wage inflation have increased our personnel costs and turnover. Some of our employees and contractors are, or may become, represented by labor unions, which could result in work stoppages or increased costs. The loss of key executives, or our inability to attract, train and retain qualified employees at reasonable cost, could impair our operations and our ability to execute our strategy.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

Our operating income has recently been only modestly greater than our interest expense, which leaves our earnings highly sensitive to changes in operating results or borrowing costs.

In fiscal 2024, interest expense consumed nearly all of our operating income, and our income before income taxes and our net income each declined substantially from the prior year. Because our margin of earnings above our fixed financing costs is narrow, a further decline in revenue, an increase in operating costs, impairment charges or higher interest rates could cause us to report pre-tax and net losses. A sustained period of low or negative earnings could adversely affect our credit ratings, our compliance with financial covenants, our ability to obtain financing on acceptable terms, and the market price of our common stock. Depreciation and amortization are significant non-cash charges, and our operating cash flow has substantially exceeded our net income. However, investors, lenders and rating agencies may focus on reported earnings and coverage ratios when they assess our financial condition.

We may be unable to refinance our indebtedness on favorable terms, and rising interest rates could increase our costs.

We have significant long-term debt, and we will need to refinance portions of it as it matures. Our ability to refinance depends on conditions in the credit markets, our financial performance, the value of any properties securing the debt, and lender appetite for exposure to our property types. Lenders have become more cautious about office collateral, and higher benchmark interest rates have increased the cost of new borrowings. If we refinance at higher rates, our interest expense will increase, which would further reduce our already narrow earnings. If we cannot refinance on acceptable terms, we may be forced to sell assets at unfavorable prices, use cash that we would otherwise allocate to other purposes, or, in the case of secured debt, lose the encumbered properties through foreclosure. To the extent any of our borrowings bear interest at variable rates, increases in market rates would raise our interest costs directly. Hedging arrangements may not fully protect us and expose us to counterparty risk.

Our financing agreements contain covenants that could restrict our operations and, if breached, could accelerate our obligations.

Our debt agreements contain financial covenants, which may include limits on leverage, minimum interest or fixed charge coverage and minimum unencumbered asset values, as well as operating covenants that restrict our ability to incur additional debt, grant liens, sell assets, make investments and repurchase stock. Declines in our operating income or asset values, or impairment charges, could make it more difficult to remain in compliance. A breach of these covenants could result in an event of default, which could permit lenders to accelerate repayment and, through cross-default provisions, trigger defaults under other agreements. Even if we remain in compliance, these covenants could limit our flexibility to respond to changing market conditions or to pursue attractive opportunities.

We have substantial long-term obligations in addition to our indebtedness.

Our other non-current liabilities are significantly larger than our long-term debt and represent the majority of our total liabilities. These obligations may include ground lease and other lease obligations, deferred income tax liabilities, security deposits, development and construction commitments, and other long-term liabilities. Ground leases may contain rent resets or escalations that increase our costs over time. Some ground leases also carry the risk that we lose the improvements on the property when the lease ends. These obligations reduce the cash flow available for operations, debt service and stockholder returns. They could also be considered by lenders and rating agencies in evaluating our leverage. A reassessment of the assumptions used to measure these liabilities could increase their reported amounts.

Our share repurchase program reduces our liquidity and may not enhance long-term stockholder value.

We repurchased a substantial amount of our common stock during fiscal 2024. The combined amount of our share repurchases and our purchases of property and equipment exceeded our net cash provided by operating activities, and we funded the difference in part with proceeds from asset dispositions and existing cash balances. Our cash and cash equivalents declined during the year. Continued repurchases at this level would reduce the liquidity available to fund capital expenditures, development, debt repayment and unanticipated needs. They could also depend on our continuing ability to complete dispositions. The timing and amount of repurchases are at the discretion of our board of directors, and we may suspend or discontinue the program at any time. Repurchases could increase volatility in the market price of our stock, and the price we pay may exceed the price at which our shares later trade. We do not currently pay dividends on our common stock, and stockholders may need to rely on stock price appreciation to realize a return on their investment.

Our access to capital depends on conditions in the financial markets and on our credit profile.

We rely on cash from operations, asset sales, bank credit facilities and capital markets transactions to fund our business. Disruptions in the debt or equity markets, a downgrade of our credit ratings, or negative investor sentiment toward real estate or our property types could increase our cost of capital or prevent us from obtaining financing when needed. If we issue additional equity to raise capital, existing stockholders would be diluted. If capital is unavailable on acceptable terms, we may be unable to refinance maturing debt, complete development projects, maintain our properties or execute our strategy.

Risks Related to Legal, Regulatory and Tax Matters

As a taxable corporation, we are subject to income taxes, and changes in tax laws or property tax assessments could increase our costs.

We have not elected to be taxed as a real estate investment trust. As a result, our earnings are subject to U.S. federal, state and local income taxes, and gains on property sales may be taxed at the corporate level. Our effective tax rate increased in fiscal 2024 compared with the prior year, and it may continue to fluctuate because of changes in the mix of our earnings, the timing of asset sales, non-deductible items, valuation allowances and changes in tax law. With pre-tax income at a low level, relatively small items can cause large swings in our effective tax rate. Changes in federal, state or local tax laws, including changes to depreciation rules, interest deductibility limitations or corporate tax rates, could increase our tax liability. If we were to seek to qualify as a real estate investment trust in the future, we would need to restructure our services and development businesses and satisfy distribution requirements, and we might be unable to do so on acceptable terms. Real estate taxes are among our largest operating costs, and reassessments, rate increases or the loss of tax abatements could raise them materially.

We could incur significant costs related to environmental matters.

Under federal, state and local environmental laws, a current or former owner or operator of real property may be liable for the investigation and remediation of hazardous substances at, on, under or migrating from the property. That liability can apply regardless of whether the owner knew of, or caused, the contamination. Some of our properties, particularly industrial facilities and retail centers that have housed dry cleaners or gasoline stations, may contain contamination, asbestos-containing materials, underground storage tanks or other environmental conditions. We could also be liable for conditions at sites where we sent waste for disposal. Environmental conditions could reduce the value of affected properties, limit our ability to sell or finance them, and expose us to claims for personal injury or property damage.

We are subject to accessibility, building performance, housing and other regulations that could increase our costs or restrict our operations.

Our properties must comply with the Americans with Disabilities Act, the Fair Housing Act, fire and life safety codes, and other federal, state and local laws. Noncompliance could result in fines, required modifications or private litigation. A growing number of jurisdictions have adopted building energy performance, emissions and benchmarking requirements that could require significant capital investment in building systems or result in penalties for noncompliance. Several jurisdictions have adopted or are considering rent control, rent stabilization, tenant protection or eviction restrictions that could limit our ability to raise residential rents or recover possession of units. Changes in zoning and land use regulations could restrict our development plans. Compliance with these laws, and with future laws that we cannot predict, could materially increase our operating and capital costs.

We are subject to litigation and regulatory scrutiny, including with respect to our residential pricing practices.

We are, from time to time, involved in legal proceedings arising in the ordinary course of business. These include tenant and resident disputes, personal injury claims, employment matters, construction disputes and claims by services clients. In addition, the use of data-driven revenue management tools in the multifamily industry has attracted scrutiny from government agencies and private plaintiffs alleging antitrust and consumer protection violations. Legislation restricting the use of such tools has been proposed or adopted in certain jurisdictions. We use pricing and leasing tools in our Residential Communities segment, and we could become subject to investigations, litigation or new restrictions related to their use. Litigation is inherently uncertain, could be costly and time-consuming, and could result in judgments, settlements or changes to our business practices that adversely affect our results of operations.

General Risks

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors. These include changes in our operating results, the results of our competitors, conditions in the real estate and credit markets, changes in interest rates, analyst recommendations, changes in market valuations of real estate companies, sales of significant blocks of our stock, and general economic and political conditions. Because we are taxed as a corporation and do not pay dividends, investors may value our common stock differently from the securities of real estate investment trusts, which could affect its trading price and liquidity.

Provisions of Delaware law and of our governing documents could delay or prevent a change in control.

Our certificate of incorporation and bylaws contain provisions that could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire control of the Company without the approval of our board of directors. These include the ability of our board to issue preferred stock without stockholder approval, advance notice requirements for stockholder proposals and director nominations, and limitations on the ability of stockholders to call special meetings or act by written consent. We are also subject to Section 203 of the Delaware General Corporation Law, which restricts certain business combinations with interested stockholders. These provisions could discourage transactions that some stockholders might consider favorable or that could result in a premium over the market price of our common stock.

If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, the accuracy and timeliness of our financial reporting could be adversely affected.

Our financial statements involve significant judgments and estimates, including those related to impairment of real estate and goodwill, revenue recognition for leases and services contracts, capitalization of development costs, and income taxes. If we identify material weaknesses in our internal control over financial reporting, or fail to maintain effective controls as our business changes, we could be unable to report our financial results accurately or on time. That failure could result in restatements, regulatory action and a loss of investor confidence.

Public health crises, geopolitical events and other external disruptions could adversely affect our business.

Pandemics, epidemics, acts of terrorism, civil unrest, armed conflicts, trade disputes and other events outside our control could reduce demand for our properties and services. They could also disrupt our tenants' businesses and impair their ability to pay rent, interrupt construction and supply chains, and cause volatility in the financial markets. Past public health emergencies accelerated changes in how tenants use office and retail space, and future events could have similar or more severe effects on our business, financial condition and results of operations.
