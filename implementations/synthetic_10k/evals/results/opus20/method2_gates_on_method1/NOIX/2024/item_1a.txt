Item 1A. Risk Factors

Investing in our securities involves risk. Before making an investment decision, you should consider the risks described below together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. That information includes Part II, Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," our consolidated financial statements and the related notes in Part II, Item 8, and the cautionary statements regarding forward-looking information in this report.

The risks below are those we currently believe to be material. Additional risks that we do not presently know of, or that we now consider immaterial, may also harm our business. Any of these risks could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows and the trading price of our common stock. Some risks bear most heavily on one of our three reportable segments, Commercial Systems, Defense and Space, and Aftermarket Services, while others affect the Company as a whole. Many of these risks interact with one another. The order in which they appear does not indicate their relative likelihood or potential severity.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Our results depend heavily on production rates in the commercial aircraft industry, which is cyclical and concentrated among a small number of airframe and engine manufacturers.

Revenue in our Commercial Systems segment is driven mainly by the rates at which our airframe and engine manufacturer customers build and deliver aircraft. Those rates depend on airline profitability, passenger and cargo traffic, fuel prices, the availability and cost of aircraft financing, fleet retirement decisions and general economic conditions. All of these factors are outside our control. Historically, the commercial aerospace industry has gone through pronounced cycles. Economic downturns, pandemics, terrorist events and regional conflicts have caused airlines to defer or cancel orders, and those deferrals and cancellations have led manufacturers to cut production. In recent years, airframe and engine manufacturers have also had difficulty meeting announced production rates because of their own quality, certification and supply chain problems. When a customer cuts or delays production, we generally have limited contractual protection. We may also be left holding inventory, tooling and capacity sized for higher volumes.

The commercial aircraft industry is highly concentrated. A small number of large manufacturers account for a substantial portion of our Commercial Systems original equipment sales. A production halt, quality-related stoppage, regulatory grounding, labor strike or financial distress at any one of these customers could quickly and significantly reduce our revenue and operating income. These customers also have considerable bargaining power. They regularly seek price reductions, longer payment terms, greater risk sharing and expanded warranty commitments, any of which could compress our margins.

Our aftermarket business is sensitive to air traffic, fleet utilization and the retirement of older aircraft.

Aftermarket Services sales generally carry higher margins than original equipment sales and contribute disproportionately to our profitability. Demand for spare parts, repairs and overhauls depends on how intensively commercial operators fly their fleets and on military readiness requirements. Reduced air travel or reduced flight hours would lower aftermarket demand, and so would airline consolidation, operators drawing down spare parts inventories, or accelerated retirement of aircraft that carry our content. Operators facing financial pressure may defer maintenance, use surplus or used serviceable material in place of new parts, or shift work to independent repair providers. In addition, regulators may approve alternative replacement parts manufactured by third parties, which could erode our share of aftermarket demand on platforms where we are the original equipment supplier. Because aftermarket earnings are a significant source of our operating margin and cash flow, even a moderate decline in this business could have a disproportionate effect on our consolidated results.

A substantial portion of our Defense and Space revenue depends on United States government spending, which is subject to budget, political and policy uncertainty.

The United States government buys from us both as a direct customer and as the ultimate customer under subcontracts with defense prime contractors, and in both capacities it represents a substantial portion of our Defense and Space revenue. Government spending priorities can shift with changes in administration, Congress, threat assessments and fiscal policy. Programs on which we participate could be reduced, delayed, restructured or cancelled. Funding for government contracts is generally appropriated annually, and Congress has frequently been unable to complete appropriations before the start of the government fiscal year. When that happens, continuing resolutions restrict new program starts and production increases, and they can delay contract awards and payments. Debt ceiling disputes, government shutdowns and sequestration-style spending caps could have similar or more severe effects.

We also sell to allied foreign governments, both directly and through the Foreign Military Sales program. These sales depend on foreign budgets, United States foreign policy, export approvals and diplomatic relationships, any of which may change with little notice.

We face intense competition, and our competitive position depends on winning positions on new platforms that may not enter production for many years.

We compete with large, diversified aerospace and defense suppliers, specialized component manufacturers and, increasingly, with in-house capabilities at our own customers. Some competitors have greater financial resources, broader product portfolios or lower cost structures than we do. In the aftermarket, we also compete with independent repair providers, parts manufacturers approved by regulators and airline-affiliated maintenance organizations.

Commercial and defense system selections are typically made years before first delivery, and an incumbent supplier generally holds its position for the production life of the platform. If we fail to win positions on new aircraft, engines, defense platforms or spacecraft, our revenue could decline over time as existing programs mature and go out of production, and the effect may not become apparent until many years after the competitive outcome is decided. To win new positions we must often commit to aggressive pricing, extensive non-recurring engineering investment and demanding performance specifications. These commitments may not produce the returns we expect if the platform is delayed, sells fewer units than forecast or is cancelled.

Our future success depends on our ability to develop and commercialize new technologies, including electrified, more-electric and secure connected systems.

Our strategy relies on technology leadership in areas such as electrified and more-electric aircraft architectures, advanced flight controls, thermal management, secure connected avionics and resilient space subsystems. Research and development represents a significant and recurring part of our cost structure, and we expect to continue investing substantially in these areas. Some of these technologies may not mature as expected, may cost more to develop than we anticipate, may not achieve certification on schedule, or may be overtaken by competing approaches. Customer adoption of new propulsion and power architectures, including hybrid-electric and alternative-fuel aircraft, is uncertain. If we invest in technologies that the market does not adopt, or fail to invest in technologies that it does adopt, our competitive position and results of operations could suffer.

Geopolitical conflict, trade restrictions and international operations expose us to risks beyond our control.

We operate manufacturing, engineering and repair facilities in the Americas, Europe, the Middle East and Asia-Pacific, and we sell to customers around the world. Our international activities are subject to political and economic instability, armed conflict, sanctions, changes in trade policy, currency fluctuations, restrictions on repatriating cash, differing labor and employment regimes, and the challenges of managing a geographically dispersed workforce.

Tariffs and retaliatory trade measures on aerospace products, raw materials or components could raise our costs and reduce the competitiveness of products we export. They could also disrupt the bilateral arrangements under which civil aircraft parts have historically moved between jurisdictions with reduced trade barriers. Escalating tensions between major economies could limit our access to certain markets, customers or suppliers. Ongoing regional conflicts have also contributed to shortages and price volatility in certain metals and energy, and to airspace closures that affect airline operations and aftermarket demand.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Fixed-price contracts expose us to the risk of cost overruns and losses.

A significant portion of our commercial supply agreements and government contracts are priced on a fixed or largely fixed basis, sometimes with escalation provisions that may not fully reflect our actual cost increases. Under these arrangements we bear the risk that our costs to design, certify and produce the product will exceed the contract price. Fixed-price development contracts carry particular risk, because they require us to estimate the cost of maturing technologies that have not yet been fully designed or tested.

Cost growth can arise from technical difficulties, design changes, labor inefficiencies, supplier failures, inflation in materials and wages, and schedule delays caused by customers or regulators. If our estimated costs at completion exceed the expected revenue on a contract, we must recognize a loss provision for the full amount of the expected loss in the period in which we determine that the loss is probable. Because cost of revenue represents a large majority of our revenue, relatively small percentage increases in our costs can have a meaningful effect on our gross and operating margins. Changes in contract estimates could cause our results to fluctuate significantly from period to period.

Our use of contract accounting requires significant estimates, and changes in those estimates could materially affect our reported results.

We recognize revenue on many long-term contracts over time based on the progress we have made toward completion. This approach requires us to estimate total contract revenue and total costs at completion, including the effects of variable consideration, award and incentive fees, learning curves, labor productivity and future material prices. These estimates involve substantial judgment and are revised as programs progress. Revisions may result in cumulative catch-up adjustments that increase or reduce operating income in the period recognized, and individual adjustments on large programs could be significant. Estimating these amounts is particularly difficult on programs that combine new technology, long durations and multiple contract phases.

Disruptions in our supply chain, including shortages of raw materials, castings, forgings and electronic components, could impair our ability to deliver products on time and at expected cost.

We depend on a broad base of suppliers for specialty metals such as titanium, nickel-based alloys and aluminum, as well as castings, forgings, precision-machined parts, semiconductors, printed circuit assemblies and other electronic components. Many of these materials and parts are available from only a limited number of qualified sources, and some come from a single source. Qualifying a new supplier can take months or years because of certification requirements. The aerospace supply chain has experienced persistent capacity constraints, labor shortages, quality escapes and financial distress among smaller sub-tier suppliers.

Supply disruptions have caused, and may continue to cause, late deliveries to our customers, contractual penalties, expediting costs and increased inventory levels as we buffer against shortages. Our inventories increased during fiscal 2024, reflecting in part these conditions as well as higher production requirements. A prolonged disruption, the failure of a key supplier, or sharp price increases that we cannot pass on to customers could materially and adversely affect our results of operations and cash flows. Some of our suppliers are also subject to export control, sanctions and domestic-sourcing restrictions, which can further limit our alternatives.

Product defects, quality failures or safety incidents involving aircraft or systems that contain our products could result in significant liability, costs and reputational harm.

Our products are critical to the safe operation of aircraft, spacecraft and defense systems. A defect in design, manufacturing or materials, or a lapse in our quality management or documentation practices, could result in product recalls, regulatory directives, retrofit campaigns, warranty claims, production stoppages and lost business. An accident involving an aircraft or system on which we supply content, even one not caused by our product, could lead to lawsuits, government investigations and damage to our reputation. Recent heightened regulatory scrutiny of quality systems throughout the aerospace supply chain has increased the likelihood and potential cost of audits, findings and corrective actions. Our insurance coverage may not be adequate to cover all claims, and insurance may not remain available on acceptable terms. Losses not covered by insurance could be substantial.

Our operations depend on attracting and retaining skilled engineers, technicians and production workers, and labor disruptions or shortages could affect our performance.

We employ about 25,300 people. Our business requires engineers, certified technicians, machinists, inspectors and program managers with specialized skills, and many positions in our Defense and Space segment require security clearances. Competition for this talent is intense, both within aerospace and from other technology-intensive industries. Rising wage rates and labor shortages have increased our costs and, at times, constrained our output. In addition, a portion of our workforce in the United States and abroad is represented by labor unions or works councils. Work stoppages at our facilities, or at the facilities of our customers or suppliers, could disrupt production and deliveries. The loss of key leaders or technical personnel, or our failure to transfer knowledge from experienced employees as they retire, could also affect our ability to execute our programs.

Cybersecurity incidents and failures of our information technology systems could disrupt our operations, compromise sensitive information and expose us to liability.

We rely on information technology systems to design, manufacture and support our products, to manage our supply chain and to process financial transactions. Some of our products, including connected avionics, data links and digital health monitoring tools, themselves process and transmit data. As a defense contractor and supplier of critical aerospace systems, we are a frequent target of sophisticated cyber attacks, including attacks by nation-state actors seeking access to controlled technical data and classified information. Threats include ransomware, phishing, insider threats and attacks introduced through suppliers and third-party software.

A successful attack could interrupt production, compromise intellectual property or export-controlled information, harm customer relationships and result in regulatory penalties, contract termination or suspension from government contracting. Compliance with evolving government cybersecurity requirements for defense contractors and their supply chains is increasingly demanding. Failure to achieve or maintain required certifications could make us ineligible for certain contracts. In addition, disclosure obligations relating to material cybersecurity incidents could increase the reputational and legal consequences of an incident.

We may not successfully complete, integrate or realize the anticipated benefits of acquisitions.

Selective acquisitions are part of our capital allocation strategy, and we have made significant acquisitions that broadened our product portfolio and platform positions, including acquisitions that contributed to growth in goodwill and other non-current assets during fiscal 2024. Acquisitions involve risks that include the following:

difficulty integrating operations, systems, quality programs and personnel; diversion of management attention; loss of key customers, suppliers or employees of the acquired business; assumption of unknown liabilities, including environmental, product and contract liabilities; and failure to achieve the revenue growth, cost savings or other benefits we expected.

Acquisitions of businesses holding government contracts or export-controlled technology may also require regulatory approvals or mitigation arrangements that delay closing or limit our ability to operate the business as planned. Antitrust authorities have heightened their scrutiny of transactions in the aerospace and defense industries, which could prevent or condition future acquisitions.

Natural disasters, climate-related events and other business interruptions could disrupt our facilities and those of our suppliers and customers.

Many of our products are manufactured at a single facility, and some of our processes require specialized equipment and certifications that cannot be readily transferred. Fires, floods, severe storms, earthquakes, extended power outages, pandemics or other disruptive events at our facilities or at those of key suppliers or customers could halt production for extended periods. The physical effects of climate change may increase the frequency and severity of some of these events. Our business continuity plans and insurance may not fully mitigate the resulting losses.

Risks Related to Financing and Capital Structure

Our indebtedness and related obligations could limit our financial flexibility, and rising interest rates have increased our borrowing costs.

We carry a significant amount of long-term debt, which increased during fiscal 2024. Our interest expense also increased during the year, reflecting higher borrowings and a higher interest rate environment. Our debt service obligations reduce the cash available for capital expenditures, research and development, acquisitions, dividends and share repurchases. Our indebtedness could also make us more vulnerable to downturns in our end markets, and it could limit our ability to obtain additional financing on favorable terms.

Our debt agreements contain covenants that restrict certain activities, and some require us to maintain specified financial ratios. A breach of these covenants could result in acceleration of amounts outstanding. A downgrade of our credit ratings could raise our borrowing costs, reduce our access to the commercial paper and capital markets, and lead customers or suppliers to seek more restrictive terms.

We maintain a relatively modest cash balance and depend on operating cash flow and access to credit markets to meet our obligations.

Our cash and cash equivalents are small relative to our current liabilities and annual operating expenditures. We rely on cash generated from operations, our revolving credit facility and access to the debt capital markets to fund working capital, capital expenditures, debt maturities, dividends and share repurchases. Our operating cash flow can vary significantly from period to period for several reasons:

changes in working capital, including inventory build-ups, the timing of customer advances and milestone payments, and collection of receivables; delays in government payments during budget impasses; and customer requests for extended payment terms.

During fiscal 2024, our investing activities, including acquisition-related outflows, and our returns of capital to stockholders together required us to increase our borrowings. If operating cash flow falls short of our expectations, or if credit markets become disrupted or more expensive, we may need to reduce investment, suspend or reduce share repurchases or dividends, or seek financing on less favorable terms.

Goodwill and acquired intangible assets represent a large portion of our total assets, and an impairment could materially reduce our reported earnings and equity.

As a result of past acquisitions, goodwill is the largest single asset on our consolidated balance sheet and exceeds our total stockholders' equity. We also carry significant acquired intangible assets, which give rise to substantial ongoing amortization expense. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually and more frequently if events indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. Indicators of impairment could include the following:

a sustained decline in commercial aircraft production or air traffic; cancellation of significant defense programs; loss of a major platform position; increases in interest rates and discount rates; and a sustained decline in our market capitalization.

An impairment charge would not directly affect our cash flows. It could, however, materially reduce our net income and stockholders' equity in the period recorded, and it could affect compliance with financial covenants.

Our substantial long-term liabilities depend on estimates and assumptions that may change.

Other non-current liabilities represent a large portion of our total liabilities and are substantial relative to our stockholders' equity. These obligations include long-term liabilities whose ultimate amounts depend on actuarial, legal, tax and contractual estimates. Examples include employee benefit obligations, long-term customer and contract-related liabilities, environmental remediation reserves, uncertain tax positions and deferred tax liabilities. Changes in discount rates, investment returns, mortality and health care cost assumptions, litigation outcomes, or the terms of customer arrangements could require us to increase these liabilities or make cash payments sooner or in greater amounts than anticipated.

We may not continue to pay dividends or repurchase shares at current levels.

We have returned capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases. Decisions to declare dividends and repurchase shares are made by our Board of Directors at its discretion. Those decisions depend on our earnings, cash flow, financial condition, debt obligations, acquisition opportunities and other factors. Any reduction in or suspension of dividends or repurchases could adversely affect the trading price of our common stock.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

As a government contractor, we are subject to extensive regulation, audits and investigations, and violations could result in penalties, suspension or debarment.

Our contracts with the United States government and with prime contractors under government programs are subject to procurement regulations and cost accounting standards. They are also subject to requirements concerning truthful cost or pricing data, domestic sourcing, counterfeit parts prevention, supply chain security and business ethics. Government agencies routinely audit our cost accounting practices, business systems and incurred costs. These audits could result in adjustments to contract costs and fees, withholding of payments, or determinations that our business systems are deficient.

If we are found to have violated applicable laws or regulations, we could face several consequences, including:

civil or criminal penalties and repayment of amounts previously received; termination of contracts; and suspension or debarment from future government contracting.

Any of these outcomes could have a material adverse effect on our Defense and Space segment and on our reputation with commercial customers. In addition, the government may terminate contracts for its convenience, in which case we would generally be unable to recover anticipated profit on unperformed work.

Our products and operations are subject to aviation safety regulation, and changes in certification requirements or regulatory actions could delay programs and increase costs.

Our commercial products must be designed, produced and maintained in accordance with the requirements of the Federal Aviation Administration and corresponding foreign aviation authorities. Obtaining and maintaining the necessary design approvals, production certificates and repair station certificates requires substantial resources. Regulators have increased their oversight of aircraft certification and manufacturing quality, which has lengthened certification timelines for new aircraft and derivatives and has in some cases constrained customer production rates. Airworthiness directives affecting our products could require costly inspections, modifications or replacements. Changes in certification standards, including standards relating to software, cybersecurity and new propulsion technologies, could increase our development costs and delay our programs.

We are subject to export control, sanctions, anti-corruption and other international trade laws, and violations could result in significant penalties and loss of business.

Many of our products, technical data and services are subject to United States export control regulations governing defense articles and dual-use items, as well as economic sanctions programs and the export control laws of other countries in which we operate. Obtaining export licenses can be time-consuming, and licenses may be denied, restricted or revoked based on foreign policy considerations. Our international business also requires compliance with anti-corruption laws, including in transactions involving government-owned airlines, foreign defense ministries and third-party sales representatives. Violations of these laws, including violations by our employees, agents, joint venture partners or acquired businesses, could result in substantial fines, denial of export privileges, debarment, criminal prosecution and reputational harm.

We are subject to environmental, health, safety and climate-related laws and regulations that could impose significant costs and liabilities.

Our operations involve the use, storage and disposal of hazardous materials, including chemicals used in surface finishing, plating, machining and testing. We are subject to laws governing air emissions, wastewater discharges, waste management and the remediation of contaminated sites. In some cases, these laws impose liability for remediation regardless of fault, including at sites we formerly owned or operated or to which we sent waste. Regulators continue to tighten restrictions on substances commonly used in aerospace manufacturing, including certain per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances and hexavalent chromium compounds. Qualifying substitute materials and processes can require lengthy recertification.

Emerging regulations and customer requirements related to greenhouse gas emissions and climate disclosure could increase our compliance costs and affect demand for aircraft and our products. Those requirements include aviation emissions standards, carbon pricing mechanisms and sustainable fuel mandates.

Changes in tax laws or adverse outcomes of tax examinations could increase our effective tax rate and reduce our cash flows.

We are subject to income and other taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate has been below the United States federal statutory rate, reflecting research and development tax credits, deductions available for foreign-derived income, the geographic mix of our earnings and other factors. Changes in United States or foreign tax laws could increase our effective tax rate and our cash tax payments. Such changes include the implementation of global minimum tax rules, changes in the treatment of research and experimental expenditures, and modifications to credits and incentives. Our tax positions are subject to examination by tax authorities, and the outcome of those examinations could differ from the amounts we have recorded.

We may be unable to adequately protect our intellectual property, and we may face claims that we infringe the rights of others.

Our competitive position depends in part on proprietary designs, processes, software and know-how. We rely on patents, trade secrets, copyrights and contractual protections, which may not prevent competitors from developing similar technologies or prevent misappropriation, particularly in jurisdictions with weaker enforcement. Under many government contracts, the government obtains rights to use technical data and software developed under the contract, which may limit our ability to protect that technology from competitors. We may also be subject to claims that our products infringe the intellectual property of others, which could be costly to defend and could require us to redesign products or pay royalties.

We are involved in litigation and other legal proceedings that could result in significant costs.

From time to time we are party to lawsuits, claims, investigations and other proceedings. These matters can involve product liability, contract disputes, employment matters, intellectual property, environmental liabilities and government contracting. The outcomes of these proceedings are inherently uncertain. An adverse result in one or more of them could require us to pay significant damages or fines or change our business practices.

General Risks

Inflation, interest rates and general economic conditions could adversely affect our costs, our customers and our results.

Inflation in wages, materials, energy and freight has increased our costs. Our ability to pass these increases through to customers is limited by fixed-price contracts, multi-year pricing agreements and escalation formulas that may lag or understate actual cost changes. Higher interest rates increase our own borrowing costs. They also raise the cost of aircraft financing for airlines and lessors, which could reduce demand for new aircraft. A recession or slowdown in major economies could reduce air travel, weaken airline finances and pressure government budgets.

Fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates could affect our results.

A portion of our revenue, costs and assets is denominated in currencies other than the United States dollar, while many commercial aerospace transactions are priced in dollars. Our results therefore depend in part on exchange rates. Changes in those rates can affect the competitiveness of our products, the reported value of our foreign operations and the margins on contracts in which revenue and costs are denominated in different currencies. Our hedging activities may not fully offset these effects.

Pandemics and other public health crises could significantly reduce demand and disrupt our operations.

A widespread outbreak of disease could sharply reduce air travel and aircraft utilization. It could also lead customers to cut production and defer deliveries, and it could disrupt our workforce and supply chain. Past public health crises have demonstrated how severely and quickly such events can affect commercial aerospace demand. A recurrence could materially and adversely affect our Commercial Systems and Aftermarket Services segments in particular.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly. It may respond to our operating results, changes in our guidance or analyst expectations, developments affecting our major customers or programs, government budget decisions, industry conditions and general market volatility. Some of these factors are unrelated to our own performance.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could delay or prevent a change in control.

We are a Delaware corporation. Certain provisions of Delaware law and of our certificate of incorporation and bylaws could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us without the approval of our Board of Directors, even if the acquisition would benefit our stockholders. In addition, ownership of businesses holding classified government contracts may be subject to foreign ownership, control or influence restrictions, which could limit the universe of potential acquirers. These provisions and restrictions could discourage takeover attempts and reduce the price that investors might be willing to pay for our common stock.
