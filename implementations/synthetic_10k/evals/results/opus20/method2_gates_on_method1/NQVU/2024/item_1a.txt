Item 1A. Risk Factors

Our business, operating results, financial condition and the market price of our common stock are subject to a range of risks and uncertainties. You should consider the risks described below together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8. Any of these risks, alone or in combination, could materially and adversely affect our business, results of operations, cash flows or financial condition. The risks below are not the only ones we face. Risks we do not currently know about, or that we currently consider immaterial, could also impair our business. Some of the statements in this section are forward-looking. See the cautionary statement regarding forward-looking information elsewhere in this report.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Our sales and profitability depend on consumer discretionary spending, which is sensitive to economic conditions outside our control.

Apparel, footwear, accessories, beauty and home merchandise are discretionary purchases. Customers tend to reduce or defer them when they feel less confident about their financial position. Many factors can reduce consumer spending on our categories. These include inflation in food, housing, energy and insurance costs, elevated interest rates, high levels of consumer debt, tighter consumer credit, slower wage growth, unemployment, declines in household wealth, changes in government benefit programs and the resumption or increase of required payments on consumer obligations. In fiscal 2024, our revenue and net income both declined compared with fiscal 2023, and our gross margin narrowed. If these pressures continue or worsen, customers may visit our stores and digital platforms less often, buy fewer items, trade down to lower-priced merchandise or move their spending to competitors they see as offering better value. Because many of our costs are fixed or semi-fixed in the short term, even a modest decline in sales can reduce our operating income and margins by a larger proportion.

We operate in a highly competitive retail environment, and our failure to compete effectively could reduce our sales, margins and market share.

We compete with other department stores, off-price and outlet retailers, mass merchants, specialty apparel and beauty retailers, warehouse clubs, digital-only retailers and marketplaces, social commerce platforms and resale sites. We also compete with the brands we carry when they sell directly to consumers through their own stores, websites and applications. Competitors compete on price, assortment, brand access, convenience, delivery speed, loyalty benefits, customer service and store experience. Some have greater financial, technological or marketing resources, lower cost structures, more efficient fulfillment networks or a greater willingness to accept low or negative margins to gain share. Competitors that rely heavily on low-cost overseas production and direct shipment to consumers have increased price pressure in several of our categories. To respond, we may need to raise promotional activity, reduce prices, increase marketing or accelerate capital and technology investments. Any of these actions could lower our gross margin and operating income. If we fail to respond effectively, we could lose customers and market share.

If we misjudge fashion trends or customer preferences, or fail to manage inventory effectively, we may incur higher markdowns, lower sales and reduced margins.

Our success depends on our ability to anticipate, identify and respond to changing fashion trends and customer demand, often many months before merchandise reaches our stores and digital platforms. Customer preferences in apparel, footwear, accessories and beauty can change quickly. Social media, influencers and digital marketplaces have made these shifts faster and harder to predict. If we buy too much of the wrong merchandise, we may need to take significant markdowns, move excess inventory through our Value Stores segment at lower margins, or liquidate it, any of which would reduce gross margin. If we buy too little of merchandise that customers want, we may lose sales and disappoint customers, and they may shop elsewhere. Our proprietary labels carry additional inventory risk because we make the design, sourcing and quantity decisions and generally cannot return unsold product to a vendor. Inventory planning errors, inaccurate allocation of product across stores and fulfillment centers, and disruptions in the timing of receipts could also adversely affect sales, margins and working capital.

Our recent declines in sales and operating income may continue, and our strategic initiatives may not produce the benefits we expect.

In fiscal 2024, our revenue, gross profit, operating income and net income each declined from fiscal 2023. Our strategy depends on initiatives to improve assortments, expand our proprietary labels, integrate our store and digital channels, grow our loyalty, retail media and marketplace businesses, modernize our supply chain and reduce operating costs. These initiatives require significant management attention and capital and operating investment. They may take longer than expected, cost more than planned or fail to deliver the anticipated improvements in sales, margins or productivity. Cost-reduction efforts could disrupt operations, harm employee morale or affect the customer experience. If we cannot stabilize and grow sales while maintaining cost discipline, our profitability and cash flows could continue to decline.

Many of our full-line stores depend on traffic at shopping malls and other retail centers, which may continue to decline.

A significant portion of our Full-Line Stores segment operates in regional shopping malls and lifestyle centers. Traffic at these properties depends on the overall appeal of the center, the performance and occupancy of other tenants, the financial health and investment decisions of landlords, local economic and demographic conditions, and perceptions of safety. Long-term shifts in consumer behavior toward digital shopping and off-mall formats have reduced traffic at many centers. The closure of anchor or other major tenants, landlord financial distress, or a center's decline in quality could reduce traffic to our stores. In some cases, these events could also impair the value of the related store assets or lead us to close stores before the end of their lease terms. Our Value Stores segment depends similarly on traffic at the strip, power and outlet centers where those stores are located.

We depend on key national brand vendors, and changes in our relationships with them could adversely affect our assortments, sales and margins.

National brands are an important part of our assortments and a significant driver of customer traffic. Our vendors are not generally bound by long-term agreements that obligate them to supply us. Many brands have expanded their own direct-to-consumer channels and reduced the number of wholesale accounts they serve. Some have restricted the distribution of certain products, or changed their pricing, allocation and promotional policies in ways that are less favorable to department store retailers. If key vendors limit or end their supply of merchandise to us, choose to sell certain products only through their own channels or other retailers, raise wholesale prices, reduce marketing support or markdown allowances, or experience financial or operational difficulties, our assortments could become less compelling. Our sales and margins could then decline.

Our business is seasonal, and weak performance during peak selling periods could disproportionately affect our annual results.

We generate a disproportionate share of our sales and operating income during the fourth fiscal quarter, which includes the holiday season. Back-to-school and other seasonal events also matter significantly to our results. We make inventory, staffing and marketing commitments well in advance of these periods. Unfavorable economic conditions, unseasonable weather, a shortened holiday calendar, aggressive competitor promotions, supply chain delays, systems outages or other disruptions during peak periods could cause sales to fall short of plan. Such shortfalls could leave us with excess inventory that we must clear at reduced margins, and could have a materially adverse effect on our annual results of operations.

Damage to our reputation or to the reputation of our banners and proprietary labels could reduce customer demand.

The Novqua, Novqua Outlet and proprietary label brands are important assets. Our reputation could be harmed by many events. These include product quality or safety problems, poor customer service, failures in our delivery or return processes, data security incidents, unfavorable publicity about our labor, sourcing or environmental practices or those of our vendors, positions we take or decline to take on public issues, or the conduct of our employees, brand partners or influencers associated with us. Because negative information can spread quickly through social media, reputational damage may occur before we can respond. A loss of customer trust could reduce traffic, sales and loyalty program engagement, and could harm our relationships with vendors and our credit card partner.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Disruptions in our global supply chain could delay merchandise deliveries and increase our costs.

We and our vendors source a substantial portion of merchandise from manufacturers outside the United States, primarily in Asia, with significant volumes also coming from other regions. Our supply chain is exposed to political instability, armed conflict, trade disputes, labor disputes and work stoppages at ports or among carriers, shipping lane disruptions, container and freight capacity constraints, factory closures, public health emergencies, natural disasters and currency fluctuations in sourcing countries. Disruptions could delay or prevent delivery of merchandise in time for key selling seasons, raise ocean, air and domestic freight costs, and require us to use more expensive expedited shipping. We also rely on a network of distribution and fulfillment centers. A significant disruption at any of these facilities, whether from fire, severe weather, equipment failure, systems issues or labor shortages, could impair our ability to replenish stores and fulfill digital orders.

A cybersecurity incident or other compromise of our information systems or of customer data could disrupt our operations, harm our reputation and expose us to significant liability.

We collect, process and store large volumes of personal information about customers, loyalty members, credit card program participants and employees. We also process large numbers of payment card and digital wallet transactions. Our operations depend on information systems operated by us and by third-party service providers, including cloud platforms, payment processors, marketplace sellers and logistics partners. Retailers are frequent targets of ransomware, credential theft, account takeover, payment card skimming, phishing and other attacks, and the techniques used continue to evolve and become more sophisticated. Despite our investment in security controls, monitoring and employee training, we cannot eliminate the risk of a successful attack, an error or misconduct by an employee or contractor, or a vulnerability at a vendor. A significant incident could interrupt store and digital sales, compromise sensitive data, trigger notification obligations, regulatory investigations, payment network fines and litigation, and damage customer trust. Our insurance may not cover all resulting costs.

We depend on information technology systems and on large-scale technology modernization projects, and failures or delays could disrupt our business.

We rely on complex information technology systems to manage merchandise planning and allocation, pricing, inventory, point-of-sale transactions, order management, fulfillment, loyalty, marketing, finance and human resources. We are in the process of upgrading or replacing several important systems and migrating workloads to third-party cloud platforms. Implementing new systems involves significant cost and complexity, and these projects may take longer or cost more than planned. Implementation problems, integration failures, data conversion errors, system outages or the failure of third-party providers to perform could disrupt store and digital operations. They could also impair our ability to forecast demand and manage inventory, delay financial reporting or weaken internal controls. We may also not achieve the anticipated benefits of new technologies, including artificial intelligence tools used in personalization, forecasting and customer service, and the use of these tools may create new operational, legal and reputational risks.

Growth in our Digital Commerce segment may pressure our margins, and we may not keep pace with customer expectations for digital shopping and fulfillment.

Digital orders generally carry higher fulfillment, shipping and return costs than store purchases, and customers increasingly expect free or low-cost shipping, fast delivery and convenient returns. As a greater share of our sales moves to digital channels, our gross margin and operating expenses may come under pressure unless we improve fulfillment productivity and manage return rates. Our digital channels compete with platforms that have significant scale advantages in logistics and technology. If our websites and applications are not reliable, fast and easy to use, or if we do not continue to improve search, personalization, payment options and delivery speed, customers may shop elsewhere. Our marketplace also depends on third-party sellers whose product quality, regulatory compliance and service levels we do not fully control, and problems with their performance could harm our reputation or expose us to liability.

Our results depend on attracting, training and retaining a large workforce, and increases in labor costs could reduce our profitability.

We employ about 57,400 people, many of whom work in stores, distribution and fulfillment centers and customer service operations in hourly positions. Labor is one of our largest operating costs. Competition for hourly workers remains intense, particularly during peak seasons. Increases in federal, state and local minimum wages, changes in overtime and scheduling regulations, higher health care and benefit costs, and market-driven wage increases could significantly raise our expenses. If we cannot attract and retain qualified associates, our customer service, in-stock levels and fulfillment speed could suffer. Union organizing activity in the retail and logistics sectors has increased, and unionization of portions of our workforce could increase costs, reduce operational flexibility or result in work stoppages.

We derive meaningful income from our credit card program, and changes in that program or in the regulation of consumer credit could reduce our earnings.

We receive payments from an unaffiliated financial institution under a long-term agreement for our co-branded and private label credit cards. These payments depend in part on the performance of the card portfolio, including credit sales volumes, interest and fee income, and credit losses. Although the financial institution bears the credit risk on cardholder accounts, rising delinquencies and charge-offs, slower card usage or lower balances would reduce the payments we receive. Regulatory actions limiting late fees, interest rates or other card charges could also reduce program profitability and our income. Changes in the financial institution's underwriting standards or financial condition, a dispute under the agreement, or the expiration or termination of the agreement on terms less favorable to us could reduce this income and weaken a key element of our loyalty strategy.

Inventory shrink, including organized retail theft and fraud, could adversely affect our margins and store operations.

We experience inventory losses from external theft, organized retail crime, employee theft, vendor fraud, administrative errors and damage. Return fraud, payment fraud and loyalty program abuse also cause losses in our store and digital channels. Shrink has been elevated across the retail industry in recent years. The measures we take to reduce it, including security personnel, technology, product protection and changes to store layouts and merchandise display, increase costs and may make shopping less convenient. Incidents in our stores may also affect customer and employee perceptions of safety. If shrink rises or our mitigation measures prove ineffective, our gross margin and operating results could be adversely affected.

Weather conditions, natural disasters and climate change could affect demand for our merchandise and disrupt our operations.

Our sales of seasonal apparel, outerwear, footwear and home merchandise are sensitive to weather. Unseasonably warm winters, cool springs or extended periods of extreme weather can reduce demand for seasonal goods and lead to higher markdowns. Hurricanes, floods, wildfires, winter storms and other severe events can force temporary store or facility closures, damage property and inventory, disrupt supply chains and reduce customer traffic in affected regions. Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of such events and may raise our costs for insurance, energy and compliance with emerging climate-related regulations.

Risks Related to Financing and Capital Structure

Our indebtedness could limit our flexibility and expose us to interest rate and refinancing risk.

We have a significant amount of long-term debt, which increased modestly during fiscal 2024. Our interest expense also increased compared with the prior year. We must use a portion of our operating cash flow to pay interest and principal, which reduces the funds available for capital investment, dividends, share repurchases and other purposes. Our ability to refinance maturing debt on acceptable terms depends on our operating performance, credit ratings and conditions in the credit markets, which may be volatile. Higher interest rates would increase the cost of refinancing and of any borrowings under variable-rate facilities. Our debt agreements contain covenants that restrict certain actions. A failure to comply with them could result in acceleration of our indebtedness. A significant increase in leverage, or a downgrade of our credit ratings, could raise our borrowing costs, limit our access to capital and lead vendors or other counterparties to seek less favorable terms.

Our long-term lease commitments and other fixed costs create significant operating leverage.

We lease a large majority of our stores, distribution and fulfillment centers and offices under long-term, non-cancelable leases. These leases give rise to substantial right-of-use assets and lease liabilities on our balance sheet, and they make up a large share of our non-current assets and non-current liabilities. Lease payments, together with many store payroll, depreciation and other occupancy costs, do not decline proportionately when sales fall. As a result, a decline in revenue can reduce operating income and cash flows significantly, as it did in fiscal 2024. If a store or facility becomes unprofitable, we generally remain obligated under its lease. Exiting it early may require costly lease termination payments, subleasing at lower rents or continued payments for an unused property.

We may be required to record impairment charges on store assets and other long-lived assets.

We evaluate our property and equipment, operating lease right-of-use assets, goodwill and other long-lived assets for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying value may not be recoverable. Declining sales or profitability at individual stores, decisions to close or relocate stores, deteriorating conditions at shopping centers, or changes in our strategic plans could require us to recognize impairment charges. Given the significant carrying value of our store-level and lease-related assets, impairment charges could be material to our reported results in the periods in which they are recognized, although they would not directly affect our cash flows.

We may not continue to pay dividends or repurchase shares at historical levels.

In fiscal 2024, we returned a substantial amount of capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases. Together, these represented a significant share of our cash flow from operations and contributed to declines in our cash balance and stockholders' equity during the year. Our Board of Directors determines future dividends and repurchases at its discretion based on our results of operations, cash flows, financial condition, capital requirements, debt obligations, credit rating considerations and other factors. If our operating cash flows continue to decline, or if we need to fund investments, debt repayments or unexpected obligations, we may reduce, suspend or eliminate dividends or share repurchases. Any such change could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

Changes in trade policy, including tariffs and import restrictions, could increase our costs and disrupt our sourcing.

Much of the merchandise we sell is manufactured outside the United States, and we are therefore exposed to changes in trade policy. The United States has imposed and may continue to impose tariffs, quotas, duties and other restrictions on imports from countries where our merchandise and our vendors' merchandise is produced. Further increases are possible, as is retaliatory action by other countries. Changes to duty exemptions for low-value shipments, rules of origin, forced labor import restrictions and related enforcement can delay or prevent the entry of goods and raise compliance costs. We may be unable to shift sourcing to other countries quickly or economically, and we may be unable to pass higher costs on to customers without reducing demand. Any of these developments could reduce our gross margin and adversely affect our results of operations.

We are subject to extensive and evolving privacy, data protection and consumer protection laws, and our failure to comply could result in significant liability.

Our loyalty program, personalized marketing, retail media business and digital platforms depend on the collection and use of customer data. A growing number of state, federal and international laws regulate the collection, use, sharing, retention and security of personal information. Many of them grant consumers rights to access, delete and opt out of certain uses of their data, and impose restrictions on targeted advertising, tracking technologies and automated decision-making. These laws vary across jurisdictions and continue to change, and regulators and private plaintiffs have become more active in enforcement. Compliance requires significant resources and may limit our ability to personalize marketing or monetize audience insights. Alleged violations of these laws, of telemarketing and text messaging rules, or of consumer protection, pricing, promotion and gift card laws could result in fines, litigation, including class actions, and reputational harm.

We are subject to employment, product safety and other laws that may increase our costs and expose us to claims.

We must comply with numerous federal, state and local laws governing wages and hours, meal and rest breaks, scheduling, leave, workplace safety, discrimination, accessibility and immigration. Retailers are frequently the subject of class and collective actions under these laws. We also sell products, including our proprietary labels, children's apparel, cosmetics and home goods, that are subject to product safety, labeling, chemical content and textile regulations. A product defect or regulatory violation could lead to recalls, fines, product liability claims and reputational damage. Our vendors' indemnification and insurance may be insufficient to cover these costs, and as the owner of our proprietary labels, we bear more direct exposure for those products. Changes in these laws, or in their interpretation, could increase our compliance costs and require changes to our operations.

Changes in tax laws or the outcome of tax examinations could adversely affect our results.

We are subject to income, sales, property, payroll and other taxes in the United States and in the international jurisdictions in which we operate. Our effective tax rate could be affected by changes in tax laws and regulations, changes in the interpretation of existing laws, changes in the mix of earnings among jurisdictions, and the outcome of examinations by tax authorities. Expanded obligations to collect sales and use taxes, changes to the treatment of marketplace sales and new taxes on digital services or advertising could increase our costs or administrative burden. Adverse resolution of tax audits or disputes could require us to pay additional taxes, interest and penalties.

We are involved in litigation and regulatory proceedings that could result in substantial costs.

In the ordinary course of business, we are party to legal proceedings involving customers, employees, vendors, landlords, intellectual property owners, competitors and government agencies. These include claims related to employment practices, consumer protection, accessibility, product liability, privacy and intellectual property, including claims that our proprietary label designs infringe third-party rights. Litigation is inherently unpredictable. Defending claims can be costly and can divert management attention regardless of their merit. An adverse outcome in one or more proceedings could result in significant damages, settlements, fines or injunctive relief that could affect our operations.

General Risks

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors. These include variations in our comparable sales, margins and operating results; changes in guidance or analyst expectations; announcements by us, our competitors or our vendors; changes in our dividend or share repurchase practices; developments affecting the retail sector or the broader economy; and general stock market conditions. Many of these factors are outside our control. Our quarterly results may also vary because of seasonality, the timing of promotional events and weather, and may not be indicative of future performance.

Geopolitical events, public health emergencies and other large-scale disruptions could materially affect our business.

Armed conflicts, terrorism, civil unrest, political instability, pandemics and other public health crises could reduce customer traffic, cause temporary store closures, disrupt supply chains and labor availability, and weaken consumer confidence. Past public health emergencies demonstrated that such events can sharply and abruptly reduce store sales, change customer behavior and require significant changes to our operations. The duration and severity of any future disruption, and its effect on our business, would be difficult to predict.

The loss of key executives or failure to attract and retain qualified talent could harm our business.

Our success depends on the leadership of our senior management team and on our ability to attract, develop and retain skilled merchants, planners, designers, technologists, data scientists and other specialized personnel. Competition for these individuals is intense. The unexpected loss of key executives, or ineffective succession planning, could disrupt the execution of our strategy. If we cannot recruit and retain talent with the skills our transformation initiatives require, our business could be adversely affected.

Increasing scrutiny of our environmental, social and governance practices could increase our costs and affect our reputation.

Customers, investors, employees, regulators and other stakeholders are paying increasing attention to corporate practices related to climate, sustainable sourcing, labor conditions in supply chains, product content, diversity and governance. Expectations in these areas differ, sometimes sharply, among stakeholder groups. New disclosure and due diligence requirements are being adopted in various jurisdictions. Meeting these expectations and requirements may increase our costs. Failure to achieve any goals we announce, inaccurate disclosures, or decisions that some stakeholders view unfavorably could harm our reputation, affect customer and employee relationships and expose us to litigation or regulatory action.

Changes in accounting standards, critical estimates or the effectiveness of our internal controls could adversely affect our reported results.

Our financial statements rely on significant estimates and judgments, including those related to inventory valuation and markdown reserves, shrink, vendor allowances, loyalty program obligations, gift card breakage, lease accounting, asset impairment, self-insurance reserves and income taxes. Actual results may differ from these estimates, and changes in estimates could materially affect our reported results. New accounting standards or changes in their interpretation could also change how we report our results. If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, including as we implement new systems, we may be unable to report our results accurately or on time. Such a failure could reduce investor confidence and the market price of our common stock.
