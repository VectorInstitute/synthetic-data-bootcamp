Item 1A. Risk Factors

Investing in our securities involves a high degree of risk. You should carefully consider the following risk factors, in addition to the other information contained in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including our consolidated financial statements and related notes, before making an investment decision. The risks and uncertainties described below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks and uncertainties not presently known to us or that we currently deem immaterial may also materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, and cash flows. If any of the following risks actually occur, our business, financial condition, and results of operations could suffer.

Risks Relating to Our Business and Industry

Our financial results are highly sensitive to global macroeconomic conditions and cyclical downturns in our end markets. Demand for our heavy earthmoving equipment, material handling systems, and industrial automation technologies is closely tied to the broader economic environment. Our products are significant capital investments for our customers. During periods of economic uncertainty, recession, or tightening credit conditions, our customers frequently delay, scale back, or cancel capital expenditure programs. This cyclicality is particularly pronounced in our Heavy Industrial Equipment segment, where fleet replacement cycles can be extended significantly if construction or mining activity slows. A prolonged economic downturn could lead to a substantial decrease in our original equipment sales, reduced manufacturing capacity utilization, and severe margin compression due to our high fixed manufacturing costs. While our Aftermarket Parts and Services segment provides some resilience, it cannot fully offset the financial impact of a major contraction in new equipment orders.

Fluctuations in global commodity prices can adversely affect demand from our mining and extraction customers. A significant portion of our revenue is derived from multinational mining corporations and regional quarry operators who utilize our specialized heavy machinery for the extraction of coal, copper, iron ore, aggregates, and precious metals. The profitability and capital expenditure budgets of these customers are highly dependent on global commodity prices, which are notoriously volatile and subject to global supply and demand dynamics, geopolitical tensions, and currency fluctuations. When commodity prices decline or remain depressed, mining operators typically reduce their capital spending, delay the opening of new mines, and defer the replacement of existing heavy machinery fleets. Such reductions in customer investment would directly reduce demand for our high-margin extraction equipment and could materially and adversely affect our results of operations.

Our business is significantly dependent on public and private infrastructure spending, which can be unpredictable. Sales of our heavy earthmoving and excavation equipment are heavily reliant on large-scale construction and infrastructure development projects. These projects are frequently funded by federal, state, and local governments, both domestically and internationally. Government infrastructure spending is subject to political processes, budgetary constraints, legislative approvals, and changing national priorities. Delays in the passage of highway or public works funding bills, reductions in infrastructure budgets, or shifts in government spending away from physical infrastructure could significantly reduce demand for our construction machinery. Furthermore, private commercial and residential real estate development, which also drives demand for our equipment, is highly sensitive to interest rates and regional economic health. A decrease in either public or private infrastructure investment could harm our financial performance.

We operate in highly competitive markets, and failure to compete effectively could reduce our market share and profitability. The global markets for industrial machinery, automation solutions, and critical infrastructure equipment are intensely competitive. We face competition from other large multinational corporations, as well as specialized regional manufacturers. Competition is based on a variety of factors, including product performance, durability, fuel efficiency, technological innovation, pricing, total cost of ownership, and the quality of aftermarket support. Some of our competitors may have greater financial resources, lower production costs, or more extensive dealer networks in certain emerging markets. If competitors introduce machinery with superior capabilities, more advanced automation software, or aggressive pricing strategies, we may be forced to lower our prices or increase our research and development expenditures to maintain market share. An inability to compete effectively would negatively impact our revenue and gross profit margins.

Changes in the equipment rental market or the purchasing patterns of our rental company customers could negatively impact our sales. A substantial portion of our heavy machinery and material handling equipment is sold to large, independent equipment rental companies. The equipment rental industry has experienced significant consolidation, resulting in fewer, larger rental fleet operators with immense purchasing power. These large rental customers often demand favorable pricing, extended warranty terms, and guaranteed buyback arrangements. If the rental market experiences a downturn, these companies may reduce their fleet sizes, delay the replacement of aging equipment, or flood the secondary market with used machinery. An oversupply of used equipment can depress the prices of new machinery and reduce our original equipment sales. Additionally, if we fail to maintain our premium brand positioning and strong residual values, rental companies may shift their purchases to our competitors.

The transition to alternative power solutions requires significant investment and carries technological and market risks. The industrial machinery sector is undergoing a structural shift toward reduced emissions and sustainable operations. We are actively investing in alternative power solutions, including battery-electric and hydrogen fuel cell technologies, particularly within our Heavy Industrial Equipment and Material Handling segments. The development of these technologies requires substantial research and development expenditures and complex engineering to ensure they meet the rigorous torque, durability, and duty-cycle demands of harsh industrial environments. There is no guarantee that our alternative power solutions will achieve commercial viability, meet customer expectations, or be adopted at the rate we anticipate. Furthermore, if our competitors bring more efficient or cost-effective zero-emission machinery to market before we do, our competitive position and future revenue growth could be materially compromised.

Risks Relating to Our Operations and Supply Chain

We rely on a complex global supply chain, and disruptions or shortages could impair our ability to manufacture and deliver products. Our manufacturing operations depend on the timely delivery of raw materials, fabricated components, hydraulic systems, and electronic parts from a vast network of global suppliers. Our Automation and Control Systems segment, in particular, relies heavily on advanced semiconductors and specialized electronic components. Supply chain disruptions can arise from numerous factors, including supplier financial distress, manufacturing delays, logistical bottlenecks, port congestion, natural disasters, or geopolitical conflicts. Any interruption in the supply of critical components can halt our lean assembly processes, delay product deliveries, and result in lost sales or contractual penalties. While we maintain a resilient supply chain strategy, prolonged shortages of essential parts could severely impact our production volumes and operational efficiency.

Increases in the cost of raw materials, energy, and freight could negatively impact our gross margins if we are unable to pass these costs to customers. Our business is highly material-intensive. We purchase significant quantities of steel, iron, rubber, plastics, and other commodities to manufacture our heavy earthmoving equipment and material handling systems. Our manufacturing processes also consume substantial amounts of electricity and natural gas. The prices of these raw materials and energy sources are subject to global market fluctuations and inflationary pressures. Additionally, the physical size and weight of our products result in substantial inbound and outbound freight costs. If the costs of raw materials, energy, or transportation increase significantly, our cost of revenue will rise. Due to competitive pressures and long-term customer contracts, we may not be able to implement price increases rapidly enough or to a sufficient extent to fully offset these higher costs, which would result in a contraction of our gross profit margins.

Our reliance on an independent dealer network means our sales and customer relationships are partially outside our direct control. We employ a multi-channel go-to-market strategy, with our primary distribution channel being a global network of independent, authorized dealers. These dealers are responsible for localized sales of new and used equipment, distribution of aftermarket parts, and the provision of warranty and repair services. Because these dealers are independent businesses, we cannot fully control their operations, inventory stocking decisions, marketing efforts, or customer service quality. The financial health of our dealers is critical to our success; if dealers experience liquidity issues or fail to secure adequate floorplan financing, they may reduce their purchases of our equipment and inventory. Furthermore, if a dealer fails to provide adequate aftermarket support, our brand reputation in that region could be severely damaged, leading to a loss of long-term customer loyalty.

Our manufacturing processes are highly capital-intensive and require significant continuous investment in property, plant, and equipment. We maintain substantial property, plant, and equipment on our consolidated balance sheet, reflecting the heavy industrial nature of our manufacturing footprint. Fabricating massive steel structures and assembling complex machinery requires specialized, large-scale production facilities, advanced robotics, and heavy-duty tooling. We must continually invest cash in capital expenditures to maintain, upgrade, and expand these facilities to support our growth and technological advancements. This high degree of capital intensity means we have significant fixed costs. If our production volumes decrease due to falling customer demand, we cannot easily scale down our manufacturing infrastructure. Consequently, lower capacity utilization would lead to an under-absorption of fixed overhead costs, significantly reducing our operating income and overall profitability.

Failure to successfully develop, integrate, and market our industrial automation and control technologies could impair our growth strategy. Our Automation and Control Systems segment is a critical driver of our future growth, focusing on robotics, automated guided vehicles, and sophisticated control software. The market for industrial automation is characterized by rapid technological change, evolving industry standards, and increasingly complex customer requirements. Developing these technologies requires significant investment in software engineering and systems integration. If we fail to anticipate technological trends, experience delays in software development, or produce systems that do not seamlessly integrate with our customers' existing manufacturing environments, our products may become obsolete. Additionally, successfully marketing these complex solutions requires highly specialized sales personnel. If we cannot effectively demonstrate the return on investment of our automation technologies, our revenue growth in this segment will suffer.

We maintain substantial inventories, which subjects us to the risk of inventory obsolescence and pricing markdowns. To support our global dealer network and ensure rapid fulfillment of replacement parts in our Aftermarket Parts and Services segment, we carry a significant balance of inventories. While maintaining extensive inventory is necessary to minimize equipment downtime for our customers, it exposes us to the risk of inventory obsolescence. Changes in customer preferences, rapid technological advancements, the introduction of new machine generations, or sudden economic downturns can reduce demand for specific product lines or legacy components. If we accumulate excess inventory that cannot be sold at expected prices, we may be forced to offer significant discounts or record write-downs to the net realizable value of our inventory, which would negatively impact our gross profit and net income.

Our connected machinery and telemetry services expose us and our customers to cybersecurity threats and data privacy risks. A core component of our strategy is the integration of telemetry and connected technologies into our machinery, enabling remote diagnostics, predictive maintenance, and real-time fleet management. This digital connectivity requires the continuous transmission, processing, and storage of substantial volumes of operational data. As our machinery becomes increasingly connected, it becomes a potential target for cyberattacks, hacking, malware, and ransomware. A successful cyberattack on our proprietary software platforms could disrupt our customers' operations, cause physical damage to machinery, or result in the unauthorized disclosure of sensitive industrial data. Such an event could lead to severe reputational damage, loss of customer trust, costly litigation, and significant regulatory fines, ultimately harming our business and financial condition.

A disruption at one of our primary manufacturing facilities could severely impact our production capacity. We operate specialized manufacturing facilities across the Americas, Europe, and the Asia-Pacific region. Many of our product lines are manufactured at a single facility or rely on specific plants for critical components, such as engines or hydraulic pumps. A major disruption at one of these key facilities due to equipment failure, labor strikes, power outages, fires, natural disasters, or regulatory interventions could halt production for an extended period. Because of the specialized nature of our tooling and assembly lines, it is difficult and time-consuming to shift production to alternative sites. Any prolonged interruption in manufacturing would prevent us from fulfilling customer orders on time, leading to delayed revenue recognition, contractual penalties, and potential loss of market share to competitors who can meet customer demand.

Risks Relating to International Operations

Our global operations expose us to geopolitical, economic, and regulatory risks in multiple jurisdictions. As a multinational enterprise, we generate a significant portion of our revenue from international markets and operate facilities across various continents. Our global footprint exposes us to a myriad of risks inherent in international business. These include political instability, civil unrest, expropriation of assets, and economic volatility in emerging markets. Additionally, we must navigate a complex web of diverse and sometimes conflicting local laws and regulations, including anti-corruption statutes, labor laws, and environmental standards. Compliance with these varied regulatory regimes requires significant administrative resources. A sudden change in the political or economic climate of a key international market could disrupt our local operations, reduce regional demand for our products, and adversely affect our consolidated financial results.

Fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates could adversely affect our reported financial results and international competitiveness. Because we conduct business globally, a substantial portion of our revenue and operating expenses is denominated in currencies other than the US dollar. We are subject to translational risk when the financial statements of our foreign subsidiaries are converted into US dollars for consolidated reporting. A strong US dollar relative to other currencies reduces the reported value of our international revenue and earnings. Furthermore, we face transactional risk when our manufacturing costs are incurred in one currency while the finished products are sold in another. If the US dollar strengthens significantly, our US-manufactured equipment becomes more expensive for foreign buyers, potentially reducing our international sales volumes and allowing foreign competitors with local cost bases to gain market share at our expense.

Changes in international trade policies, tariffs, and retaliatory actions could increase our costs and reduce our global sales. Our global supply chain and international distribution networks rely heavily on the free flow of goods across borders. Changes in international trade policies, the imposition of import duties, or the implementation of quotas can significantly disrupt our business model. Tariffs on imported raw materials, such as steel and aluminum, or on specialized electronic components, directly increase our manufacturing costs. If we are unable to pass these increased costs to our customers, our margins will be compressed. Additionally, if the United States or other jurisdictions where we operate engage in trade disputes, retaliatory tariffs may be levied against our finished machinery by foreign governments. Such retaliatory measures would make our products less competitive abroad, leading to a decrease in our international market share and revenue.

Risks Relating to Financing and Capital Structure

Our customer financing programs and accounts receivable expose us to credit risks and potential defaults. We carry a substantial balance of accounts receivable on our consolidated balance sheet, reflecting the credit terms we extend to our independent dealers and direct customers. In addition, we occasionally provide extended financing arrangements or lease options to facilitate the sale of our heavy equipment. These practices expose us to significant credit risk. If our customers or dealers experience financial difficulties, liquidity shortages, or bankruptcy, they may default on their payment obligations to us. During economic downturns, the rate of defaults and the aging of our receivables typically increase. While we maintain allowances for doubtful accounts based on historical experience and current economic assessments, a sharp increase in customer defaults could exceed our reserves, resulting in substantial bad debt expenses and reduced cash flow.

We require substantial capital to operate our business, and restricted access to credit markets could constrain our operations. Designing, manufacturing, and supporting advanced industrial machinery requires significant ongoing liquidity. While our operating activities generally provide positive cash flow, we also rely on access to debt and capital markets to fund large capital expenditures, strategic acquisitions, and working capital needs during periods of seasonal or cyclical inventory build-ups. We maintain long-term debt and other non-current liabilities as part of our capital structure. If global credit markets experience disruptions, or if our corporate credit rating is downgraded, our ability to secure new financing or refinance existing debt on favorable terms could be severely restricted. An inability to access adequate capital could force us to curtail our research and development programs, delay facility upgrades, or abandon strategic growth initiatives.

Increases in interest rates could negatively impact our borrowing costs and reduce the purchasing power of our customers. Our business is highly sensitive to the interest rate environment. For our company, higher interest rates directly increase the cost of servicing any variable-rate debt we hold and make future borrowing more expensive. More significantly, our customers rely heavily on external financing, leasing, and credit facilities to purchase our heavy earthmoving equipment and automation systems. When interest rates rise, the cost of capital for our customers increases, which can significantly reduce their return on investment for new equipment. Consequently, a high interest rate environment often leads customers to delay capital expenditures, cancel orders, or opt for short-term rentals rather than outright purchases. This reduction in customer purchasing power can lead to a material decrease in our sales volumes and operating income.

Our capital allocation strategy, including share repurchases and dividends, may not yield the intended long-term value for stockholders. We utilize our cash flow for various capital allocation priorities, including funding organic growth, pursuing strategic acquisitions, paying cash dividends, and executing share repurchases. Our decisions regarding capital allocation are based on management's assessment of market conditions and our financial performance. However, there is no guarantee that our share repurchase programs will enhance long-term stockholder value, especially if our stock price experiences subsequent volatility. Furthermore, the declaration and payment of future dividends are at the discretion of our Board of Directors and depend on our future earnings, capital requirements, and financial condition. If we are forced to reduce or suspend our dividend payments or halt share repurchases due to operational difficulties, our stock price could be negatively impacted.

Impairment of our goodwill or other long-lived assets could result in significant non-cash charges to our earnings. Through our history of strategic acquisitions, we have accumulated a substantial amount of goodwill and other intangible assets on our balance sheet, alongside our significant property, plant, and equipment. We are required to test goodwill for impairment at least annually, or more frequently if indicators of impairment exist. We also evaluate long-lived assets for impairment whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. Factors such as prolonged economic downturns, adverse changes in the business climate, loss of key customers, or failure to realize anticipated synergies from acquisitions could reduce the fair value of our reporting units. If we determine that these assets are impaired, we would be required to record a significant non-cash impairment charge, which would adversely affect our reported net income and total stockholders' equity.

Risks Relating to Legal, Regulatory, and Environmental Matters

We are subject to stringent environmental, health, and safety laws, and compliance costs or liabilities could be substantial. Our heavy manufacturing operations, remanufacturing centers, and the materials we use are subject to comprehensive global environmental, health, and safety regulations. These laws govern the discharge of pollutants into the air and water, the handling, storage, and disposal of hazardous substances, and the occupational safety of our workforce. Compliance with these regulations requires continuous monitoring, facility upgrades, and substantial administrative oversight. If we fail to comply with these laws, we could face significant fines, operational suspensions, or civil and criminal sanctions. Furthermore, we could be held strictly liable for the investigation and remediation of environmental contamination at our current or former facilities, or at third-party disposal sites, regardless of fault. The costs associated with such environmental liabilities could be material to our financial condition.

Evolving emissions regulations for heavy machinery require continuous product engineering and could restrict our sales in certain markets. The heavy earthmoving and material handling equipment we manufacture is subject to increasingly stringent engine emissions regulations worldwide, such as the Tier 4 standards in the United States and Stage V standards in Europe. Regulatory authorities continuously evaluate and tighten limits on particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, and greenhouse gas emissions. To ensure our products remain compliant, we must continuously invest a significant portion of our research and development budget into advanced powertrain engineering, exhaust aftertreatment systems, and engine calibrations. If we are unable to engineer compliant machinery within the regulatory deadlines, or if the cost of compliance makes our products prohibitively expensive for customers, we could be barred from selling our equipment in key geographic markets, resulting in a substantial loss of revenue.

We may be subject to product liability claims, warranty expenses, or product recalls that could harm our reputation and financial condition. Our machinery operates in harsh, demanding environments and is used to perform heavy-duty tasks where failure can result in severe property damage, personal injury, or loss of life. Despite our rigorous quality control and testing protocols, our products may contain design or manufacturing defects. If our equipment fails to perform as intended or causes injury, we could face substantial product liability lawsuits. Even if we successfully defend against such claims, the legal costs and negative publicity could damage our brand reputation. Additionally, we provide warranties on our equipment. If we experience higher-than-anticipated defect rates, our warranty expenses will increase. In severe cases, we may be required to initiate widespread product recalls, which involve significant logistical costs and can severely disrupt our Aftermarket Parts segment operations.

Failure to protect our intellectual property could diminish our competitive advantage in automation and heavy machinery design. Our success depends in part on our ability to protect our proprietary technologies, including our advanced machinery designs, control software for automation systems, and telemetry algorithms. We rely on a combination of patents, trademarks, copyrights, and trade secrets to safeguard our intellectual property. However, the intellectual property laws of some countries do not protect our rights to the same extent as the laws of the United States. Competitors may attempt to reverse-engineer our products, independently develop similar technologies, or infringe upon our patents. Enforcing our intellectual property rights through litigation is expensive, time-consuming, and uncertain in outcome. If we fail to adequately protect our intellectual property, our competitors could offer similar products, eroding our market share and pricing power.

We are subject to diverse and complex global tax laws, and changes in these laws or unfavorable audit outcomes could increase our tax expense. As a multinational corporation, we are subject to income taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate is influenced by the geographic mix of our earnings, changes in statutory tax rates, and the valuation of deferred tax assets. The international tax environment is highly complex and continuously evolving. Initiatives by organizations such as the OECD, as well as unilateral changes in tax laws by individual countries, could significantly alter the taxation of our cross-border operations. Furthermore, our tax returns are routinely subject to audit by domestic and foreign tax authorities. If these authorities successfully challenge our intercompany transfer pricing policies or other tax positions, we could be liable for additional taxes, interest, and penalties, which would decrease our net income and cash flows.

General Risk Factors

Our success depends on our ability to attract, retain, and develop a highly skilled global workforce. Our ability to innovate, manufacture complex machinery, and provide advanced digital services relies fundamentally on our human capital. We require highly educated software engineers, mechanical designers, data scientists, and experienced management personnel. Additionally, our manufacturing and remanufacturing facilities depend on a stable supply of skilled tradespeople, including specialized welders, machinists, and assembly technicians. The market for such talent is highly competitive. If we are unable to offer competitive compensation packages, maintain a positive corporate culture, or provide adequate career development opportunities, we may experience high turnover or fail to recruit the personnel necessary to execute our strategic objectives. A shortage of skilled labor could delay our research and development projects and reduce our manufacturing efficiency.

Natural disasters, pandemics, or other catastrophic events could disrupt our business and global supply chains. Our global operations, as well as those of our suppliers and dealers, are vulnerable to disruption from catastrophic events that are beyond our control. Earthquakes, hurricanes, floods, extreme weather events exacerbated by climate change, global pandemics, or acts of terrorism could cause severe physical damage to our manufacturing facilities, distribution hubs, or corporate headquarters. Such events could also paralyze regional transportation networks, preventing the delivery of raw materials to our plants or finished goods to our customers. The resulting operational downtime, loss of inventory, and recovery costs would negatively impact our financial performance. While we maintain business continuity plans and insurance coverage, they may not fully compensate us for the operational losses and lost revenue caused by a major catastrophe.

Our stock price may experience volatility due to factors internal and external to our operations. The market price of our common stock has been and may continue to be subject to significant volatility. Numerous factors can cause our stock price to fluctuate rapidly, including our ability to meet the earnings expectations of financial analysts, announcements of new technological advancements by us or our competitors, changes in macroeconomic indicators, or shifts in the broader stock market sentiment regarding the industrials sector. Additionally, variations in our quarterly financial results, large block trades of our stock, or changes in our credit ratings can trigger substantial price movements. This volatility can occur independently of our underlying operational performance and may result in a loss of investment value for our stockholders.
