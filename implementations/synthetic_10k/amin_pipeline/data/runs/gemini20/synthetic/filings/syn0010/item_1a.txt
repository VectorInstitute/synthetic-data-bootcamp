Item 1A. Risk Factors

RISK FACTORS.

Our business, financial condition, results of operations and cash flows are subject to various risks and uncertainties. The following risk factors should be read carefully in connection with evaluating our company and the forward-looking statements contained in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. Any of the following risks could materially adversely affect our business, operating results, financial condition and the actual outcome of matters as to which forward-looking statements are made.

Macroeconomic and Industry Risks

Volatility in global economic conditions and cyclicality in the industries we serve may adversely affect our sales and profitability.
Our financial performance is highly dependent on the level of activity in the construction, mining, energy, and manufacturing industries. These sectors are inherently cyclical and are influenced by factors beyond our control, including global GDP growth, interest rates, inflation, and availability of credit. During periods of economic downturn or uncertainty, our customers may delay or cancel large-scale capital investments, leading to reduced demand for our heavy machinery and power systems. For instance, in our Construction and Infrastructure segment, a slowdown in residential or commercial real estate development can lead to a significant decrease in the sale of backhoe loaders and excavators. Because a substantial portion of our cost structure is fixed, a decline in revenue can result in a disproportionate impact on our operating income, which was $11,422 million in fiscal 2024.

Fluctuations in commodity prices can significantly impact the demand for our products and services.
Many of our customers in the mining and energy sectors base their capital expenditure budgets on the prevailing prices of commodities such as oil, natural gas, copper, iron ore, and coal. When commodity prices are low or volatile, mining and energy companies often reduce production levels and defer equipment upgrades or replacements. This directly impacts our Industrial Power and Energy segment, which provides critical engines and turbines for these operations. Furthermore, sustained low commodity prices can lead to increased competition for a smaller pool of projects, putting downward pressure on our margins. If these industries experience prolonged periods of depressed pricing, our results of operations and cash flows could be materially and adversely affected.

Our business is subject to intense competition from both established global manufacturers and emerging regional competitors.
The industrial machinery and power systems markets are characterized by vigorous competition based on product performance, reliability, technological innovation, and price. We compete with several large, diversified multinational corporations that have significant financial resources and global reach. Additionally, we face increasing competition from manufacturers in emerging markets who may benefit from lower labor and production costs, allowing them to offer products at lower price points. If we are unable to maintain our technological lead or if competitors introduce products that offer better value or performance, we may lose market share. Our ability to compete also depends on the strength of our dealer network and our ability to provide superior after-sales support. Failure to compete effectively could lead to reduced revenue and a decline in our gross margin, which stood at 29.9% in fiscal 2024.

Changes in government spending and infrastructure policy can affect the demand for our products.
A significant portion of the demand for our construction equipment is driven by public sector investment in infrastructure, such as highways, bridges, and airports. Government spending is subject to political processes, budget constraints, and shifting policy priorities. Reductions in government funding or delays in the approval of infrastructure projects can lead to decreased demand for our machinery. Similarly, changes in tax incentives for industrial investment or environmental subsidies for power systems can influence customer purchasing decisions. As we serve customers globally, we are exposed to the fiscal health and policy directions of numerous national and local governments, any of which could change in a manner that negatively impacts our business.

Operational Risks

Disruptions in our global supply chain or increases in the cost of raw materials could adversely affect our manufacturing operations.
We rely on a complex global network of suppliers for raw materials, components, and sub-assemblies. We are particularly exposed to fluctuations in the prices of steel and aluminum, which are primary inputs for our machinery. While we attempt to manage these costs through long-term contracts and hedging strategies, we may not be able to fully offset price increases through productivity gains or by passing costs on to our customers. Furthermore, any disruption in our supply chain due to natural disasters, geopolitical instability, labor disputes, or transportation bottlenecks could delay our production schedules and increase our costs. If a key supplier fails to deliver critical components on time or meets our quality standards, it could result in production halts and damage our reputation with customers.

Our reliance on an independent dealer network poses risks to our distribution and service capabilities.
We distribute the majority of our products through a network of independent dealers. These dealers are responsible for the primary relationship with the end-user, including sales, maintenance, and repair services. The financial health and operational effectiveness of these dealers are critical to our success. If a significant number of our dealers experience financial difficulties or fail to invest in the necessary infrastructure and personnel to support our products, our market share and brand reputation could suffer. While we provide training and support to our dealers, we do not control their daily operations. Any failure by our dealers to provide high-quality service or to maintain adequate inventories of parts and equipment could lead to customer dissatisfaction and lost sales.

We face risks associated with our large and diverse global workforce.
With about 126,100 employees, we are subject to various labor-related risks, including the ability to attract and retain skilled personnel in a competitive labor market. Our manufacturing operations require specialized engineering and technical expertise. A shortage of qualified labor or significant increases in labor costs could impact our ability to meet production targets and maintain our margins. Additionally, a portion of our workforce is represented by unions or works councils. Any labor disputes, strikes, or work stoppages could disrupt our operations and result in significant costs. We also face the challenge of managing a large workforce across diverse legal and cultural environments, which increases the complexity of our human resources and compliance functions.

Manufacturing complexities and the management of production capacity could impact our financial results.
Operating a global network of manufacturing facilities involves significant fixed costs and complex logistical coordination. We must constantly balance our production capacity with fluctuating market demand. If we overestimate demand, we may face underutilization of our facilities, leading to higher per-unit costs and reduced profitability. Conversely, if we underestimate demand, we may be unable to fulfill customer orders in a timely manner, resulting in lost revenue and potential damage to customer relationships. The introduction of new products or manufacturing technologies also carries risks of production delays, quality issues, and higher-than-expected ramp-up costs.

Information technology security threats and service disruptions could harm our business.
We rely heavily on sophisticated information technology systems to manage our operations, including manufacturing, supply chain, sales, and financial reporting. We also offer digital solutions to our customers, such as telematics and fleet management software, which involve the collection and storage of data. Like other large global corporations, we are subject to constant threats from cyberattacks, including malware, ransomware, and phishing. A significant breach of our systems could result in the theft of intellectual property, the disruption of our business operations, or the unauthorized disclosure of confidential information. Such an event could lead to legal liability, regulatory fines, and significant reputational damage. Furthermore, any failure or interruption of our IT infrastructure could impede our ability to process orders and manage our global business effectively.

Strategic and Technology Risks

Our failure to innovate and develop new technologies could render our products obsolete.
The industrial machinery and power systems industries are undergoing rapid technological transformation. We must continuously invest in research and development to stay ahead of competitors and meet evolving customer needs. In fiscal 2024, our research and development expenses were $1,986 million. Our focus includes the development of autonomous machinery, electric and hybrid power systems, and advanced digital tools for the smart factory. If we fail to successfully develop and commercialize these technologies, or if we are slower to market than our competitors, our competitive position could be significantly weakened. There is also no guarantee that our investments in new technologies will result in commercially viable products or achieve the desired return on investment.

The transition to a low-carbon economy presents both challenges and risks to our traditional product lines.
There is increasing global pressure to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and transition away from fossil fuels. This trend affects many of the industries we serve, particularly energy and mining. While we are developing engines that run on alternative fuels and expanding our renewable energy solutions, a significant portion of our current revenue is derived from products that utilize internal combustion engines. If the transition to alternative energy sources occurs more rapidly than we anticipate, or if we are unable to develop competitive low-emission products, our traditional business segments could face declining demand. Furthermore, new regulations aimed at carbon reduction could increase our manufacturing costs and the cost of operating our equipment for our customers.

We may be unable to adequately protect our intellectual property or may be subject to infringement claims.
Our success depends in part on our ability to protect our proprietary technology and brand. We rely on a combination of patents, trademarks, and trade secrets, but these protections may not be sufficient in all jurisdictions. Competitors may independently develop similar technologies or find ways to circumvent our patents. In some markets, the legal systems may not provide the same level of intellectual property protection as the United States. Additionally, we may be subject to claims that our products infringe on the intellectual property rights of others. Such claims, even if without merit, can be expensive to defend and may result in significant legal costs, damage awards, or requirements to redesign our products or license technology from third parties.

Financial and Capital Risks

Our significant current liabilities and debt obligations could limit our financial flexibility.
As of December 31, 2024, our total current liabilities were $34,205 million, and we had long-term debt of $2,278 million. Our ability to meet our debt obligations and fund our operations depends on our ability to generate sufficient cash flow from operations, which was $13,653 million in fiscal 2024. If our cash flow declines due to an economic downturn or other factors, we may be forced to reduce capital expenditures, sell assets, or seek additional financing on less favorable terms. A high level of short-term liabilities requires careful liquidity management and constant access to credit markets. Any disruption in the global credit markets or a downgrade in our credit rating could increase our borrowing costs and restrict our ability to fund our business.

Our capital allocation strategy, including share repurchases and dividends, may not be optimal.
In fiscal 2024, we returned a significant amount of capital to stockholders, including $7,803 million in share repurchases and $567 million in dividends. While these actions are intended to enhance stockholder value, they reduce the amount of cash available for other purposes, such as research and development, acquisitions, or debt reduction. If our business environment worsens, our prior decisions to repurchase shares could be viewed as an inefficient use of capital. Furthermore, there is no guarantee that we will continue to repurchase shares or pay dividends at the same levels in the future, and any reduction in these programs could negatively impact our stock price.

We are exposed to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates.
As a global company with significant operations and sales outside the United States, our financial results are reported in U.S. dollars but are influenced by the strength of the dollar against other currencies. When the U.S. dollar strengthens, our international sales and earnings, when translated, may decrease. Conversely, a weak dollar can increase the cost of imported raw materials and components. While we use hedging instruments to manage some of this exposure, these measures may not be fully effective and can themselves result in losses. Persistent volatility in exchange rates can make it difficult to predict our financial performance and can adversely affect our competitive position in international markets.

Interest rate volatility could increase our financing costs and affect customer demand.
Changes in interest rates can impact our business in several ways. Higher interest rates increase the cost of borrowing for our company, which could impact our net income, which was $8,951 million in fiscal 2024. Perhaps more significantly, higher interest rates increase the cost of financing for our customers, many of whom rely on loans or leases to purchase our heavy machinery. An increase in the cost of capital for our customers can lead to deferred purchases and lower demand for our products. While we monitor interest rate trends, sudden or sustained increases in rates could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition and results of operations.

Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance Risks

Our operations are subject to extensive and increasingly stringent environmental regulations.
We are subject to a wide range of environmental laws regarding air emissions, wastewater discharge, and hazardous waste management. In particular, our engines must comply with strict emission standards set by agencies like the Environmental Protection Agency in the United States and similar bodies globally. Meeting these standards requires ongoing investment in technology and can increase our production costs. Failure to comply with these regulations could result in substantial fines, product recalls, or restrictions on our ability to sell certain products. Furthermore, we may be held liable for the costs of cleaning up contaminated sites, including sites we no longer own or operate. As environmental standards continue to evolve, the cost of compliance is likely to increase.

We face risks related to product liability and other litigation.
As a manufacturer of heavy machinery and high-pressure power systems, we are exposed to potential product liability claims if our products are alleged to have caused injury or property damage. These claims can be expensive to defend and can result in significant settlements or judgments. While we maintain insurance coverage, it may not be sufficient to cover all potential liabilities. In addition to product liability, we are involved in various other legal proceedings, including commercial disputes, employment litigation, and regulatory investigations. The outcome of litigation is inherently uncertain, and any unfavorable result could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition and reputation.

Changes in trade policy and the imposition of tariffs could harm our business.
We operate in a global trade environment that is subject to frequent changes in policy. The imposition of tariffs on raw materials like steel and aluminum, or on finished goods imported from certain countries, can significantly increase our costs and impact our pricing strategy. Trade disputes between major economies can also lead to retaliatory measures that restrict our access to key markets. Furthermore, we must comply with complex export control laws and economic sanctions. Any violation of these laws could lead to severe penalties and the loss of export privileges. Uncertainty regarding future trade agreements and international relations makes it difficult to plan our global manufacturing and distribution strategy.

Our effective tax rate may fluctuate, and we may be subject to additional tax liabilities.
We are subject to income taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate, which was 20.9% in fiscal 2024, is based on current tax laws and our interpretation of those laws. Changes in tax legislation, such as modifications to the corporate tax rate or the treatment of foreign earnings, could significantly impact our financial results. Additionally, our tax returns are subject to audit by tax authorities. If these authorities disagree with our tax positions, we could be required to pay additional taxes, interest, and penalties. The ongoing evolution of international tax standards, including efforts to implement a global minimum tax, adds further uncertainty to our future tax obligations.

General Risk Factors

The physical and transition risks of climate change could adversely affect our operations.
Climate change poses both physical risks to our facilities and transition risks to our business model. Extreme weather events, such as floods, hurricanes, or wildfires, could damage our manufacturing sites or disrupt our supply chain. In addition to physical damage, climate change may lead to long-term shifts in demand for our products as customers adapt to new environmental realities. For example, increased water scarcity could affect mining operations, while changes in agricultural patterns could impact construction demand in certain regions. Our efforts to mitigate these risks through sustainable manufacturing and the development of green technologies involve significant costs and may not be fully successful.

Natural disasters, pandemics, or other catastrophic events could disrupt our business.
Our global operations are susceptible to disruption from events beyond our control, including earthquakes, tsunamis, and public health crises. Such events can lead to the temporary closure of our facilities, disruptions in our supply chain, and a decrease in demand for our products. For instance, a pandemic can lead to labor shortages and restrictions on international travel and trade. While we have business continuity plans in place, a major catastrophic event could have a significant and prolonged impact on our ability to manufacture and distribute our products, potentially leading to a material decline in our financial performance.

Geopolitical instability and social unrest could impact our international operations.
We operate in many regions of the world that experience political volatility, social unrest, or armed conflict. Such conditions can pose risks to our employees and assets, disrupt our local operations, and make it difficult to conduct business. Geopolitical tensions can also lead to sudden changes in regulation, nationalization of assets, or the imposition of sanctions. Our ability to operate successfully in international markets depends on a stable political and legal environment, and any significant instability could adversely affect our results of operations and growth prospects.

The results of our operations could be affected by changes in accounting standards.
Our financial statements are prepared in accordance with U.S. generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP). These principles are subject to change by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). New accounting standards or changes in the interpretation of existing standards can significantly impact how we report our financial results, including our revenue recognition, lease accounting, and the valuation of assets and liabilities. Such changes can make it difficult for investors to compare our performance over time and could affect our compliance with financial covenants.

Any of the risks described above, as well as other risks and uncertainties not currently known to us or that we currently deem immaterial, could have a material adverse effect on our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows and the trading price of our common stock.
