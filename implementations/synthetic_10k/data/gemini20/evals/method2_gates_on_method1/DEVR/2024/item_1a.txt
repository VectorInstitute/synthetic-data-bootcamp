Item 1A. Risk Factors

Our business, financial condition, results of operations, and cash flows can be affected by a number of factors, many of which are beyond our control. Any of the following risks could have a material adverse effect on our business, reputation, results of operations, financial condition, and the price of our common stock.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

We face intense competition from a wide range of competitors and may not be able to maintain our market share or pricing.

The medical technology industry is highly competitive and characterized by rapid technological change and frequent product introductions. Our competitors include large, multi-national medical device companies with significant financial resources, as well as smaller, specialized firms and emerging startups that may develop disruptive technologies. In each of our portfolios, including Cardiovascular, Neuroscience, and Medical Surgical, we compete based on product performance, clinical outcomes, ease of use, and the quality of our technical support. If our competitors introduce products that are more effective, safer, or less expensive than ours, or if they are more successful in their marketing and sales efforts, our market share and revenue could decline. Furthermore, we face increasing pricing pressure from healthcare providers and government agencies who are consolidating their purchasing power and seeking lower costs. If we are unable to demonstrate the clinical and economic value of our products, we may be forced to reduce our prices, which would negatively impact our gross margins and profitability.

Our long-term success depends on our ability to innovate and develop new products, which involves significant research and development costs and inherent risks.

To remain competitive, we must continuously invest in research and development to create new therapies and improve our existing products. This process is expensive, time-consuming, and uncertain. We commit a substantial portion of our annual revenue to research and development activities, and there is no guarantee that these investments will result in commercially successful products. The development of medical devices involves complex engineering and clinical challenges. We may encounter delays in the design, testing, or manufacturing phases, or we may fail to meet the clinical endpoints required for regulatory approval. If we are unable to bring new products to market in a timely manner, or if our new products do not gain acceptance from the medical community, our growth prospects and results of operations will be adversely affected.

Changes in the reimbursement practices of third-party payers could limit the demand for our products or the prices we can charge.

The sales of our medical devices depend heavily on the availability of adequate reimbursement from third-party payers, including government healthcare programs such as Medicare and Medicaid, and private health insurance plans. In many markets, healthcare payers are implementing cost-containment measures that include stricter reimbursement criteria, lower payment rates, and a shift toward value-based care models. If these payers refuse to provide coverage for our products, or if they set reimbursement levels too low to allow for a reasonable profit, hospitals and physicians may be less likely to use our technologies. Additionally, the process of obtaining reimbursement for new and innovative therapies is often lengthy and requires extensive clinical data. Any delay or failure in obtaining favorable reimbursement status could significantly limit the commercial success of our products.

Consolidation in the healthcare industry could lead to increased purchasing power for our customers and downward pressure on our prices.

There is a continuing trend of consolidation among healthcare providers, including hospital systems and physician groups, particularly in the United States. As these organizations grow larger, they often centralize their purchasing functions and use their increased bargaining power to negotiate lower prices and more favorable terms with medical device suppliers. Many of these organizations also implement "single-source" or "dual-source" procurement strategies, which could exclude us from certain markets if we are not selected as a preferred vendor. This consolidation, along with the rise of group purchasing organizations, has intensified competition and put significant pressure on our margins. If we are unable to maintain our relationships with these large healthcare systems or if we are forced to accept lower prices to remain a supplier, our financial results could be materially harmed.

The long-term growth of our business is dependent on our ability to successfully expand into emerging markets.

We are increasingly focused on expanding our presence in emerging markets where healthcare spending is expected to grow. However, operating in these regions presents unique challenges, including political and economic instability, fluctuating exchange rates, and complex regulatory environments. We also face competition from local manufacturers who may have lower cost structures or benefit from government policies that favor domestic products. Furthermore, our ability to succeed in these markets depends on our capacity to adapt our products and marketing strategies to local clinical practices and economic conditions. If we are unable to effectively navigate these challenges, our international growth may be slower than expected, and our overall financial performance could suffer.

Risks Related to Our Global Operations

Our global operations expose us to risks associated with foreign currency exchange rate fluctuations.

As a company with significant international sales and operations, we are exposed to volatility in foreign currency exchange rates. While we report our financial results in U.S. dollars, a large portion of our revenue and expenses are denominated in other currencies, such as the Euro, the Japanese Yen, and the Chinese Renminbi. Strengthening of the U.S. dollar against these currencies can reduce the value of our international sales when translated into U.S. dollars and make our products more expensive for foreign customers. Although we engage in hedging activities to mitigate some of this risk, these strategies may not be fully effective and can themselves result in losses. Significant fluctuations in exchange rates could have a material impact on our reported revenue, net income, and cash flows.

Disruptions in our global supply chain or manufacturing operations could adversely affect our ability to meet customer demand.

We rely on a complex global network of suppliers and manufacturing facilities to produce our medical devices. Many of our products require specialized components, raw materials, and electronics that are sourced from a limited number of suppliers. Any disruption in our supply chain, whether due to natural disasters, geopolitical tensions, labor disputes, or the financial failure of a key supplier, could result in production delays and product shortages. Furthermore, our manufacturing processes are highly technical and must comply with strict quality standards and regulatory requirements. If we experience manufacturing defects, equipment failures, or regulatory interventions at our facilities, we may be unable to fulfill orders, which could damage our reputation and lead to lost sales.

The ongoing global geopolitical environment and trade tensions could impact our international operations and financial results.

Our business is subject to risks associated with changes in trade policies, tariffs, and international relations. Increased trade tensions between major economies, particularly the United States and China, have led to the imposition of tariffs on certain medical products and components. These tariffs can increase our cost of revenue and reduce our competitiveness in affected markets. Additionally, geopolitical instability in certain regions can disrupt our operations, limit our access to customers, and threaten the safety of our employees. Changes in government leadership or shifts in nationalistic policies could also lead to more restrictive regulations or preferential treatment for local competitors, further complicating our global business strategy.

We are subject to risks associated with our large and diverse global workforce.

With approximately 78,900 employees located around the world, we face various labor-related risks. These include the challenge of attracting and retaining highly skilled clinical, technical, and management personnel in a competitive labor market. Our ability to innovate and execute our strategy depends on the talent and dedication of our workforce. We also face risks related to labor disputes, unionization efforts, and compliance with diverse labor laws and regulations in multiple jurisdictions. Increases in wages, benefits, and payroll taxes can significantly impact our selling, general and administrative expenses. If we are unable to effectively manage our human capital or if we experience significant labor disruptions, our operations and financial performance could be negatively affected.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

Our products are subject to extensive and rigorous government regulation, and failure to obtain or maintain necessary approvals could harm our business.

The design, testing, manufacturing, labeling, and distribution of our medical devices are subject to oversight by the FDA and similar regulatory bodies in other countries. The process of obtaining regulatory clearance or approval, such as a 510(k) clearance or a Pre-Market Approval (PMA), can be lengthy, expensive, and uncertain. Regulatory agencies may require additional clinical trials or data, which can delay product launches and increase costs. Even after a product is on the market, we are subject to ongoing regulatory requirements, including post-market surveillance and reporting of adverse events. If we fail to comply with these regulations, we could face enforcement actions, including fines, injunctions, product seizures, or the withdrawal of marketing authorizations. Furthermore, changes in regulatory frameworks, such as the implementation of the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) in the European Union, can impose significant new compliance burdens and costs.

We are subject to significant product liability risks and may be involved in costly litigation.

As a manufacturer of medical devices, many of which are implanted in the human body or used in life-sustaining applications, we face an inherent risk of product liability claims. If our products are alleged to be defective or to have caused injury or death, we may be named as defendants in lawsuits, including class actions. Product liability litigation is often complex, time-consuming, and expensive to defend. Even if we prevail, these claims can damage our reputation and divert management's attention from the business. If we are found liable, we could be required to pay substantial damages, which may exceed our insurance coverage. A significant product liability judgment or a series of claims could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition and cash flows.

Our failure to protect our intellectual property rights or allegations that we infringe the intellectual property of others could harm our competitive position.

Intellectual property is a critical asset for our business, and we rely on a combination of patents, trademarks, and trade secrets to protect our innovations. However, our patents may be challenged, invalidated, or circumvented by competitors. The process of enforcing our intellectual property rights through litigation is costly and uncertain. Conversely, we operate in an industry where patent litigation is frequent, and we may be accused of infringing the intellectual property of others. If a court determines that our products infringe a third party's patents, we could be required to pay significant damages, stop selling the infringing products, or obtain a license at a high cost. Any of these outcomes could materially disrupt our business and reduce our profitability.

We are subject to various anti-corruption, fraud and abuse, and other healthcare laws and regulations.

Our interactions with healthcare professionals, hospitals, and government agencies are subject to strict laws, including the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA), the U.S. Anti-Kickback Statute, and the False Claims Act. These laws are designed to prevent bribery and ensure that medical decisions are based on the best interests of patients rather than improper financial incentives. Compliance with these laws is complex and requires robust internal controls and training programs. If we or our employees, distributors, or agents are found to have violated these laws, we could face significant criminal and civil penalties, exclusion from government healthcare programs, and damage to our reputation. The costs of investigating and defending against such allegations can also be substantial.

Changes in tax laws or challenges to our tax positions could increase our effective tax rate and impact our financial results.

As a global company, we are subject to income taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate is influenced by the mix of our earnings across different tax jurisdictions and our ability to utilize tax credits and incentives. Changes in tax laws, such as those resulting from the OECD's Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) project or shifts in U.S. federal tax policy, could significantly increase our tax liabilities. Additionally, our tax returns are subject to audit by tax authorities. If these authorities challenge our transfer pricing arrangements or other tax positions, we could be required to pay additional taxes, interest, and penalties. Any material increase in our tax burden would reduce our net income and cash flows.

Risks Related to Financing and Capital Structure

Our substantial indebtedness and interest expense could adversely affect our financial health and limit our operational flexibility.

We maintain a significant amount of debt, including both short-term and long-term obligations. Our ability to make payments on our debt depends on our ability to generate sufficient cash flow from operations. If our cash flow declines, we may need to refinance our debt, sell assets, or seek additional capital, which may not be available on favorable terms. Furthermore, a portion of our debt may be subject to variable interest rates, and any increase in market interest rates would increase our interest expense and reduce our profitability. Our debt levels could also limit our ability to pursue acquisitions, invest in research and development, or return capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases.

We have a significant amount of goodwill and other intangible assets on our balance sheet, which could become impaired.

A substantial portion of our total assets consists of goodwill and intangible assets resulting from past acquisitions. We are required to test goodwill for impairment at least annually, or more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value may not be recoverable. Factors that could lead to an impairment include a significant decline in our stock price, a decrease in our projected future cash flows, or a deterioration in the economic environment. If we determine that the fair value of a reporting unit is less than its carrying value, we would be required to record a non-cash impairment charge. Such a charge could be material and would reduce our reported stockholders' equity and net income.

Our ability to return capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases is subject to various factors and may be limited.

We have historically paid dividends and repurchased shares of our common stock. However, our ability to continue these activities depends on our financial performance, cash requirements, and legal and regulatory restrictions. Our board of directors has the discretion to change our dividend policy or suspend share repurchases at any time. If we are unable to generate sufficient cash flow or if we prioritize other uses of capital, such as debt repayment or acquisitions, we may reduce or eliminate our capital return programs. Any such change could negatively impact our stock price and the perception of our company among investors.

Volatility in the credit and capital markets could impact our ability to access funding.

We rely on access to the credit and capital markets to fund our operations, acquisitions, and other capital needs. If these markets experience significant volatility or disruption, our ability to issue new debt or equity securities could be impaired. A downgrade in our credit ratings could also increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to certain types of funding. If we are unable to obtain financing when needed or on acceptable terms, we may be forced to delay or scale back our investment plans, which could harm our long-term growth prospects.

General Risk Factors

Cybersecurity threats and failures in our information technology systems could disrupt our operations and compromise sensitive data.

We rely heavily on sophisticated information technology systems to manage our global operations, including our manufacturing, supply chain, and research and development activities. We also maintain digital health platforms that collect and store sensitive patient data. Like many large organizations, we are frequently targeted by cyberattacks, including malware, ransomware, and phishing attempts. While we have implemented extensive security measures, there is no guarantee that we can prevent all unauthorized access or system failures. A significant cybersecurity breach could lead to the theft of intellectual property, the disruption of our business, and the exposure of confidential information. This could result in legal liability, regulatory fines, and significant damage to our reputation.

Our business could be negatively impacted by public health emergencies or other catastrophic events.

The occurrence of a global pandemic, such as COVID-19, or other public health crises can significantly disrupt our business and the healthcare industry as a whole. Such events can lead to the deferral of elective medical procedures, which reduces demand for many of our products. They can also cause disruptions in our supply chain, manufacturing operations, and clinical trials. Furthermore, natural disasters, such as earthquakes, floods, or severe weather events, could damage our facilities or those of our suppliers and customers. The increasing frequency and intensity of extreme weather events due to climate change may exacerbate these risks. Any major catastrophic event could have a material adverse effect on our results of operations and financial condition.

We are subject to evolving environmental, social, and governance (ESG) expectations and regulations.

There is increasing focus from investors, regulators, and other stakeholders on the ESG practices of public companies. We are expected to demonstrate progress in areas such as climate change mitigation, diversity and inclusion, and ethical supply chain management. New regulations requiring more detailed ESG disclosures are being implemented in various jurisdictions. If we fail to meet these expectations or comply with new regulations, we could face reputational damage, increased compliance costs, and a loss of investor confidence. Additionally, our efforts to meet ESG goals may require significant capital expenditures and changes to our business practices, which could impact our short-term financial results.

Our results of operations may fluctuate significantly from quarter to quarter.

Our quarterly financial results are subject to various factors that can cause significant fluctuations. These include the timing of new product launches, the impact of seasonal trends in elective surgeries, and the timing of large orders from hospital systems or government agencies. Our revenue and profitability can also be affected by the timing of research and development expenses, the integration of acquisitions, and changes in foreign currency exchange rates. Because a significant portion of our costs, such as research and development and selling, general and administrative expenses, are relatively fixed in the short term, even small variations in revenue can have a disproportionate impact on our operating income and net income. These fluctuations may make it difficult for investors to predict our future performance and could lead to volatility in our stock price.

We may be unable to successfully integrate future acquisitions or realize the expected benefits from them.

As part of our growth strategy, we may acquire other companies or technologies that complement our existing business. However, acquisitions involve numerous risks, including the challenge of integrating different corporate cultures, information technology systems, and manufacturing processes. We may also face difficulties in retaining key employees and customers of the acquired businesses. If we pay too much for an acquisition or if the acquired business fails to perform as expected, we may not realize the anticipated synergies or financial returns. Furthermore, the process of integration can divert management's attention from our core operations. Failure to successfully manage our acquisition activities could harm our financial results and growth prospects.

Changes in healthcare policy and legislation could have a material impact on our business.

The healthcare industry is subject to constant legislative and regulatory changes. In the United States, ongoing debates over the future of the Affordable Care Act and potential changes to Medicare and Medicaid programs create uncertainty for medical device manufacturers. Legislative proposals aimed at reducing healthcare costs, such as those targeting drug and device pricing, could directly impact our revenue and margins. Outside the United States, many countries are also implementing healthcare reforms that could alter reimbursement patterns and market access for our products. We cannot predict the nature or timing of these changes, but any major shift in healthcare policy could significantly affect the demand for our products and our overall business environment.

Our reputation is a critical asset, and any damage to it could harm our business.

Our success depends on the trust and confidence of healthcare professionals, patients, regulators, and investors. Our reputation can be damaged by various factors, including product recalls, safety concerns, legal or regulatory non-compliance, and negative publicity related to our business practices. In the age of social media, information—whether accurate or not—can spread rapidly and have an immediate impact on our brand image. If our reputation is significantly tarnished, we may find it more difficult to sell our products, attract and retain talented employees, and maintain favorable relationships with our business partners. Any material damage to our reputation could have a long-term negative impact on our financial performance.

Significant changes in the global economic environment could reduce demand for our products.

Our business is influenced by general economic conditions, including inflation, interest rates, and consumer confidence. A significant economic downturn or recession could lead to reduced healthcare spending by governments and private individuals. In such an environment, hospitals may delay capital equipment purchases or implement stricter cost-containment measures. Patients may also defer elective procedures that require our medical devices if they face higher out-of-pocket costs or loss of insurance coverage. While many of our products treat chronic or life-threatening conditions, a prolonged period of economic instability could still lead to lower procedure volumes and negatively impact our revenue growth and profitability.

The large scale of our operations makes us susceptible to internal control failures.

Maintaining effective internal control over financial reporting is essential for providing reliable financial reports and preventing fraud. Given the size and complexity of our global operations, which include multiple segments and numerous legal entities, there is a risk that a material weakness in our internal controls could go undetected. Any failure to maintain effective internal controls could result in misstatements in our financial statements, lead to regulatory investigations, and diminish investor confidence. While we continuously monitor and test our internal control systems, no system can provide absolute assurance against all potential errors or irregularities.

Our use of hazardous materials and the environmental impact of our manufacturing processes could lead to liability.

Our manufacturing operations involve the use of various chemicals and materials that are subject to environmental, health, and safety regulations. We are responsible for the proper handling, storage, and disposal of these materials. If we fail to comply with environmental laws, we could face significant fines, clean-up costs, and legal liability. Additionally, new regulations aimed at reducing carbon emissions and waste could require us to make significant investments in our manufacturing facilities and supply chain. Any environmental incident or failure to adapt to new environmental standards could damage our reputation and result in substantial financial costs.

Failure to effectively manage our inventory levels could lead to write-downs and impact our cash flows.

We maintain a significant amount of inventory to ensure that we can meet customer demand and respond to surgical needs promptly. However, managing inventory in the medical device industry is challenging due to the high number of product variations and the risk of technological obsolescence. If we overproduce certain products or if demand shifts more rapidly than expected toward newer technologies, we may be forced to record write-downs for excess or obsolete inventory. Conversely, if we fail to maintain adequate inventory levels, we may experience product shortages and lose sales to competitors. Any significant imbalance in our inventory levels could negatively impact our gross margins and cash flows.

The loss of a major customer or distributor could significantly impact our revenue in certain regions.

In many of our markets, we rely on a limited number of large hospital systems or third-party distributors to sell our products. While no single customer accounts for a dominant portion of our total global revenue, the loss of a major contract or a significant distributor in a key geographic region could lead to a material decline in sales for that area. Distributors also play a vital role in navigating local regulatory and clinical requirements in many international markets. If a key distributor fails to perform or if our relationship with them terminates, we may face difficulties in maintaining our market presence and serving our patients in those regions.

We are subject to risks related to the use of robotic-assisted surgical systems and digital health platforms.

Our growth strategy includes increasing the adoption of robotic-assisted surgical platforms and digital health services. These technologies involve unique risks, including the potential for software bugs, hardware failures, and user errors that could lead to patient injury. The complexity of these systems also requires extensive training for surgeons and clinical staff. If our robotic systems are perceived as unreliable or if they do not deliver the expected clinical benefits, their adoption may be slower than anticipated. Furthermore, the integration of data from implanted devices into our digital platforms increases our exposure to data privacy and cybersecurity risks, as any breach could compromise sensitive clinical information.

Our success depends on our ability to navigate the complex and evolving global intellectual property landscape.

The medical technology field is characterized by a high volume of patent filings and complex licensing arrangements. In addition to our own research, we often license technology from academic institutions and other companies. These licenses may be subject to various conditions and performance requirements. If we fail to meet these requirements or if our licenses are terminated, we could lose the right to use critical technologies. Furthermore, as we expand into new therapeutic areas and geographic markets, we may encounter different intellectual property laws and enforcement standards, which could complicate our efforts to protect our innovations and avoid infringement claims.
