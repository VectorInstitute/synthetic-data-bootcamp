Item 1A. Risk Factors

RISK FACTORS.

The following risk factors could have a material adverse effect on our business, financial condition, results of operations, and cash flows. You should carefully consider these risks in conjunction with the other information contained in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including the Consolidated Financial Statements and related notes. The risks described below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks and uncertainties not currently known to us or that we currently deem immaterial may also impair our business operations. If any of the following risks actually occur, the trading price of our common stock could decline, and you could lose all or part of your investment.

STRATEGIC AND INDUSTRY RISKS

Our business is heavily dependent on the health of the commercial aerospace industry and global passenger traffic levels. The Commercial Aerospace Systems segment generates a significant portion of our total revenue. Demand for our commercial products and services is closely tied to the financial health of airline operators and the overall volume of global air travel. Historically, the commercial aviation industry has been cyclical and subject to significant volatility. Factors such as global economic downturns, regional instability, or public health crises can lead to a rapid decrease in passenger demand. When airlines face lower traffic levels or reduced profitability, they may defer or cancel orders for new aircraft and reduce spending on maintenance, repair, and overhaul services. Such actions directly impact our original equipment sales and our higher-margin aftermarket revenue. A sustained period of reduced commercial flight activity would adversely affect our ability to achieve our growth targets and maintain our current operating margins.

We rely on government spending and defense priorities which are subject to political and budgetary uncertainty. A substantial part of our revenue is derived from the Defense, Space and Security segment, where our primary customers are national governments and their agencies. Defense spending is determined by national security priorities, geopolitical conditions, and the availability of government funding. Changes in political leadership, shifts in foreign policy, or domestic fiscal constraints can lead to the cancellation, reduction, or delay of major defense programs. Because many of our defense contracts are long-term in nature, any mid-program adjustment to procurement volumes or funding levels can disrupt our production schedules and result in significant stranded costs. Furthermore, the competitive bidding process for government contracts is rigorous and often involves long lead times. Failure to secure follow-on contracts for major platforms could result in a material loss of future revenue and a decrease in our defense market share.

The aerospace and defense markets are intensely competitive and characterized by rapid technological change. We operate in a highly competitive environment where we face pressure from both large-scale diversified aerospace firms and smaller, specialized manufacturers. Our competitors may have greater financial resources or more extensive technical capabilities in specific niche areas. Competition is based on various factors, including technical performance, product reliability, safety records, and total cost of ownership. If we fail to innovate or if our competitors introduce superior technologies—such as more efficient propulsion systems or advanced autonomous capabilities—our products may become obsolete. The trend toward flight electrification and sustainable aviation requires significant and sustained investment. If our research and development efforts do not yield commercially viable solutions ahead of our competitors, our long-term competitive position and financial performance will be harmed.

Our success depends on our ability to win positions on new aircraft and defense platforms. The lifecycle of aerospace products is exceptionally long, often spanning several decades from initial design to the final retirement of the fleet. Securing a position as a supplier on a new aircraft platform is critical because it locks in a long-term stream of both original equipment and recurring aftermarket revenue. The selection process is highly competitive and requires significant upfront investment in engineering and prototyping. If we are not selected for major new platforms or if those platforms fail to achieve commercial success, we may not be able to recover our initial investments. Additionally, if an aircraft manufacturer chooses to vertically integrate and produce components that we currently supply, our addressable market would decrease significantly.

OPERATIONAL AND MANUFACTURING RISKS

Disruptions in our supply chain or increases in the cost of raw materials could adversely affect our production. We rely on a complex, global network of suppliers for raw materials, specialized components, and sub-assemblies. Some of these materials, such as aerospace-grade alloys, composites, and electronic components, are sourced from a limited number of suppliers or from regions subject to geopolitical instability. Any disruption in the supply chain due to labor disputes, natural disasters, financial distress of a key supplier, or trade barriers could delay our production and delivery schedules. Furthermore, the cost of raw materials and energy is subject to market volatility. While we attempt to mitigate these risks through long-term supply agreements and price escalation clauses, we may not be able to fully pass on increased costs to our customers, which would result in a decrease in our gross margins.

We face risks associated with the complexity of manufacturing and the introduction of new products. The manufacturing of aerospace and defense systems involves highly complex processes, sophisticated machinery, and stringent quality standards. Any defects or failures in our manufacturing processes could lead to product recalls, warranty claims, or significant reputational damage. When we introduce new products or ramp up production for a new program, we often face "learning curve" challenges that can lead to higher-than-anticipated costs and production delays. If we are unable to achieve the necessary yields or meet delivery deadlines, we may be subject to contractual penalties or the cancellation of orders. Our ability to manage these manufacturing risks is essential to maintaining our profitability and customer relationships.

Our business requires a highly skilled workforce and the loss of key personnel could hinder our operations. We employ approximately 25,300 people, many of whom possess specialized technical expertise and high-level security clearances required for defense work. The aerospace industry faces intense competition for talent in engineering, digital technology, and advanced manufacturing. If we are unable to attract, train, and retain qualified personnel, our ability to execute on our research and development programs and meet production targets will be compromised. An aging workforce and the potential loss of institutional knowledge through retirements also pose a risk. Furthermore, a significant portion of our workforce is represented by labor unions. Any work stoppages or inability to negotiate favorable collective bargaining agreements could disrupt our operations and increase our labor costs.

Failures in our research and development efforts could result in significant losses. Innovation is the primary driver of our competitive position, and we invest heavily in research and development. In fiscal year 2024, our R&D spending represented a significant portion of our revenue. These investments are often made years in advance of any commercial return. There is no guarantee that our R&D activities will result in the development of successful new products or that we will be able to obtain the necessary regulatory certifications for our innovations. If we fail to accurately predict market trends or if our technological bets—such as those in autonomous flight or hybrid-electric propulsion—do not gain market acceptance, our financial condition and future growth prospects will be materially and adversely affected.

FINANCIAL AND CAPITAL RISKS

Our substantial level of indebtedness could adversely affect our financial health and limit our flexibility. As of December 31, 2024, we had a significant amount of long-term debt. This level of indebtedness requires us to dedicate a portion of our cash flow from operations to interest and principal payments, thereby reducing the funds available for research and development, capital expenditures, and other corporate purposes. Our ability to make scheduled payments or to refinance our debt depends on our future financial performance and market conditions. If our cash flows decrease or if interest rates increase significantly, we may face challenges in meeting our debt obligations. Furthermore, our credit agreements may contain restrictive covenants that limit our ability to take certain actions, such as incurring additional debt, making acquisitions, or paying dividends, which could constrain our strategic flexibility.

The significant amount of goodwill on our balance sheet is subject to periodic impairment testing. Goodwill represents a substantial portion of our total assets, reflecting our history of strategic acquisitions. Under accounting principles generally accepted in the United States, we are required to test goodwill for impairment at least annually, or more frequently if indicators of impairment exist. Factors that could lead to an impairment include a significant decline in our stock price, a decrease in projected future cash flows, or an increase in the discount rates used in our valuation models. If we determine that the fair value of a reporting unit is less than its carrying value, we would be required to record a non-cash impairment charge. Such a charge could be substantial and would have a material adverse effect on our reported net income and stockholders' equity.

We are exposed to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates. While we are headquartered in the United States and report our financial results in U.S. dollars, we conduct a significant portion of our business in international markets. We are exposed to currency exchange rate risk when we enter into contracts denominated in foreign currencies or when we translate the financial statements of our foreign subsidiaries into U.S. dollars. Although we use hedging instruments to manage some of this exposure, these hedges may not be fully effective. A significant appreciation of the U.S. dollar against other major currencies could make our products more expensive for international customers and reduce the value of our overseas earnings, thereby impacting our overall revenue and profitability.

Our effective tax rate may fluctuate and we may face additional tax liabilities. We are subject to income taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate is influenced by the geographical mix of our earnings, changes in tax laws, and the outcome of tax audits. In fiscal year 2024, our effective tax rate was 17.7%. Any changes in corporate tax rates in the U.S. or international tax reforms could increase our tax burden and reduce our net income. Additionally, we are subject to regular examination by tax authorities. If these authorities disagree with our tax positions, we could be required to pay additional taxes, interest, and penalties, which could have a material adverse effect on our financial results.

LEGAL, REGULATORY, AND COMPLIANCE RISKS

We are subject to stringent government regulations and certification requirements. The aerospace industry is one of the most heavily regulated sectors in the world. Our products must meet rigorous safety and performance standards established by the Federal Aviation Administration in the United States, the European Union Aviation Safety Agency, and other global regulatory bodies. Obtaining these certifications is a time-consuming and costly process, and there is no guarantee that new products will be approved. If we fail to maintain our existing certifications or if new regulations are introduced that require expensive modifications to our products, our ability to sell our systems will be impaired. Furthermore, any safety incidents involving aircraft equipped with our components could lead to regulatory investigations, grounding of fleets, and significant legal liability.

Our government contracts are subject to audit and potential termination. As a government contractor, we are subject to specialized rules and regulations, including the Federal Acquisition Regulation and Cost Accounting Standards. Our government contracts are subject to audit by agencies such as the Defense Contract Audit Agency. If an audit reveals that we have mischarged costs or failed to comply with regulatory requirements, we could be subject to price adjustments, fines, or suspension from future government bidding. Additionally, most government contracts contain "termination for convenience" clauses that allow the customer to cancel the contract at any time without cause. The termination of a major defense program would result in a loss of anticipated revenue and could adversely affect our segment operating margins.

We face risks related to environmental laws and climate change regulations. Our manufacturing operations are subject to extensive environmental, health, and safety laws. These regulations govern the use and disposal of hazardous materials, air emissions, and water discharges. Compliance with these laws requires significant capital expenditure and ongoing operating costs. We may also be held liable for the remediation of contaminated sites, including those formerly owned or operated by us or our predecessors. Furthermore, increasing global focus on climate change has led to new regulations aimed at reducing carbon emissions from aviation. If we are unable to develop products that help our customers meet these evolving environmental standards, or if our own operations are subject to carbon taxes or stricter emission limits, our costs could increase and our competitive position could be weakened.

Failure to protect our intellectual property could harm our competitive advantage. Our success depends on our ability to protect our proprietary technology, including patents, trademarks, and trade secrets. We rely on a combination of legal protections and internal security measures to safeguard our intellectual property. However, these protections may not be sufficient to prevent unauthorized use or misappropriation by third parties. In some international jurisdictions, intellectual property laws may provide less protection than those in the United States. If our competitors gain access to our trade secrets or if our patents are challenged or invalidated, our ability to maintain a technological lead would be compromised. Additionally, we may face claims that our products infringe on the intellectual property rights of others, which could lead to expensive litigation and the need to pay royalties or redesign our products.

GENERAL RISKS

A cybersecurity breach or failure of our information technology systems could disrupt our business. We rely heavily on sophisticated information technology systems to manage our operations, protect our intellectual property, and communicate with our customers and suppliers. As a major defense contractor, we are a frequent target of cyberattacks, including state-sponsored espionage and ransomware. While we invest in robust cybersecurity defenses, there is no guarantee that we can prevent all breaches. A successful attack could result in the theft of sensitive defense data, the loss of proprietary technology, or the disruption of our manufacturing processes. Such an event could lead to significant financial losses, legal liability, and damage to our reputation, particularly with government customers who demand high levels of data security.

Geopolitical instability and international trade tensions could adversely affect our global operations. We operate in numerous countries and are subject to risks associated with international trade, including tariffs, export controls, and economic sanctions. Changes in trade policies or the imposition of retaliatory tariffs could increase the cost of our components and make our products less competitive in foreign markets. Geopolitical conflicts, such as those in Europe or Asia, can disrupt global supply chains and lead to economic sanctions that restrict our ability to do business in certain regions. Furthermore, our international operations are subject to the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and similar anti-bribery laws. Any violation of these laws by our employees or agents could result in significant fines and the loss of government contracts.

The occurrence of natural disasters or other catastrophic events could disrupt our operations. Our headquarters in Princeton, New Jersey, and our various manufacturing facilities around the world are subject to risks from natural disasters, such as hurricanes, floods, or earthquakes. A catastrophic event at one of our primary production sites or at a key supplier's facility could cause significant property damage and long-term disruptions to our production schedules. While we maintain business interruption insurance, it may not be sufficient to cover all our losses. Additionally, such events could impact the global economy and reduce the demand for air travel, further affecting our financial performance.

Unfavorable macroeconomic conditions could lead to decreased demand and financial instability. Our financial results are influenced by general economic conditions, including inflation, interest rates, and corporate earnings levels. In an environment characterized by high inflation, our operating expenses, including labor and material costs, may increase faster than we can raise our prices. High interest rates can increase our borrowing costs and reduce the ability of our airline customers to finance new aircraft purchases. A global recession would likely lead to a decrease in both commercial passenger traffic and government tax revenues, potentially resulting in lower demand across both of our operating segments. Because these macroeconomic factors are outside of our control, we cannot accurately predict their timing or the severity of their impact on our business.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile. The trading price of our common stock is subject to significant fluctuations in response to various factors, including our quarterly operating results, changes in earnings estimates by securities analysts, and general market conditions. Announcements of major contract wins or losses, regulatory developments, or safety incidents in the aerospace industry can also lead to stock price volatility. Furthermore, our capital allocation decisions, such as share repurchases and dividend payments, may impact investor perception and the market price of our shares. In fiscal year 2024, we engaged in significant share repurchases, but there is no guarantee that such activities will enhance long-term shareholder value or stabilize our stock price.

These forward-looking statements are based on our current assumptions and estimates, which are inherently uncertain. Our actual results may differ materially from these projections due to the risks and uncertainties described in this Item 1A. If our financial performance falls below the expectations of investors or analysts, the price of our common stock could decrease significantly. We undertake no obligation to update our forward-looking statements, except as required by law.
