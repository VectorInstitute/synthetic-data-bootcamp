Item 1A. Risk Factors

The following risks could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, or cash flows. These risk factors should be considered in conjunction with the other information included in this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

The aerospace and defense industry is highly cyclical and sensitive to global economic conditions.

Our business is heavily dependent on the health of the commercial aviation industry, which has historically been cyclical and subject to significant fluctuations based on global economic growth, fuel prices, and geopolitical stability. A downturn in the global economy typically leads to reduced passenger traffic and lower profitability for airlines. When airlines experience financial distress, they often defer or cancel orders for new aircraft and reduce discretionary spending on maintenance and upgrades. Because a significant portion of our revenue is derived from the sale of original equipment and aftermarket services for commercial platforms, a prolonged decline in air travel demand could result in reduced orders for our avionics, thermal management systems, and structural components. Such a reduction in demand would adversely impact our production schedules and overall financial performance.

Our results are subject to fluctuations in government defense spending and changes in military priorities.

A significant portion of our revenue is derived from contracts with the United States government and its allied nations, either directly or as a sub-tier supplier. Defense budgets are subject to the political process, which can be unpredictable and influenced by shifting national security priorities, fiscal constraints, and changes in administration. Reductions in overall defense spending, the cancellation of specific aircraft or space programs, or delays in the appropriation of funds could materially reduce our backlog and future revenue. Furthermore, our defense contracts are often subject to audits and investigations; any failure to comply with complex government contracting regulations could lead to fines, penalties, or suspension from future bidding opportunities.

We operate in a highly competitive environment with rapid technological advancements.

The markets for aerospace systems and defense solutions are characterized by intense competition and constant innovation. We compete against several large, well-capitalized global companies, some of which may have greater financial resources or broader product portfolios than we do. Our ability to maintain our market position depends on our success in developing and commercializing new technologies that meet the evolving needs of our customers, such as more fuel-efficient propulsion components and advanced autonomous flight systems. If our competitors develop superior technologies or offer similar products at lower price points, our margins and market share could be adversely affected. During the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, we invested Research and development expense of $409 million to maintain our competitive edge, but there is no guarantee that these investments will result in successful or profitable products.

We depend on a limited number of large customers for a significant portion of our sales.

Our customer base is concentrated among a few major commercial airframe manufacturers and engine OEMs, as well as government defense agencies. The loss of any of these key customers, or a significant reduction in their purchase volumes, would have a material adverse effect on our business. Because our products are often designed into specific aircraft platforms, our success is inextricably linked to the success of those platforms in the marketplace. If a major aircraft program for which we provide critical systems experiences technical delays, safety issues, or poor sales performance, our own production volumes and revenue will suffer accordingly. Furthermore, the high degree of customer concentration gives our largest customers significant leverage to negotiate lower prices or more demanding contract terms.

Technological shifts toward sustainable aviation may require significant capital investment and render existing products obsolete.

The aerospace industry is facing increasing pressure to reduce its carbon footprint, leading to the development of electric, hybrid-electric, and hydrogen-powered aircraft. While we are investing in technologies to support these trends, the transition away from traditional combustion engines and hydraulic systems poses a risk to our legacy product lines. If we fail to adapt our portfolio to meet new environmental standards or if the industry adopts technologies where we do not have a strong intellectual property position, our long-term growth prospects could be diminished. The pace of this transition is uncertain and depends on advancements in battery density, hydrogen storage, and regulatory frameworks, all of which are outside of our direct control.

Operational Risks

Disruptions in our supply chain or increases in the cost of raw materials could adversely affect our margins.

We rely on a complex, global network of suppliers for raw materials, such as specialized alloys and carbon fiber composites, as well as electronic components and sub-assemblies. Any disruption in this supply chain due to geopolitical tensions, trade disputes, natural disasters, or the financial instability of a key supplier could lead to production delays. Additionally, we are exposed to inflationary pressures on the cost of materials and energy. While we attempt to mitigate these risks through long-term supply agreements and hedging strategies, we may not be able to pass all cost increases on to our customers, particularly under fixed-price contracts. A significant increase in input costs without a corresponding increase in our selling prices would contract our gross margin, which was 27.3% in fiscal 2024.

Our use of fixed-price contracts exposes us to the risk of cost overruns.

A substantial portion of our revenue, particularly in the Defense and Space Solutions segment, is derived from fixed-price contracts. Under these arrangements, we agree to deliver products or services at a predetermined price, regardless of the actual costs we incur. If we underestimate the technical challenges, labor requirements, or material costs associated with a project, we must absorb the resulting losses. Cost overruns can occur due to design changes, manufacturing inefficiencies, or unforeseen supply chain issues. Given the long-term nature of many aerospace programs, even small inaccuracies in our initial cost estimates can accumulate into significant financial losses over the life of a contract.

Failure to successfully develop and integrate new technologies could impact our future growth.

The development of advanced aerospace systems is technically demanding and involves significant risk. Our research and development projects may experience delays, fail to meet performance specifications, or exceed their original budgets. In 2024, our Research and development expense of $409 million represented 3.7% of our total revenue. If we are unable to successfully transition our developmental programs into high-volume production, we may not realize a return on these investments. Furthermore, as aircraft become increasingly digital and autonomous, any software defects or system integration failures could lead to grounded fleets, reputational damage, and significant liability claims.

We face risks related to our manufacturing operations and quality control.

The manufacturing of aerospace components requires extreme precision and adherence to rigorous quality standards. Any failure in our manufacturing processes or a lapse in quality control could result in defective products being delivered to customers. Such failures can lead to expensive product recalls, warranty claims, and the loss of regulatory certifications. Because our components are critical to the safety of flight, any perceived or actual quality issue could result in significant legal liability and damage our reputation with OEMs and airlines. Our global manufacturing footprint also exposes us to risks related to equipment failure, labor disputes, and disruptions at our facilities.

We depend on the availability and performance of our 27,000 employees.

Our success is highly dependent on our ability to attract and retain skilled engineers, technicians, and management personnel. The aerospace industry faces intense competition for talent, particularly in specialized fields such as avionics software and advanced materials science. If we are unable to maintain a sufficiently skilled workforce, our ability to execute on complex programs and meet production schedules would be impaired. Furthermore, about 27,000 employees are vital to our operations, and any widespread labor unrest or significant increases in labor costs could adversely affect our financial results. Changes in labor laws or the cost of healthcare and other benefits could also increase our operating expenses.

Financial and Capital Risks

Our level of indebtedness could adversely affect our financial condition and limit our operational flexibility.

As of December 31, 2024, we had Long-term debt of $1,888 million. This level of debt requires us to dedicate a portion of our cash flow from operations to interest and principal payments, reducing the funds available for working capital, capital expenditures, and R&D. Our debt agreements contain various covenants that may restrict our ability to incur additional debt, make certain investments, or engage in mergers and acquisitions. If we fail to comply with these covenants, our lenders could declare a default, making all outstanding debt immediately due and payable. A high level of leverage also makes us more vulnerable to economic downturns and limits our ability to respond to competitive pressures.

We are exposed to fluctuations in interest rates.

Changes in interest rates can impact our financial results by increasing the cost of servicing our variable-rate debt or by affecting the terms of future financings. Although we may use derivative instruments to manage our interest rate exposure, these strategies may not be fully effective. Higher interest rates could also reduce the demand for new aircraft by increasing the financing costs for our airline customers, which would indirectly affect our revenue. During fiscal 2024, our interest expense was $85 million, and any significant increase in market rates could lead to higher borrowing costs in the future.

Our capital allocation strategy, including dividends and share repurchases, may not be sustainable.

We have historically returned significant capital to stockholders. In fiscal 2024, we paid dividends of $314 million and conducted Share repurchases of $961 million. While these actions are intended to enhance stockholder value, they reduce the amount of cash available for internal investment or debt reduction. If our operating cash flow declines or if we face unexpected capital requirements, we may be forced to reduce or eliminate our dividend and share repurchase programs. Such a move could lead to a decline in our stock price and negatively impact investor confidence. Our ability to continue these capital returns depends on our future financial performance and the maintenance of adequate liquidity.

Disruptions in the financial markets could limit our access to capital.

We rely on access to the capital markets to fund our operations, manage our debt obligations, and support our growth strategy. Volatility in the global credit markets or a downgrade in our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs or restrict our ability to obtain new financing on favorable terms. If we are unable to access sufficient capital when needed, we may be forced to delay important R&D projects, scale back our manufacturing expansion, or forego strategic acquisitions. The availability of credit is also important for our customers and suppliers; if they face liquidity constraints, it could lead to payment delays or disruptions in our supply chain.

Legal, Regulatory, and Geopolitical Risks

We are subject to stringent and evolving aviation regulations.

Our products and manufacturing processes are subject to extensive regulation by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) in the United States and similar regulatory authorities worldwide. Obtaining and maintaining certifications for new aerospace components is a time-consuming and expensive process. Changes in safety standards or the introduction of new regulatory requirements could necessitate costly modifications to our products or delay their entry into the market. If any of our products are found to be non-compliant with these regulations, we could face significant fines, the grounding of aircraft using our parts, and the suspension of our production approvals.

International operations expose us to geopolitical, economic, and local regulatory risks.

With a global footprint and customers in numerous countries, we are subject to the risks of doing business internationally. These include changes in trade policies, tariffs, and export controls, as well as fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates. Geopolitical instability, such as armed conflict or diplomatic tensions, can disrupt our operations in certain regions and complicate our relationships with international customers. We must also comply with various local laws, including the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) and other anti-bribery regulations. Any violation of these laws could result in substantial legal penalties and damage our reputation in the global marketplace.

Government contracts subject us to unique risks and rigorous audit requirements.

As a provider of defense and space solutions, we are subject to specific government contracting regulations that differ significantly from commercial business practices. Our government contracts often allow the customer to terminate the agreement for convenience, which can lead to the sudden loss of expected revenue. We are also subject to routine audits and investigations by government agencies. If an audit reveals that we have overcharged the government or failed to comply with cost accounting standards, we may be required to refund payments or pay significant penalties. Furthermore, any negative findings in a government audit could jeopardize our eligibility for future contract awards.

Compliance with environmental, health, and safety laws is costly and complex.

Our manufacturing operations involve the use of hazardous materials and the generation of industrial waste, making us subject to numerous environmental, health, and safety (EHS) laws. These regulations govern air emissions, water discharges, and the handling and disposal of toxic substances. Compliance with these laws requires significant ongoing expenditures for pollution control equipment and waste management. We could also be held liable for the costs of cleaning up contaminated sites, even if the contamination occurred before we acquired the property or was caused by a third party. As environmental regulations become increasingly stringent, particularly regarding carbon emissions and chemical usage, our compliance costs are expected to rise.

Cybersecurity threats and data breaches could disrupt our operations and damage our reputation.

We rely heavily on information technology systems to manage our business, design our products, and communicate with our customers and suppliers. These systems are vulnerable to cyberattacks, including malware, ransomware, and phishing attempts. As a defense contractor, we may be targeted by sophisticated actors seeking to steal sensitive technical data or intellectual property. A successful breach of our security systems could result in the loss of proprietary information, the disruption of our manufacturing processes, and the unauthorized disclosure of confidential employee or customer data. Such an event could lead to significant financial losses, legal liability, and a loss of trust among our government and commercial partners.

Intellectual property risks could undermine our competitive advantage.

Our success depends in part on our ability to protect our proprietary technology and intellectual property. We rely on a combination of patents, trademarks, and trade secrets to safeguard our innovations. However, our patents may be challenged, invalidated, or circumvented by competitors. In some international jurisdictions, the protection of intellectual property rights may be weak or difficult to enforce. If we are unable to adequately protect our technology, our competitors may be able to develop similar products, which would erode our market position and reduce our profitability. Conversely, we may face claims that our products infringe on the intellectual property rights of others, leading to expensive litigation and potential royalty payments.

General Risks

Unforeseen catastrophic events could disrupt our business.

Our operations could be adversely affected by natural disasters, extreme weather events, pandemics, or acts of terrorism. Such events could damage our manufacturing facilities, disrupt our supply chain, or lead to a sudden decline in air travel demand. For example, a major earthquake in a region where we or our key suppliers operate could cause significant production delays. While we maintain business interruption insurance, the coverage may not be sufficient to compensate us for all losses. The frequency and severity of certain weather-related events may also be increasing due to the effects of climate change, posing a long-term risk to our physical assets and operational stability.

The effects of climate change may impact our operations and financial results.

Beyond the regulatory and technological risks associated with the transition to a low-carbon economy, physical climate change poses risks to our business. These include the potential for increased cooling costs at our facilities, disruptions to transportation networks used for our supply chain, and the risk of flooding at coastal manufacturing sites. Furthermore, if the public perception of the environmental impact of aviation worsens, it could lead to permanent changes in travel behavior and a long-term reduction in the demand for commercial aircraft. We may also face increased pressure from investors and other stakeholders to achieve ambitious sustainability targets, which could require significant additional investment.

Our results of operations may be affected by changes in tax laws and effective tax rates.

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and various foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate can be influenced by changes in tax laws, the mix of our earnings across different regions, and the outcome of tax audits. In fiscal 2024, our effective tax rate was 19.5%, compared to 20.6% in fiscal 2023. Any future changes in corporate tax rates or the elimination of certain tax incentives, such as those for research and development, could increase our tax expense and reduce our net income. We also maintain deferred tax assets, and if we determine that it is more likely than not that these assets will not be realized, we would be required to record a valuation allowance, which would adversely impact our financial results.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock is subject to significant fluctuations in response to various factors, including our quarterly financial results, changes in earnings estimates by analysts, and general market conditions. The aerospace and defense sector as a whole can be volatile, and news regarding major aircraft programs or geopolitical events can lead to rapid changes in our stock price. Furthermore, large-scale sales of our stock by institutional investors or the perception that such sales might occur could put downward pressure on the market price. Volatility in our stock price could make it more difficult for us to use our equity as currency for acquisitions or to attract and retain employees through equity-based compensation.

Our headquarters in Columbus, Ohio, and our Delaware incorporation subject us to specific legal frameworks.

As a Delaware corporation, we are governed by the Delaware General Corporation Law, which includes provisions that could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us, even if such an acquisition would be beneficial to our stockholders. These include various anti-takeover protections and rules regarding the conduct of stockholder meetings. Additionally, our significant presence in Columbus, Ohio, subjects us to local economic conditions and state-level regulations. Any adverse changes in the business climate in Ohio or new state-level mandates could impact our operating costs and our ability to manage our headquarters effectively.

Total Revenue and scale of operations.

In fiscal 2024, our Revenue was $10,928 million, reflecting the scale and complexity of our global operations. The management of a business of this size requires sophisticated internal controls and reporting systems. Any failure to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting could lead to inaccuracies in our financial statements and a loss of investor confidence. As we grow through internal investment and potential acquisitions, the complexity of our operations will continue to increase, requiring ongoing enhancements to our management and information systems. If we are unable to scale our infrastructure effectively, our ability to manage the risks described above will be diminished.
