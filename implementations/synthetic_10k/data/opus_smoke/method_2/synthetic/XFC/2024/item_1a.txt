# ITEM 1A. RISK FACTORS

Our business, financial condition, results of operations, liquidity, and prospects are subject to many risks and uncertainties. You should carefully consider the risks described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. That information includes Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," Item 7A, "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk," and our consolidated financial statements and the related notes in Item 8, "Financial Statements and Supplementary Data."

Any of the following risks could materially and adversely affect us. If that occurs, the trading price of our common stock and other securities could decline. The risks below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks that we do not currently know about, or that we currently consider immaterial, could also impair our business. Many of these risks are interrelated, and the occurrence of one may increase the likelihood or severity of others.

The risks are organized under headings for convenience only. A risk that appears under one heading may also be relevant to others. The order in which the risks appear is not a representation of their relative likelihood or potential magnitude. Some statements in this Item 1A are forward-looking statements. Please also refer to the cautionary statement regarding forward-looking statements included elsewhere in this Annual Report.

## Summary of Risk Factors

The following is a summary of the principal risks that could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

**Insurance operations and underwriting**

- Our loss and loss adjustment expense reserves may prove inadequate, which would reduce our earnings and capital.
- Legacy asbestos and environmental exposures, and other long-latency claims, may develop adversely.
- Social inflation and adverse litigation trends may increase the frequency and severity of casualty claims beyond our expectations.
- Economic inflation in repair, construction, medical, and labor costs may cause loss costs to outpace our pricing.
- Our pricing and underwriting decisions may prove inaccurate, and we may be unable to obtain adequate rates.
- The cyclical and highly competitive nature of the property and casualty industry may reduce our premium volume and margins.

**Catastrophe, climate, and reinsurance**

- Natural and man-made catastrophes, including events whose frequency or severity may be increasing because of climate change, could cause significant losses.
- Catastrophe models and our own exposure management tools may not accurately estimate our potential losses.
- Reinsurance may become unavailable or more expensive, and our reinsurers may fail to pay amounts owed to us.

**Distribution, competition, and ratings**

- We depend on independent agents, brokers, and program administrators, and consolidation or disruption in these channels could reduce our business.
- Technological change, new competitors, and changes in vehicle technology could erode our competitive position.
- A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could impair our ability to write business and access capital.

**Analytics, technology, and operations**

- Our proprietary models, analytics, and artificial intelligence tools may be flawed, misused, or restricted by regulation.
- Cybersecurity incidents, system failures, and disruptions at third-party providers could harm our operations and reputation.
- We may fail to execute our technology modernization program successfully.
- We may be unable to attract and retain key personnel.

**Investments**

- Changes in interest rates, credit spreads, and equity markets, along with defaults, illiquidity, and valuation uncertainty, could reduce the value of our investment portfolio and our investment income.

**Regulation and legal matters**

- Extensive and evolving insurance and other regulation may restrict our pricing, underwriting, data use, capital management, and ability to exit markets.
- Assessments from residual markets and guaranty associations, and adverse outcomes in litigation and regulatory proceedings, could increase our costs.

**Capital, liquidity, and our common stock**

- As a holding company, we depend on dividends from our insurance subsidiaries, which are restricted by law.
- Our capital and liquidity could be insufficient under severe stress, and we may be unable to raise capital on acceptable terms.
- Insurance laws and our governing documents may discourage takeover attempts, and the price of our common stock may be volatile.

## Risks Relating to Our Insurance Operations and Underwriting

***Our reserves for losses and loss adjustment expenses are estimates and may prove inadequate, which could materially reduce our earnings and stockholders' equity.***

We maintain reserves to cover our estimated ultimate liability for losses and loss adjustment expenses on claims that have been reported and on claims that have been incurred but not yet reported. Reserves do not represent an exact calculation of liability. They are management's best estimates at a given point in time. We develop them using actuarial and statistical projections, our claims professionals' judgment about individual claims, and assumptions about future events and trends. Those trends include claim frequency and severity, the timing of claim reporting and settlement, economic and social inflation, medical cost trends, judicial interpretations of coverage and liability, legislative changes, and the effectiveness of our claims-handling practices.

Many of these factors are outside our control and cannot be measured or predicted precisely. The inherent uncertainty is greatest in long-tail casualty lines, including general liability, commercial umbrella and excess liability, workers' compensation, professional and management liability, and excess and surplus casualty. In these lines claims may be reported or settled many years after the policy period. Reserve estimates for newer products, including cyber, are also more uncertain because we and the industry have limited historical data to support our assumptions. Changes in our own claims practices, including the adoption of new claims technologies, automation, and analytics, may distort the historical development patterns on which our actuarial methods rely.

If actual losses exceed our reserve estimates, we must increase reserves. Any increase is recorded as a charge to earnings in the period in which the deficiency is identified, even though the underlying losses may relate to policies written in prior years. As of December 31, 2024, our total liabilities were approximately $88.6 billion, compared with total stockholders' equity of approximately $21.7 billion. Total liabilities include our reserves for losses and loss adjustment expenses, unearned premiums, debt, and other obligations. Because of this relationship, a change in our liability estimates that is small in relative terms can have a disproportionate effect on our equity. For illustration, an increase in total liabilities equal to 1% of their year-end carrying amount, with no offsetting change in assets, would reduce stockholders' equity by approximately $886 million, or about 4.1%.

Significant reserve increases could:

- materially reduce our net income and stockholders' equity;
- reduce the statutory surplus of our insurance subsidiaries and their capacity to write business;
- result in rating agency downgrades;
- limit the dividends our insurance subsidiaries can pay to the holding company; and
- undermine the confidence of investors, regulators, and distribution partners in our reserving practices.

***Our legacy asbestos and environmental exposures, and other long-latency or newly emerging claims, may develop in ways we cannot reliably predict.***

Corporate and Other includes the run-off of asbestos and environmental exposures arising from general liability policies written many years ago by our predecessor companies. Estimating ultimate liability for these claims is far more uncertain than for most other categories of claims. The reasons include long and variable reporting lags, unresolved and evolving legal issues regarding coverage and liability, the difficulty of identifying the sources of contamination and allocating responsibility among potentially responsible parties, and uncertainty about the number of future claimants and the cost of remediation.

Courts have at times interpreted older policy language in ways that expand coverage beyond what the original underwriters intended. Examples include rulings on the number of occurrences, the allocation of losses across policy periods, the application of aggregate limits, and the treatment of defense costs. Bankruptcies of defendants, the creation of settlement trusts, and changes in plaintiffs' litigation strategies have also shifted claim activity among defendants and insurers in ways that are difficult to anticipate. Legislative or regulatory changes, such as more stringent cleanup standards or the designation of new hazardous substances, could increase our exposure.

We also face the risk that new categories of latent or mass tort claims will emerge from coverage we have written or are writing today. Examples include claims alleging injury from emerging contaminants, chemicals, or products, privacy and biometric data claims, and claims arising from statutes that revive previously time-barred abuse or molestation claims. Coverage for such claims may not have been contemplated in our pricing or reserving. Our dedicated run-off unit and periodic reserve studies may not identify adverse trends in time, and future reserve increases for legacy or emerging exposures could be material.

***Social inflation and adverse litigation trends could increase the frequency and severity of casualty claims beyond what we have anticipated in our pricing and reserves.***

Our Commercial Lines, Specialty Lines, and Personal Lines casualty businesses are exposed to litigation trends commonly described as "social inflation." These trends include:

- increasing attorney involvement in claims;
- larger jury verdicts, including so-called "nuclear" verdicts that substantially exceed economic damages;
- plaintiff-friendly changes in tort law, rules of evidence, and procedure;
- expanded theories of liability and the erosion of tort reform measures;
- third-party litigation funding that can prolong litigation and raise settlement expectations;
- aggressive advertising by plaintiffs' firms; and
- changing public attitudes toward corporations and insurers.

These forces affect commercial automobile, general liability, commercial and personal umbrella, and excess liability most acutely. They also affect personal automobile, professional and management liability, and premises exposures within our habitational and hospitality verticals. Because the effects emerge gradually and vary significantly by jurisdiction, venue, and even individual judge, they are difficult to quantify and may not appear in our loss data until years after the related policies are written. Our umbrella and excess coverages are particularly sensitive: a moderate increase in the severity of underlying claims can produce a much larger proportional increase in losses that pierce higher attachment points.

Our litigation management practices, staff and panel counsel arrangements, claims analytics, and early-intervention strategies may not offset these trends. If loss cost trends in our casualty lines continue to exceed our assumptions, we may need to strengthen reserves for prior accident years and raise rates further. We may be unable to obtain those rate increases in a timely manner or at all, particularly in competitive markets or in states where rate approval is required.

***Economic inflation in repair, construction, medical, and labor costs could cause our loss costs to rise faster than our premiums.***

The cost of settling property and casualty claims is directly affected by economic inflation. Building materials, construction labor, vehicle parts, vehicle repair labor, rental car costs, medical services, pharmaceuticals, and wage levels all influence the amounts we pay on claims. In recent years many of these costs have risen sharply and, at times, unpredictably, driven by supply chain disruptions, labor shortages, tariffs and trade policy, and post-catastrophe demand surges.

Vehicle repair severity has been especially affected by the increasing presence of advanced driver assistance systems, sensors, cameras, and other technologies that are costly to repair and recalibrate. Elevated used vehicle values have increased the cost of total losses. In property lines, rising replacement costs may cause insured values to lag actual rebuilding costs. That lag can result in inadequate premiums relative to exposure, and it can increase our losses where policies provide extended or guaranteed replacement cost coverage.

Because premiums are set before losses are known, and many claims take time to settle, inflation that exceeds our assumptions can make our in-force business unprofitable and leave our reserves inadequate. Inflation-related assumptions are embedded in our rate indications, our reserve estimates, and our catastrophe modeling. Errors in any of them could adversely affect our results. Inflation also affects our operating expenses, including compensation, technology, and vendor costs. Conversely, if we raise rates to address inflation and inflation subsequently moderates more than we expect, our pricing may become uncompetitive and our retention may decline.

***Our pricing and underwriting decisions may prove inaccurate, and we may be unable to obtain or maintain adequate rates.***

Our profitability depends substantially on our ability to underwrite risks appropriately and charge premiums adequate to cover expected losses, loss adjustment expenses, underwriting expenses, the cost of reinsurance, and a reasonable return on capital. Pricing requires us to estimate the cost of losses that will be incurred over the policy term and paid, in some cases, many years later. To make those estimates we must:

- collect and properly analyze a substantial volume of internal and third-party data;
- develop, test, and apply appropriate rating formulas and models;
- monitor and respond promptly to changes in loss frequency, severity, and mix of business;
- project future loss cost trends accurately; and
- obtain regulatory approval of rate changes where required, in a timely manner.

We may fail to do any of these things accurately. Our risk selection and pricing models may be built on incomplete or erroneous data, may fail to capture emerging trends, or may be applied inconsistently by underwriters or automated systems. In our small commercial digital platform, eligible submissions may be issued without underwriter review. Errors in automated appetite, eligibility, or pricing rules could therefore affect a large number of policies before they are detected.

Regulatory approval processes, particularly in personal lines and in states where political considerations influence rate decisions, can delay or prevent the implementation of rate changes we believe are actuarially justified. Competitive pressures may also cause us to accept rates below our targets in order to retain profitable relationships. In addition, our strategy of declining business that does not meet our pricing standards may reduce our premium volume and spread fixed expenses over a smaller base, which would increase our expense ratio.

***The property and casualty insurance industry is cyclical and highly competitive, which may reduce our premium volume, margins, and market share.***

The property and casualty industry has historically experienced cyclical periods of price competition and relaxed underwriting standards, followed by periods of rising rates and tighter terms. Industry capital levels, catastrophe and loss experience, investment returns, the availability of reinsurance, and the entry and exit of competitors all influence these cycles, which may vary by line of business and geography. We cannot predict the timing or duration of market cycles. In soft market conditions, maintaining underwriting discipline may cause us to lose accounts, reduce our growth, or forgo opportunities. Writing business at inadequate rates would impair our profitability.

We compete with large national multi-line carriers, regional insurers, specialty and surplus lines insurers, mutual and reciprocal insurers, and technology-focused entrants. Many of them have greater financial, marketing, or technological resources, broader product offerings, or lower costs than we do. Personal automobile insurance is highly price-competitive and increasingly shaped by carriers with large advertising budgets, direct-to-consumer distribution, and comparative rating tools that make price comparison easy. Our emphasis on account-based personal lines business written through independent agents may not appeal to consumers who prioritize the lowest available price.

In our specialty and excess and surplus lines businesses, abundant capital from new market participants, alternative capital providers, and managing general agents supported by third-party capacity has increased competition. Competitors may offer broader coverage terms, higher limits, or lower prices than we are willing to offer. If we cannot compete effectively, our premium volume, retention, and profitability could decline.

***Our personal automobile business is exposed to changes in driving patterns, vehicle technology, repair costs, and claims practices that could adversely affect its profitability.***

Personal automobile results depend on the frequency and severity of claims. These are influenced by miles driven, distracted driving, road congestion, speeding, weather, vehicle technology, repair costs, parts availability, medical costs, and litigation trends. Shifts in driving behavior can change loss frequency quickly and unexpectedly, and our rates may not respond with sufficient speed. Examples include changes in commuting patterns resulting from hybrid work, or periods of increased reckless driving.

The growing complexity of vehicles has materially increased average repair costs, and that trend may continue as electric vehicles and more advanced driver assistance systems become prevalent. Electric vehicles can present distinct repair, total loss, and fire risks, and the industry's experience with them is still developing. The adoption of autonomous and semi-autonomous vehicles could, over time, shift liability from drivers to manufacturers and software providers, reduce the size of the personal automobile insurance market, or change how coverage is purchased. Each of these changes could adversely affect our Personal Lines segment.

Our use of telematics and other driving data is subject to evolving consumer protection and privacy requirements. Regulatory restrictions on, or consumer reluctance to share, such data could reduce the effectiveness of our pricing segmentation.

***Our Specialty Lines products involve low-frequency, high-severity exposures and, in the case of cyber insurance, the potential for correlated losses across many insureds.***

Many of the coverages written by our Specialty Lines segment can produce large losses from individual claims or events. These coverages include excess and surplus casualty and property, management liability, professional liability, cyber, and surety. Loss experience in these lines can be volatile from period to period, and a small number of large claims can significantly affect segment results. Errors in policy form wording, limit management, attachment point selection, or aggregation controls could expose us to losses we did not intend to cover.

Cyber insurance presents particular risks. The threat environment changes rapidly. Ransomware and extortion tactics, the techniques and capabilities of threat actors (including state-sponsored actors), and the vulnerabilities of widely used software and service providers all evolve constantly. A single event could affect many of our policyholders simultaneously, producing aggregate losses well beyond those reflected in our pricing or modeled expectations. Examples include a widespread malware attack, a failure of a major cloud or software provider, or an attack on critical infrastructure. Cyber catastrophe models are relatively immature, and the legal interpretation of cyber coverage terms, war and infrastructure exclusions, and silent cyber exposures in traditional property and casualty policies continues to develop. Our reinsurance protection for cyber accumulations may be insufficient or may not respond as we expect.

Surety losses tend to be correlated with economic downturns, construction industry stress, and rising interest rates. During such periods, contractor defaults may increase across our book at the same time.

***Our delegated authority arrangements with program administrators and managing general agents expose us to risks that could result in unexpected losses and regulatory or reputational harm.***

In our program business and certain other arrangements, we grant underwriting, binding, and in some cases claims authority to program administrators, managing general agents, and other third parties. These parties may:

- exceed their authority;
- deviate from our underwriting guidelines;
- write business outside our intended appetite;
- misprice risks;
- fail to remit premiums;
- handle claims improperly;
- fail to comply with applicable laws and regulations; or
- engage in fraud or misconduct.

Our due diligence, written agreements, audits, data feeds, and contractual rights may not detect problems promptly or allow us to correct them before significant losses occur. Even where business is written within the scope of an agreement, the interests of a program administrator compensated on premium volume may not be fully aligned with our interest in underwriting profitability, despite profit-sharing and contingent commission structures. We remain responsible to policyholders and regulators for business written on our behalf. Regulators have also increased their scrutiny of insurers' oversight of delegated authority arrangements. Failures by our partners could result in underwriting losses, regulatory sanctions, litigation, and damage to our reputation. The termination of a significant program relationship, whether initiated by us or by the administrator, could reduce our premium volume or require us to run off a book of business at a loss.

***Changes in workers' compensation laws, medical costs, and the economy could adversely affect our workers' compensation results.***

Workers' compensation benefits are determined by state statute, and legislative, regulatory, and judicial changes can increase benefit levels, expand compensability, or alter medical fee schedules, sometimes retroactively. Examples include presumptions that certain illnesses are work-related, expansions of mental health coverage, and changes in permanent disability rating systems. Medical cost inflation, the increasing cost and use of pharmaceuticals and specialized treatments, and longer life expectancies for injured workers receiving lifetime medical benefits may cause our ultimate costs to exceed our estimates.

Workers' compensation is also exposed to catastrophic events, such as industrial accidents, terrorism, or pandemics, that injure many employees of one or more insureds at the same time. Our workers' compensation catastrophe reinsurance may not fully protect us against such events. In addition, workers' compensation premiums are generally based on payroll. An economic downturn that reduces employment and payrolls would therefore reduce our premiums, while claims frequency and duration may increase as injured workers face fewer opportunities to return to work.

***Judicial and legislative actions may expand coverage beyond our intentions or impose new liabilities.***

As industry practices and legal, judicial, social, and other conditions change, unexpected issues related to claims and coverage may emerge. Courts may interpret policy language in ways that differ from our intent or that conflict with the understanding underlying our pricing. They may also invalidate exclusions, limitations, or conditions on which we rely. Legislatures may enact laws that extend statutes of limitations, create new causes of action, mandate coverage for specified perils or circumstances, or require retroactive application of new coverage requirements. Following major catastrophes, regulators and legislatures may pressure insurers to pay claims that fall outside policy terms, such as flood losses under policies that exclude flood or business interruption claims without physical damage, or to adopt claims-handling accommodations that increase costs. The effects of these changes may not become apparent until long after the related policies were written and the premiums earned. They could materially increase our losses and require reserve strengthening.

## Risks Relating to Catastrophes, Climate, and Reinsurance

***Natural and man-made catastrophes could cause significant losses and materially affect our results of operations, financial condition, and liquidity.***

As a writer of property insurance in our Personal Lines, Commercial Lines, and Specialty Lines segments, we are exposed to losses from natural and man-made catastrophes. Natural perils include:

- hurricanes and tropical storms;
- severe convective storms, including tornadoes, hail, and straight-line winds;
- winter storms and freezing events;
- wildfires;
- earthquakes; and
- floods, to the extent covered.

Man-made perils include terrorism, civil unrest, explosions, industrial accidents, and large-scale cyber events. Catastrophes can also affect our casualty, workers' compensation, automobile physical damage, inland marine, and business interruption exposures.

The incidence and severity of catastrophes are inherently unpredictable. A single severe event, or a series of events within a short period, could cause losses that exceed our expectations, our risk tolerances, or the protection provided by our reinsurance. Our significant concentrations of business in the northern regions of the United States, including New England and the Mid-Atlantic, expose us to winter storms, nor'easters, and freezing temperatures, as well as hurricanes that track along the Atlantic coast. Severe convective storms, which may not individually reach the attachment points of our catastrophe reinsurance, can produce substantial aggregate losses over a season.

Catastrophe losses can be magnified by several factors:

- demand surge in materials and labor;
- prolonged business interruption;
- regulatory mandates such as moratoria on cancellations and extended grace periods;
- disputes over causation and coverage; and
- increased fraud.

Catastrophes may also impair our own facilities and personnel, the operations of our vendors and independent adjusters, and the ability of policyholders to pay premiums. They may require us to liquidate investments to fund claims at unfavorable prices, reduce the statutory surplus of our insurance subsidiaries, and trigger assessments from residual market mechanisms and guaranty associations. Our quarterly and annual results may fluctuate significantly as a result.

***Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of weather-related losses, reduce the availability and affordability of insurance, and affect our investments and regulatory environment.***

Changing climate conditions may be increasing the frequency, severity, duration, and geographic range of weather-related events. Examples include more intense hurricanes, increased precipitation and flooding, more frequent severe convective storms, prolonged droughts, more extensive wildfires, and rising sea levels that amplify storm surge. Loss trends in secondary perils, which historically were considered attritional, appear to be increasing, and catastrophe models may not adequately capture these changes. Population growth and development in exposed areas, including the wildland-urban interface and coastal regions, and rising property values and replacement costs compound the potential for loss.

These trends could cause our actual catastrophe losses to exceed modeled expectations and our pricing assumptions. They could increase the cost and reduce the availability of reinsurance. They could also affect the affordability and availability of insurance in some markets, increasing political pressure on insurers and regulators and potentially producing restrictions on rate increases, non-renewals, or market withdrawals. Although most of our policies have one-year terms that allow repricing, regulatory constraints may limit our ability to respond. Rapid shifts in risk may outpace our ability to adjust pricing and exposures.

Climate change and the transition to a lower-carbon economy may also affect the value of our investments. Issuers in carbon-intensive industries, municipalities and real estate in exposed locations, and securities backed by vulnerable collateral could all be affected. Evolving climate-related disclosure requirements, regulatory expectations, and stakeholder scrutiny may increase our compliance costs and expose us to reputational risk, including competing and sometimes conflicting expectations from different stakeholders and jurisdictions.

***Catastrophe models and our exposure management tools have limitations and may not accurately estimate our potential losses.***

We use licensed third-party catastrophe models, adjusted with our own views of risk, along with our property exposure and accumulation tools, to estimate probable maximum losses, set risk tolerances, price business, manage aggregations, and structure our reinsurance program. These models depend on assumptions and inputs that may prove inaccurate. They include assumptions about the frequency and severity of events, the vulnerability of insured structures, demand surge, policy terms, and the quality of our exposure data, including geocoding, construction, occupancy, and insured values.

Catastrophe models are inherently imprecise. Different models can produce materially different results, and model vendors periodically revise their models, sometimes substantially. Many perils are not modeled or are modeled with limited reliability. These include certain secondary perils, flood, wildfire in some regions, cyber, terrorism, and pandemic. Our actual losses from catastrophe events may differ materially from modeled estimates. If our models underestimate our exposure, we may write more catastrophe-exposed business than our risk appetite permits, charge inadequate premiums, or purchase insufficient reinsurance. Modeled loss estimates also inform rating agency capital assessments and regulatory capital requirements, and model changes could increase our required capital.

***Reinsurance may become unavailable or more costly, or may not provide the protection we expect.***

We purchase reinsurance to limit our exposure to large losses and catastrophe accumulations, protect our capital and earnings, and support our capacity to write business. The availability, terms, and cost of reinsurance depend on market conditions beyond our control. Those conditions include industry catastrophe experience, reinsurer capital levels, investment returns, the cost of capital, and the participation of alternative capital providers.

Property catastrophe reinsurance pricing has risen materially in recent renewal cycles, and reinsurers have sought higher attachment points, narrower coverage terms, and stricter exclusions. Casualty reinsurers have similarly responded to adverse loss trends by tightening terms and increasing pricing. If reinsurance becomes unavailable on acceptable terms, we may have to retain more risk, reduce the amount of business we write, raise our prices, or accept lower margins. Any of these could adversely affect our results, growth, and competitive position.

Reinsurance agreements may not respond as we intend. Disputes may arise over the interpretation of contract terms, the definition of an event or occurrence, the aggregation of losses, exclusions, and the allocation of losses across contract years. Our program may contain gaps in coverage, and losses may exceed the limits purchased or exhaust available reinstatements. Severe events late in a contract year, or multiple events in a single year, could leave us with less protection than planned.

***We are exposed to the credit risk of our reinsurers.***

Reinsurance does not relieve us of our primary obligations to policyholders. If a reinsurer is unable or unwilling to pay amounts owed to us, whether because of insolvency, financial deterioration, disputes, sanctions, or other reasons, we remain liable for the underlying losses. Reinsurer financial condition can deteriorate rapidly, particularly after major industry catastrophe events that affect many reinsurers simultaneously, or after adverse developments in long-tail casualty lines. Reinsurance recoverables on long-tail business may remain outstanding for many years, extending our exposure.

Collateral arrangements, including trust accounts, letters of credit, and funds withheld, may be insufficient, may decline in value, or may not be available when needed. Recent regulatory changes have reduced collateral requirements for certain qualified reinsurers, which may reduce the security available to us. Uncollectible reinsurance recoverables, or increases in our allowance for uncollectible reinsurance, could materially reduce our earnings and statutory surplus.

***Terrorism and related losses could adversely affect us, and changes to the federal terrorism risk program could increase our exposure.***

We are exposed to losses from acts of terrorism in our commercial property, workers' compensation, general liability, and other lines. Under federal law we must make terrorism coverage available to commercial policyholders in covered lines. Workers' compensation coverage generally cannot exclude terrorism. Although the Terrorism Risk Insurance Program provides a federal backstop for certified acts of terrorism, we retain significant exposure through the program's insurer deductible and coinsurance requirements, losses from events that are not certified or do not meet program triggers, and exposures in lines not covered by the program.

Our concentrations of commercial business and employees in metropolitan areas of the Northeast increase our potential exposure to terrorism losses. The program is subject to periodic reauthorization by Congress. If it were not reauthorized, or were reauthorized with higher insurer retentions or narrower coverage, our exposure would increase and terrorism reinsurance might be unavailable or prohibitively expensive. Terrorism losses involving nuclear, biological, chemical, or radiological agents could be particularly severe and are difficult to model or reinsure.

***Pandemics and other widespread public health events could adversely affect our operations, claims, and investments.***

A pandemic or other widespread public health event could affect our business in many ways:

- increasing claims under workers' compensation, management liability, event-related, and other coverages;
- prompting legislative, regulatory, or judicial efforts to require coverage for losses that our policies were not intended to cover, such as business interruption without physical damage;
- disrupting our operations, those of our distribution partners and vendors, and the economy generally;
- reducing premiums through lower exposures, payroll, and economic activity;
- impairing policyholders' ability to pay premiums, while regulators may mandate premium grace periods or refunds; and
- causing volatility and losses in our investment portfolio.

The effects of any such event would depend on its severity, duration, and the responses of governments, businesses, and individuals, none of which we can predict.

## Risks Relating to Distribution, Competition, and Ratings

***We depend on independent agents and brokers, and consolidation, disruption, or deterioration in these relationships could reduce our premium volume and profitability.***

We distribute our products primarily through independent agents, brokers, wholesale brokers, and program administrators. These parties are not our employees and generally represent multiple insurers, including our competitors. They may direct business to other carriers that offer higher commissions, broader appetite, easier technology interfaces, or more competitive pricing. Our strategy of being a meaningful market for a select group of producers, rather than a marginal market for many, means that the loss of, or a significant reduction in business from, one or more key producer relationships could have a disproportionate effect on our premium volume in particular regions or products.

Consolidation among agencies and brokerages, frequently financed by private equity, has increased the share of business produced by larger distribution organizations. These firms may have greater bargaining power to negotiate higher commissions, more favorable terms, or other concessions. They may consolidate their carrier panels, or they may develop their own underwriting facilities or managing general agencies that compete with us for business. Changes in ownership or management of agencies with which we have strong relationships could disrupt those relationships.

Producer compensation practices, including contingent and supplemental commissions, have been subject to regulatory scrutiny and litigation in the past and could be again, which could limit our ability to compensate producers in ways that align their interests with ours. In addition, if consumers and businesses increasingly purchase insurance directly from carriers, through digital platforms, or through embedded insurance arrangements with retailers, automobile manufacturers, and other partners, the independent agency channel could lose share. Our business would be adversely affected.

***Technological change and new competitors could disrupt our business model and erode our competitive advantages.***

The insurance industry is undergoing rapid technological change. Established competitors and new entrants are investing heavily in:

- digital distribution;
- telematics and connected devices;
- data analytics and artificial intelligence;
- automated underwriting and claims processing; and
- embedded insurance.

Our name and strategy reflect our commitment to analytics-driven underwriting. If competitors develop superior data, models, or technology, or if analytical capabilities become commoditized through widely available third-party tools and data, our competitive differentiation could diminish. Competitors with greater resources may be able to invest more in technology than we can, and some may be willing to accept lower returns or operate at a loss to gain market share.

Changes in technology outside the insurance industry could also reduce demand for our products or change the nature of the risks we insure. Examples include autonomous vehicles, smart home devices, advanced building technologies, and new forms of work and commerce. Our failure to anticipate or respond effectively to these changes, or to invest in the right technologies at the right time, could adversely affect our competitive position and results.

***A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could adversely affect our ability to write business, retain customers, obtain reinsurance, and access capital.***

Financial strength ratings are an important competitive factor in the property and casualty industry. Agents, brokers, commercial customers, lenders, and other counterparties consider an insurer's ratings when choosing a carrier, and some require minimum ratings as a condition of doing business. Rating agencies periodically review our ratings and may change their methodologies or capital models at any time. They consider many factors in assigning ratings, including:

- capital adequacy, as measured by their own models;
- operating performance;
- reserve adequacy;
- catastrophe exposure;
- reinsurance program;
- investment portfolio quality;
- enterprise risk management; and
- business profile.

Factors that could lead to a downgrade include significant catastrophe losses, reserve strengthening, investment losses, increased financial leverage, changes in rating agency methodologies, and adverse developments affecting the industry generally. A downgrade of the financial strength ratings of our insurance subsidiaries could reduce our ability to write new business and retain existing business, particularly in commercial and specialty lines. It could also trigger contractual rights of policyholders, reinsurers, or other counterparties, increase our cost of reinsurance, and cause producers to move business to competitors. Because our admitted insurance subsidiaries participate in an intercompany pooling arrangement and are generally rated as a group, adverse developments in one part of our business may affect the ratings of all participants. A downgrade of the holding company's debt ratings could increase our borrowing costs and reduce our access to capital markets.

## Risks Relating to Our Analytics, Technology, and Operations

***Our proprietary models and analytical tools may be flawed, may be misapplied, or may fail to perform as expected, which could lead to poor underwriting, pricing, reserving, or capital decisions.***

Predictive models, data-driven tools, and quantitative analysis are central to our strategy. We use them throughout the policy life cycle:

- prospecting and risk appetite definition;
- submission triage and risk selection;
- pricing and rate adequacy monitoring;
- portfolio and accumulation management;
- claims triage, reserving, and fraud detection; and
- distribution management.

Our economic capital model informs capital allocation, pricing, and reinsurance decisions.

Models are simplified representations of complex realities. They depend on the quality, completeness, and relevance of the data used to build and run them, and on assumptions that may prove incorrect. Models may contain design errors, coding errors, or data defects. They may be calibrated on historical data that does not reflect current or future conditions. They may degrade over time as conditions change, which is sometimes called model drift. They may be used outside the purposes for which they were designed, or relied on excessively at the expense of human judgment. The interaction of multiple models across our processes may create unanticipated correlations or compound errors.

Our model risk management program may not prevent or detect these problems in time. That program includes development standards, independent validation, performance monitoring, documentation, and change management. If our models produce inaccurate results, we may:

- misprice business;
- select risks poorly;
- accumulate excessive exposures;
- set inadequate reserves;
- make inappropriate capital or reinsurance decisions; or
- treat customers unfairly in ways that expose us to regulatory or legal consequences.

Because our models are proprietary and protected primarily as trade secrets, they could also be misappropriated by employees, vendors, or others, and our ability to enforce our rights may be limited.

***Our use of artificial intelligence, machine learning, and external data is subject to evolving regulation, legal uncertainty, and operational risks.***

We use machine learning and, more recently, generative artificial intelligence tools in selected applications. Examples include extracting information from submission documents and medical records, summarizing claim files, triaging correspondence, and assisting software developers. These technologies present risks, including:

- inaccurate, incomplete, or fabricated outputs;
- embedded bias that could produce unfairly discriminatory outcomes;
- lack of explainability;
- leakage of confidential or personal information;
- infringement of third-party intellectual property; and
- dependence on vendors whose models we do not control and cannot fully evaluate.

Our governance policies, which require human review of consequential decisions, may not prevent all errors or misuse.

Regulators and legislators are increasingly focused on insurers' use of external consumer data, algorithms, predictive models, and artificial intelligence. A growing number of states have issued the NAIC's model bulletin on artificial intelligence. Some states have adopted or proposed laws and regulations that restrict or require testing of insurers' use of external consumer data and algorithms. Some have restricted particular rating factors, such as credit-based insurance scores, education, occupation, or gender, in certain lines. These requirements vary by jurisdiction, are subject to interpretation, and continue to evolve. Further restrictions could:

## Risks Relating to Our Investments

***Changes in interest rates could reduce the value of our fixed-income portfolio, our investment income, and our stockholders' equity.***

Our investment portfolio consists predominantly of investment-grade fixed maturity securities. The fair value of these securities is inversely related to interest rates. Rising interest rates reduce the fair value of our fixed-income portfolio and, because most of these securities are classified as available for sale, reduce our stockholders' equity through accumulated other comprehensive income. Unrealized losses could also reduce the statutory surplus of our insurance subsidiaries to the extent securities are carried at fair value, or if we are required to sell securities before they recover in value.

Our total assets were approximately $110.3 billion as of December 31, 2024, compared with total stockholders' equity of approximately $21.7 billion. Our assets were therefore roughly 5.1 times our equity. At that ratio, a decline in the carrying value of our assets equal to 1%, with no offsetting change in liabilities, would reduce stockholders' equity by approximately $1.1 billion, or about 5.1%. Changes in the value of our investments can therefore have a meaningful effect on our reported equity and book value per share.

Declining interest rates would reduce the yields available on new investments and reinvested cash flows, reducing our net investment income over time. Prepayments on mortgage-backed and other callable securities generally accelerate when interest rates fall, increasing reinvestment risk. Although we manage the duration of our fixed-income portfolio in relation to the expected payout patterns of our insurance liabilities, our liabilities are estimates, and a mismatch between the timing of asset and liability cash flows could require us to sell securities at a loss or to reinvest at lower yields. Interest rates are affected by monetary policy, inflation, fiscal policy, and other factors beyond our control.

***Credit risk, including defaults, downgrades, and widening credit spreads, could cause losses in our investment portfolio.***

We are exposed to the risk that issuers of securities we hold, borrowers on commercial mortgage loans, counterparties, and the obligors underlying structured securities may default or experience credit deterioration. Our portfolio includes corporate bonds, municipal bonds, agency and non-agency mortgage-backed securities, commercial mortgage-backed securities, asset-backed securities, and commercial mortgage loans. An economic downturn, sector-specific stress, or other adverse developments could increase defaults, downgrades, and credit losses. Examples of sector stress include continued weakness in office and certain other commercial real estate sectors, fiscal stress among municipalities, or deterioration in consumer credit.

Widening credit spreads reduce the fair value of our fixed-income securities even in the absence of defaults. Downgrades of securities we hold may increase the capital charges applied under risk-based capital and rating agency models. They may also cause securities to be treated as non-admitted assets or to exceed regulatory concentration limits. Concentrations in particular issuers, industries, geographies, or asset classes could magnify our losses.

***Our investments in less liquid assets, including limited partnerships, commercial mortgage loans, and certain structured securities, may be difficult to value or sell and may produce volatile returns.***

We hold selective allocations to other invested assets, such as limited partnerships and commercial mortgage loans, and certain structured securities that may have limited trading markets. These investments may be difficult to sell quickly or at prices approximating their carrying values, particularly during periods of market stress when we might need liquidity to pay catastrophe or other claims. Limited partnership investments are often reported on a lag, may require us to fund capital calls at times we would prefer not to, and may produce returns that are more volatile than those of our fixed-income portfolio. Commercial mortgage loans are exposed to changes in property values, occupancy, tenant credit, and refinancing conditions. Commercial real estate markets have experienced significant stress in some sectors, and that stress may continue.

***The valuation of our investments and the determination of credit loss allowances involve significant judgment and may change.***

Some of our investments do not trade actively, and their fair values are estimated using models and inputs that require significant judgment. During periods of market disruption, observable market inputs may become scarcer and valuation uncertainty may increase. Valuations may also differ materially from the prices we could realize in a sale.

We evaluate available-for-sale securities, commercial mortgage loans, and reinsurance and other receivables for expected credit losses. That evaluation requires judgment about issuers' and borrowers' financial condition, the value of collateral, and our intent and ability to hold securities. Changes in our assumptions, in market conditions, or in accounting standards could result in additional credit loss allowances, impairments, or realized losses. Any of these would reduce our net income and stockholders' equity.

***Volatility in equity markets could reduce the value of our equity investments and increase the volatility of our reported earnings.***

We hold a smaller allocation to publicly traded equity securities. Changes in the fair value of these securities are recognized in net income. Declines in equity markets would therefore reduce our net income and stockholders' equity, and equity market volatility increases the volatility of our reported results. Equity market declines may also coincide with other adverse developments, such as economic recessions, credit deterioration, or catastrophe events, compounding their effect on our financial condition.

- investments;
- reserves;
- affiliate transactions;
- changes in control;
- claims handling;
- cancellation and non-renewal;

Failure to comply with applicable requirements could result in:

- the suspension or revocation of licenses;
- restrictions on our ability to write business or implement rate changes;
- monetary penalties; and
- reputational harm.

Changes in the insurance regulatory framework, including increased federal involvement, the adoption of new NAIC model laws, or international standards that affect U.S. insurers indirectly, could increase our compliance costs or restrict our business in ways we cannot predict.

Most states regulate the rates that admitted insurers may charge. Many states restrict insurers' ability to cancel or non-renew policies, particularly in personal lines. These restrictions may limit our ability to manage catastrophe concentrations, to exit markets where rates are inadequate, and to implement the underwriting actions we have taken and may continue to take to improve the risk-adjusted profitability of our homeowners and other property businesses. We are subject to federal and state laws governing privacy, data security, and consumer protection. These include the privacy provisions of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, state insurance data security laws based on the NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law, state data breach notification laws, state comprehensive consumer privacy laws, and laws governing telemarketing, electronic communications, and biometric information. ## Risks Relating to Our Capital, Liquidity, and Holding Company Structure

***As a holding company, we depend on dividends and other distributions from our insurance subsidiaries, which are subject to regulatory restrictions.***

Harborview Analytics Co. is a holding company with no significant operations of its own. It depends primarily on dividends and other distributions from its subsidiaries to pay shareholder dividends, service debt, repurchase shares, and fund holding company expenses. State insurance laws limit the dividends our insurance subsidiaries may pay without prior regulatory approval, generally by reference to statutory surplus and statutory net income. They also permit dividends to be paid only from earned or unassigned surplus. Regulators may limit or prohibit dividends they consider inconsistent with an insurer's financial condition or the interests of policyholders, even if the dividends fall within statutory limits.

Catastrophe losses, reserve strengthening, investment losses, or growth in premiums written could reduce statutory surplus and net income and, as a result, reduce the dividends our insurance subsidiaries can pay. Regulators may also restrict dividends following adverse examination findings or during periods of industry stress. If our insurance subsidiaries cannot pay sufficient dividends, we may be unable to pay dividends to our shareholders, make payments on our debt, or repurchase shares. We might also be required to raise capital at the holding company level on unfavorable terms. Our rights to participate in the assets of our subsidiaries upon their liquidation or reorganization are effectively subordinated to the claims of those subsidiaries' policyholders and creditors.

***Our capital and liquidity may be insufficient under severe stress, and we may be unable to raise additional capital on acceptable terms.***

We need liquidity to pay claims, operating expenses, reinsurance premiums, interest on our debt, and dividends. Our primary sources of liquidity are premiums, investment income, maturities and sales of investments, reinsurance recoveries, and, at the holding company, dividends from subsidiaries and access to capital markets and credit facilities. Severe catastrophe events, significant reserve strengthening, a disruption in premium collections, or the unavailability of reinsurance recoveries could create liquidity needs that exceed our available resources. In such circumstances we might be required to sell investments at a loss, particularly if markets are disrupted.

If we need to raise additional capital to support growth, replace capital lost through adverse developments, or meet regulatory or rating agency requirements, we may be unable to do so on acceptable terms or at all. Our access to capital markets depends on market conditions, our financial condition, our ratings, and investor perceptions of our business and the industry, among other factors. Market disruptions that affect our access to capital may coincide with the events that create our need for it. Equity financing would dilute existing shareholders, and debt financing would increase our leverage and interest expense and could include restrictive covenants.

***Our level of liabilities relative to equity exposes us to risk from adverse changes in our assets or liabilities.***

As of December 31, 2024, we had total assets of approximately $110.3 billion, total liabilities of approximately $88.6 billion, and total stockholders' equity of approximately $21.7 billion. Our total liabilities represented approximately 80.3% of total assets and approximately 4.1 times our stockholders' equity. Stockholders' equity represented approximately 19.7% of total assets.

This balance sheet structure is characteristic of an insurance enterprise that holds substantial assets to support policyholder obligations. It means, however, that relatively modest percentage changes in the value of our assets or the estimated amount of our liabilities can produce much larger percentage changes in our equity. For example, an adverse change of 5% in the carrying amount of our total liabilities, with no offsetting change in assets, would equal approximately $4.4 billion, or roughly 20% of our year-end stockholders' equity. Adverse developments that affect both sides of the balance sheet simultaneously would have a compounded effect. An example would be a severe catastrophe that increases our liabilities at the same time that financial market stress reduces the value of our investments.

Our debt agreements and credit facilities may contain financial and other covenants, which may include minimum net worth and maximum leverage requirements. A significant decline in our equity could cause us to breach such covenants, limit our ability to incur additional indebtedness, or require us to seek waivers or amendments that may not be available on acceptable terms.

***Our intercompany pooling arrangement may spread the adverse effects of losses in one part of our business across all participating insurers.***

Our admitted insurance subsidiaries participate in an intercompany pooling agreement under which premiums, losses, loss adjustment expenses, and underwriting expenses are combined and reallocated according to fixed participation percentages approved by regulators. Pooling allows us to manage our admitted insurers as a single economic unit and provides each participant with a consistent financial strength profile. It also means that large or catastrophic losses, or adverse reserve development, in one line of business or region affect the statutory surplus, ratings, and dividend capacity of every participant. Changes to the pooling arrangement require regulatory approval, which may not be obtained or may be subject to conditions. Regulators may also scrutinize the arrangement in the event of financial stress at any participant.

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors, many of which are beyond our control. These factors include:

They depend on our results of operations, financial condition, capital requirements, rating agency considerations, the availability of dividends from our insurance subsidiaries, and other factors. We may reduce or suspend dividends or share repurchases at any time, including following significant catastrophe losses or other adverse developments, and any such action could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

## General Risk Factors

***Adverse economic, political, and geopolitical conditions could reduce demand for our products, increase our losses, and impair our investments.***

Our business is sensitive to general economic conditions in the United States. An economic downturn could:

- reduce demand for our products and the exposures on which premiums are based, such as payroll, sales, and insured values;
- increase policy cancellations and non-payment of premiums;
- increase claims frequency in certain lines, such as workers' compensation, surety, management liability, and fraud-prone coverages;
- increase credit losses and reduce the value of our investments; and
- impair the financial condition of our reinsurers, producers, and vendors.

Conversely, periods of strong economic activity may be accompanied by inflation that increases our loss costs. Geopolitical events, including armed conflicts, trade disputes, tariffs, sanctions, and political instability, can disrupt supply chains, increase costs, cause financial market volatility, and heighten cyber threats. Changes in government fiscal and monetary policy, including government shutdowns or disputes over the federal debt limit, could also disrupt financial markets and the economy. We cannot predict the timing, duration, or severity of economic or geopolitical developments or their effects on our business.

***Damage to our reputation could adversely affect our business.***

Our reputation with policyholders, producers, regulators, rating agencies, investors, and employees is important to our ability to compete. Our reputation could be harmed by many things:

- perceived or actual failures in claims handling, particularly following high-profile catastrophe events;
- underwriting actions such as non-renewals or market withdrawals that attract public or political attention;
- regulatory findings or litigation;
- cybersecurity incidents or data privacy failures;
- allegations of unfair discrimination in our use of data and models;
- misconduct by employees, producers, or vendors; and
- our positions on public policy or environmental, social, and governance matters.

Negative publicity can spread rapidly through social media and other channels, whether or not it is accurate. Damage to our reputation could reduce our ability to attract and retain customers, producers, and employees, invite regulatory scrutiny, and adversely affect the market price of our securities.

***Our risk management policies and procedures may not be effective in identifying or mitigating all of the risks to which we are exposed.***

We maintain an enterprise risk management framework that includes a Board-approved risk appetite statement, a risk taxonomy, an economic capital model, stress testing and scenario analysis, and a three-lines-of-defense governance model. Our risk management methods are based in part on historical experience and on models and assumptions that may not accurately predict future exposures, which could be significantly greater than historical measures indicate. Some risks are not adequately captured by our models or are not identified at all. These include emerging risks, correlated risks that materialize simultaneously, and risks arising from the interaction of factors we have analyzed separately. Our risk management processes depend on the accuracy and completeness of information, on the judgment of our personnel, and on effective controls, any of which may fail. If our risk management framework is not effective, we could suffer unexpected losses that materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

***Changes in the insurance needs of our customers and in societal and demographic trends could reduce demand for our products.***

Demand for property and casualty insurance depends on factors such as economic growth, business formation, vehicle ownership, home ownership, construction activity, and the legal and regulatory requirements that make certain coverages mandatory or customary. Several developments could reduce the size of the markets in which we operate or shift demand toward products and channels in which we are less competitive. Examples include:

- changes in work patterns;
- urbanization or migration away from the regions where our business is concentrated;
- changes in vehicle ownership models, such as ride-sharing and subscription services;
- the growth of alternative risk transfer mechanisms such as captives, self-insurance, and parametric products; and
- generational shifts in how consumers prefer to research and purchase insurance.

Our failure to anticipate and adapt to such changes could adversely affect our growth and profitability.
