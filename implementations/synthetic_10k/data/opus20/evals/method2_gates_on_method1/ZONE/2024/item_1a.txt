Item 1A. Risk Factors

Our business, financial condition, results of operations and cash flows are subject to a range of risks and uncertainties. Investors should consider the risks described below together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including our consolidated financial statements and the related notes and Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations. These risks are not the only ones we face. Additional risks that we do not currently know about, or that we now consider immaterial, could also harm us. If any of these risks occurs, the trading price of our common stock could decline.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

We face intense competition, and many of our competitors have substantially greater resources than we do.

The markets for neurovascular devices, cardiac rhythm management products, ambulatory cardiac monitoring and clinical imaging software are highly competitive and are characterized by rapid technological change. In each of our segments we compete with large, diversified medical technology companies. Many of these companies have broader product portfolios, larger sales forces, greater financial resources, established relationships with hospital systems and group purchasing organizations, and greater experience obtaining regulatory approvals and reimbursement. Several of them can bundle products across multiple therapeutic areas, offer volume-based discounts or absorb pricing concessions in ways that we may not be able to match. We also compete with smaller, venture-backed companies that may introduce disruptive technologies, particularly in aspiration thrombectomy, leadless pacing, patch-based monitoring and artificial intelligence-enabled image analysis.

Competitors may develop products that are safer, more effective, easier to use or less expensive than ours, or that obtain regulatory approval or favorable clinical data before ours do. In cardiac rhythm management in particular, physician loyalty to an installed base of programmers and remote monitoring systems can make it difficult to win new accounts. If we fail to compete effectively, we may lose market share, experience pricing pressure or be required to increase our commercial spending, any of which could adversely affect our revenue and margins.

Our growth depends on our substantial investment in research and development producing commercially successful products, and that investment may not generate an adequate return.

Research and development represents our largest operating expense and consumes a significant portion of our revenue each year, a level that is high relative to many companies in the medical technology industry. We maintain this level of investment because our strategy depends on serial innovation across our neurovascular, cardiac rhythm and digital health platforms and on longer-horizon programs aimed at new therapeutic categories. Product development is inherently uncertain. Programs may fail to achieve their technical objectives, may require more time or money than anticipated, may not produce clinical results sufficient to support regulatory approval or physician adoption, or may be overtaken by competing technologies before launch.

Because a large share of our cost base is committed to research and development, our operating results are sensitive to the productivity of that spending. If revenue growth slows, we may face a difficult choice between reducing development investment, which could impair our long-term competitive position, and maintaining it, which would pressure our operating margins and cash flows. Delays or failures in significant programs, such as our next-generation aspiration technology, devices for distal and medium vessel occlusions, or expanded indications for leadless pacing, could have a material adverse effect on our future growth.

Changes in coverage and reimbursement by third-party payors could reduce demand for our products and the prices we can obtain.

Our customers generally rely on Medicare, Medicaid, other government programs, national health systems and private insurers to pay for the procedures in which our products are used and for the remote monitoring and software services we provide. Payors continue to pursue cost containment through lower payment rates, bundled or episode-based payments, prior authorization requirements, coverage restrictions and value-based purchasing arrangements. Changes to hospital inpatient and outpatient payment systems, physician fee schedules or payment for remote physiologic and cardiac monitoring could reduce the attractiveness of our products to hospitals and physicians. Reimbursement for software-based triage and analytics remains limited and evolving, and the commercial prospects of our Imaging and Digital Health segment depend in part on whether hospitals perceive sufficient clinical and economic value to fund subscriptions in the absence of specific reimbursement.

Outside the United States, reimbursement and pricing are frequently controlled by government authorities that may require health technology assessments, impose reference pricing or award contracts through competitive tenders. Unfavorable coverage or reimbursement decisions in any of our significant markets could reduce procedure volumes, delay adoption of new products and adversely affect our results of operations.

Consolidation among health care providers and the purchasing power of group purchasing organizations exert continuing pressure on our prices.

A significant share of our United States sales is made under agreements with a limited number of group purchasing organizations and integrated delivery networks. These organizations negotiate aggressively on price, frequently award contracts to a limited number of suppliers in a product category and may favor competitors with broader portfolios. Continued consolidation among hospitals and health systems is concentrating purchasing decisions further and strengthening the negotiating position of our customers. Hospital value analysis committees increasingly require clinical and economic evidence before approving new technologies, which can lengthen sales cycles. The loss of, or adverse changes to, any significant purchasing agreement, or a failure to be included on new agreements, could materially reduce our revenue, and pricing concessions required to retain business could reduce our gross margin.

Clinical research and evolving treatment guidelines could reduce the use of our products.

Demand for our neurovascular products has benefited from clinical studies that established mechanical thrombectomy as a standard of care for many patients with large vessel occlusion stroke and that extended treatment windows. Demand for our cardiac rhythm and monitoring products depends similarly on clinical evidence supporting device therapy and long-term monitoring. Future studies, including studies sponsored by competitors or independent investigators, could produce unfavorable or ambiguous results for our products or for the procedures in which they are used, could show that competing therapies such as pharmacological treatments or alternative device approaches are equally or more effective, or could identify previously unrecognized safety risks. Professional society guidelines could change in ways that reduce the number of patients treated with our devices. Any of these developments could reduce physician adoption, impair reimbursement and adversely affect our sales.

Our Imaging and Digital Health segment and our broader connected-care strategy may not succeed.

Our strategy includes integrating our devices with software that collects, interprets and communicates clinical information and building recurring subscription revenue. Imaging and Digital Health is our newest and smallest segment. It faces competition from established imaging vendors, electronic health record providers and numerous emerging software companies. Hospital technology budgets are constrained, procurement and information security reviews can be lengthy, and integrating with diverse hospital information systems requires significant ongoing investment. Customers on multi-year subscriptions may decline to renew or may renegotiate pricing. If we cannot demonstrate clinical and economic value, keep our algorithms current, or achieve sufficient scale, the segment may not reach profitability and we may not realize the strategic benefits we expect for our device businesses.

Adverse economic conditions, inflation and constraints on hospital budgets could reduce demand for our products and increase our costs.

Hospital procedure volumes, capital budgets and purchasing behavior are affected by general economic conditions, labor shortages among clinical staff, changes in patient insurance coverage and the financial health of health systems. Economic downturns, high unemployment or loss of insurance coverage could cause patients to defer elective procedures and follow-up care, and financially stressed hospitals may delay technology purchases or seek further price concessions. At the same time, inflation in wages, components, raw materials, freight and energy has increased, and may continue to increase, our operating costs. Our ability to pass these increases on to customers is limited by long-term contracts, group purchasing arrangements and competitive dynamics, which could compress our margins.

Our international operations expose us to political, economic, trade and currency risks.

We sell our products in numerous countries through direct sales organizations and independent distributors, and we source certain materials and components from suppliers outside the United States. Our international business is subject to risks that include fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates, changes in tariffs and trade policy, export controls and sanctions, political instability and armed conflict, differing and changing regulatory and reimbursement requirements, longer payment cycles, difficulty enforcing contracts and intellectual property rights, and the challenges of staffing and managing foreign operations. In certain markets we depend on a small number of independent distributors whose performance, financial condition and compliance practices we do not control. The termination of a distributor relationship or a transition from a distributor model to a direct model could disrupt sales in the affected territory.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Disruptions in our manufacturing operations or failures in our quality systems could impair our ability to supply products.

We manufacture most of our devices in a limited number of facilities, and many products are produced at a single site. Our catheters, implantable devices and monitors require precise processes, specialized equipment, controlled environments and highly trained personnel. Equipment failures, contamination, natural disasters, extreme weather, labor disruptions, cyberattacks or regulatory actions affecting a facility could interrupt production for an extended period. Qualifying a replacement facility or manufacturing line is time-consuming and generally requires regulatory notification or approval. In addition, our facilities and processes must comply with the quality system regulations of the United States Food and Drug Administration ("FDA") and comparable foreign requirements. A failure to maintain compliance could result in warning letters, import holds, production stoppages or delays in approval of new products. Supply interruptions could cause us to lose customers to competitors, particularly in procedural markets where hospitals expect reliable access to devices.

We depend on a limited number of suppliers, including single-source suppliers, for certain components, materials and services.

We obtain certain critical components, including batteries, specialized electronic components, polymers, nitinol and other metals, as well as sterilization services, from a limited number of suppliers, and in some cases from a single supplier. Several of these suppliers serve the broader electronics and medical technology industries and may give priority to larger customers during periods of constrained supply. Changes in suppliers generally require qualification, validation and, in some cases, regulatory approval, which can take considerable time. In addition, regulatory and community scrutiny of ethylene oxide sterilization has led to facility closures and capacity constraints across the industry, and further restrictions could limit our access to sterilization capacity or increase its cost. A supply disruption, quality problem at a supplier or significant price increase could delay shipments, increase our cost of revenue and adversely affect our results of operations.

Cybersecurity incidents involving our systems, our connected devices or our software platforms could harm patients, our customers and our business.

Our business relies on information technology systems to manage manufacturing, supply chain, financial reporting and customer relationships. Our cardiac rhythm products communicate with programmers and remote monitoring infrastructure, and our Sentinel platform processes medical images and patient information from hospital systems and transmits alerts to clinicians' mobile and desktop devices. These systems and products are potential targets for ransomware, unauthorized access, malware and other attacks, including attacks by sophisticated and state-sponsored actors, and they are also vulnerable to employee error, vendor compromise and software defects. A successful attack could compromise patient safety, interrupt clinical workflows at our customers, expose protected health information, disrupt our operations or require costly remediation, product updates or field actions.

Security vulnerabilities in medical devices and health software attract heightened regulatory attention, and regulators increasingly require cybersecurity documentation as part of premarket submissions and post-market surveillance. A significant incident could result in regulatory enforcement, litigation, contractual liability to hospital customers, loss of customer confidence and harm to our reputation, and our insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

We may not successfully complete or integrate acquisitions or licensing transactions, and we could be required to record impairment charges.

Our strategy includes targeted acquisitions and technology licenses that complement our portfolio. Goodwill increased during fiscal 2024, and goodwill and other non-current assets together represent the majority of our total assets. Acquisitions involve risks, including the diversion of management attention, difficulty integrating products, technologies, personnel and quality systems, the loss of key employees of acquired businesses, assumed liabilities and compliance deficiencies, and the possibility that acquired technologies fail to achieve expected clinical, regulatory or commercial milestones. If the performance of an acquired business or technology falls short of our expectations, or if market conditions, discount rates or our market capitalization change adversely, we may be required to record impairment charges for goodwill, intangible assets or other long-lived assets. Impairment charges could be material to our results of operations in the period recognized and would reduce our stockholders' equity.

Our relatively lean commercial and administrative structure may not keep pace with the growth and complexity of our business.

We seek to maintain an efficient commercial and administrative organization, and our selling, general and administrative expenses are low relative to revenue compared with many medical technology companies, although they increased in fiscal 2024. As we expand our product portfolio, enter new markets, grow our subscription software business and respond to increasing regulatory, cybersecurity and reporting requirements, we may need to invest significantly more in sales personnel, clinical specialists, compliance functions and administrative infrastructure. Continued increases in these costs could reduce our operating margins. Conversely, if we underinvest, we may be unable to support physicians in the procedure room, respond to competitive selling efforts, maintain effective internal controls or manage compliance risks adequately.

We depend on attracting and retaining highly skilled personnel and on our relationships with physicians.

Our success depends on our ability to attract, develop and retain engineers, software developers, data scientists, clinical and regulatory specialists, experienced sales personnel and senior management. We have approximately 2,700 employees, and competition for qualified talent in medical technology and software is intense, particularly in the locations where we operate. The loss of key personnel, or a failure to recruit successors, could delay product development and impair commercial execution. We also depend on collaborative relationships with physicians and academic institutions for product development, clinical research and training. These relationships are subject to extensive regulation, and limitations on them, or a loss of physician support, could slow our innovation and the adoption of our products.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

We are subject to extensive regulation, and we may be unable to obtain or maintain the clearances and approvals required to market our products.

Our products are regulated as medical devices by the FDA and by comparable authorities in other countries. Many of our products, including implantable cardiac devices and certain neurovascular devices, are subject to the premarket approval process, which typically requires extensive clinical data and can take several years. Other products require 510(k) clearance or de novo classification. Regulators may disagree with our trial designs or interpretation of data, request additional studies, impose restrictive labeling or decline to grant approval. In the European Union, the Medical Device Regulation has imposed more rigorous clinical evidence, notified body review and post-market surveillance requirements, and limited notified body capacity has lengthened certification timelines. Changes in regulatory policy, staffing or priorities at the FDA or foreign agencies could delay reviews of our submissions. Delays in obtaining or failures to obtain clearances and approvals for new products or indications, or for modifications to existing products, could adversely affect our competitive position and growth.

Product defects, recalls and other post-market actions could harm our reputation and results.

Our devices are used in complex, life-critical procedures and are implanted in or worn by patients for extended periods. Design or manufacturing defects, component failures, software errors, labeling deficiencies or misuse could cause patient injury or death. We are required to report certain adverse events and malfunctions to regulators and may initiate or be required to conduct recalls, corrections, software updates or other field actions. Regulators may also impose restrictions on marketing or require additional post-approval studies. Field actions involving implantable devices may require patient notification, additional monitoring or replacement procedures, and these can be particularly costly and damaging to physician confidence. A significant recall or safety concern could result in substantial costs, loss of revenue and market share, regulatory scrutiny and litigation.

We are exposed to product liability claims, and our insurance may not be adequate.

The design, manufacture and marketing of medical devices expose us to product liability, warranty and related claims, including individual lawsuits, mass tort proceedings and class actions. Claims involving implantable devices or devices used in the treatment of stroke may allege serious injury or death and may seek substantial damages. Product liability litigation is costly and diverts management attention, regardless of its merit. We maintain insurance coverage, but it is subject to deductibles, exclusions and limits, may not cover all claims and may become more expensive or unavailable on acceptable terms. Large judgments or settlements in excess of our coverage could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition.

We are subject to health care fraud and abuse, transparency and anti-corruption laws, and violations could result in significant penalties.

Our relationships with physicians, hospitals, distributors and other customers are subject to laws that include the federal Anti-Kickback Statute, the False Claims Act, the Physician Payments Sunshine Act, state anti-kickback and transparency laws, the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and comparable laws in other countries. These laws restrict, among other things, consulting arrangements, research grants, educational support, discounts, rebates and the provision of reimbursement support. Many of these laws are broad and are interpreted differently over time, and whistleblowers may bring actions on behalf of the government. Our work with physicians on product development and training, our reimbursement support activities and our use of independent distributors outside the United States carry heightened risk. Alleged or actual violations could result in criminal and civil penalties, exclusion from government health care programs, corporate integrity agreements and reputational harm.

Our handling of patient information subjects us to privacy and data protection laws.

Our remote monitoring services and Sentinel platform collect, store and transmit protected health information and other personal data. We are subject to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and its implementing regulations, state consumer health privacy and data breach laws, the European Union General Data Protection Regulation and similar laws in other jurisdictions. These laws are complex, are becoming more stringent and can conflict with one another, particularly with respect to cross-border data transfers. Compliance requires ongoing investment, and violations or data breaches could result in fines, litigation, contractual claims by hospital customers and loss of trust in our connected-care offerings.

Evolving regulation of software and artificial intelligence could increase our costs and limit our offerings.

Our imaging analytics and arrhythmia detection algorithms rely on machine learning and other advanced software techniques. Regulators in the United States and abroad are developing new frameworks for software as a medical device and for artificial intelligence, including requirements related to algorithm changes, transparency, bias, validation and post-market performance monitoring. New legislation or guidance could require additional clinical validation, limit our ability to update algorithms efficiently, impose new documentation or disclosure obligations, or expose us to liability if algorithmic outputs contribute to adverse patient outcomes. These developments could slow product iteration in our Imaging and Digital Health segment and increase our development and compliance costs.

We may be unable to protect our intellectual property, and we may be subject to claims that we infringe the rights of others.

Our competitive position depends on our patents, trade secrets, trademarks and proprietary know-how relating to our catheter designs, implantable device technologies and software. Our patents may be challenged, invalidated, narrowed or circumvented, and patent applications may not result in issued patents of meaningful scope. Protection may be weaker in some foreign jurisdictions. The medical device industry is characterized by extensive patent litigation, and competitors and non-practicing entities may assert that our products infringe their rights. Intellectual property litigation is expensive and unpredictable. An adverse outcome could require us to pay substantial damages or royalties, redesign products or stop selling them, and could also allow competitors to use technologies we developed.

Changes in tax laws or challenges to our tax positions could adversely affect our results.

Our effective tax rate increased in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023, and it may continue to fluctuate. Our tax expense is affected by the mix of earnings across jurisdictions, the availability of research and development credits, the tax treatment of research and experimental expenditures, changes in valuation allowances and the outcome of tax audits. Because our business is research-intensive, changes to the tax treatment of research expenditures and credits affect us more than they would a less research-intensive company. The implementation of global minimum tax rules, changes in United States federal and state tax laws, and changes in the interpretation of existing laws by tax authorities could increase our tax expense and cash tax payments and reduce our net income.

Health care reform and changes in government health policy could adversely affect our business.

Federal and state governments in the United States and governments in other countries continue to propose and enact measures intended to reduce health care costs and alter the delivery and financing of health care. These measures include changes to payment models, expanded price transparency obligations, changes to government program eligibility and funding, and increased scrutiny of medical device pricing. Changes to insurance coverage that reduce the number of insured patients, or reductions in government funding for hospitals, could reduce procedure volumes. We cannot predict what measures will be adopted or how they will affect us, but they could reduce demand for our products, increase pricing pressure or raise our compliance costs.

Risks Related to Our Indebtedness and Capital Structure

Our substantial indebtedness could limit our flexibility and adversely affect our financial condition.

We have a significant amount of long-term debt, which increased during fiscal 2024 and exceeds our total stockholders' equity. Our cash and cash equivalents are modest relative to our outstanding indebtedness, and we rely on cash generated from operations and on access to credit markets to meet our obligations. Our level of indebtedness could require us to dedicate a meaningful portion of our operating cash flow to interest and principal payments, reduce the funds available for research and development, capital expenditures, acquisitions and returns to stockholders, increase our vulnerability to adverse economic and industry conditions, and place us at a disadvantage relative to competitors with stronger balance sheets. If our operating cash flow declines, whether because of competitive pressure, a product recall, reimbursement changes or other factors, we may have difficulty servicing or refinancing our debt on acceptable terms.

Rising interest rates and changes in credit markets could increase our borrowing costs and restrict our access to capital.

Interest expense increased in fiscal 2024. To the extent our borrowings bear variable interest rates, or we refinance maturing fixed-rate debt at higher rates, our interest expense will increase. Volatility in credit markets, a downgrade of any credit ratings assigned to us, or a deterioration in our operating performance could make it more difficult or expensive to obtain financing when we need it, including to refinance maturities or fund acquisitions. Because our cash balances are limited relative to our debt obligations, constrained access to credit could require us to reduce investment or curtail capital returns.

The agreements governing our indebtedness contain covenants that restrict our activities.

Our debt agreements contain financial maintenance covenants and other restrictions on our ability to incur additional debt, grant liens, make certain investments, sell assets, engage in mergers and make certain restricted payments. These covenants could limit our ability to respond to changing business conditions, pursue acquisitions or invest in growth opportunities. A failure to comply with them could result in an event of default. That could permit lenders to accelerate repayment of outstanding amounts, and we might not have sufficient liquidity to repay them.

We may not continue to pay dividends or repurchase shares at current levels, or at all.

We return capital to stockholders through quarterly dividends and share repurchases. In fiscal 2024, cash returned to stockholders through dividends and net share repurchases, together with capital expenditures and other investing activities, exceeded our cash provided by operating activities, and we funded the difference in part with additional borrowings. That approach may not be sustainable if operating cash flow does not grow or if borrowing costs rise. Future dividends and repurchases are at the discretion of our Board of Directors and depend on our earnings, cash flows, financial condition, debt covenants, capital requirements and other factors. A reduction or suspension of our dividend or repurchase program could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

General Risks

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to factors that include variations in our quarterly results, announcements of clinical trial results or regulatory decisions affecting us or our competitors, product recalls, changes in reimbursement policy, changes in financial estimates by securities analysts, our failure to meet guidance or market expectations, acquisitions, litigation developments and general market and economic conditions. Broad declines in the market valuations of medical technology companies may affect our stock price regardless of our operating performance. In the past, securities class action litigation has often followed periods of volatility in a company's stock price.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could delay or prevent a change in control.

We are incorporated in Delaware. Provisions of the Delaware General Corporation Law and of our certificate of incorporation and bylaws could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us, even if a change in control would benefit our stockholders. Such provisions include the ability of our Board of Directors to issue preferred stock without stockholder approval, advance notice requirements for stockholder proposals and nominations, and limitations on business combinations with interested stockholders. These provisions could discourage takeover attempts and could reduce the price that investors are willing to pay for our common stock.

Natural disasters, climate change and other events beyond our control could disrupt our operations.

Our facilities, suppliers, distribution channels and customers could be affected by earthquakes, wildfires, severe storms, flooding, extreme temperatures, pandemics, power outages, terrorism or other catastrophic events. Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of some of these events and may result in new regulations that raise our energy, materials and compliance costs. Hospitals affected by such events may reduce or postpone procedures. We also face evolving expectations and disclosure requirements from regulators, investors and customers regarding environmental, social and governance matters, and our failure to meet them could harm our reputation and increase our costs.

If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, the accuracy and timeliness of our financial reporting could be impaired.

We are required to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting and disclosure controls and procedures. The growth of our subscription software business, the integration of acquired businesses, changes in our information technology systems and the complexity of accounting for revenue arrangements that combine devices, services and software increase the demands on our control environment. If we identify material weaknesses, or fail to remediate them in a timely manner, we could fail to report our financial results accurately or on time. That could cause investors to lose confidence in our reported results, subject us to regulatory scrutiny and adversely affect the price of our common stock.
