Item 1A. Risk Factors

Investing in our securities involves risk. The risks described below could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows and the trading price of our common stock. Read them together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 7, Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations, and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8. Additional risks that we do not currently know of, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also impair our business. The order in which these risks appear does not indicate their relative likelihood or significance.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Our results depend heavily on construction activity in the local and regional markets we serve, and a decline in that activity could reduce demand for our products.

Our Construction Materials segment, our largest segment by revenue, sells aggregates, ready-mixed concrete, asphalt and concrete products. Demand for these products depends on public infrastructure, private non-residential and residential construction in each market where we operate. These sectors respond to interest rates, credit availability, population and employment trends, business investment decisions, housing affordability and general economic conditions. Our materials cannot be transported economically over long distances, so we cannot readily move volume from a weak market to a strong one. A downturn concentrated in one or more of our principal metropolitan or regional markets could therefore reduce our shipments, pricing and plant utilization even if national construction activity remains stable.

Non-residential construction activity has recently been supported by projects such as warehouses, data centers, manufacturing facilities and energy installations. A slowdown in any of these project categories, or the cancellation or deferral of large projects in markets where we have concentrated operations, could reduce demand faster than we can adjust our cost structure. Weakness in residential construction would also reduce demand for aggregates and downstream products used in site development, foundations and paving.

Reductions or delays in governmental funding for highways, bridges and other public infrastructure could adversely affect our sales.

A substantial part of our construction-related demand comes from projects funded by federal surface transportation programs and by state and local transportation budgets. These programs depend on legislative authorization, annual appropriations, fuel tax and other dedicated revenue sources, and the fiscal condition of state and local governments. Federal programs may not be reauthorized when current authorizations expire, or may be reauthorized at lower funding levels or with different priorities. Delays in appropriations, budget shortfalls, competing spending priorities or changes in political leadership could defer or cancel projects in our markets. Public agencies may also face higher construction costs that reduce the amount of work their budgets support. Any of these developments could reduce volumes in our Construction Materials segment and the related transportation volumes handled by our Logistics and Services segment.

Our Industrial Minerals segment depends on the operating rates of cyclical industrial customers, and structural changes in those industries could permanently reduce demand.

Our lime, limestone and specialty mineral products are sold mainly to steel producers, electric utilities, chemical manufacturers, glass and ceramics producers, pulp and paper mills, and other industrial users. Many of these industries are cyclical. Reduced operating rates at customer facilities directly reduce the volumes they purchase under our supply agreements, many of which adjust with customer requirements. In addition, some demand rests on technologies and fuel sources that are changing. Retirements of coal-fired generating units reduce demand for lime and limestone used in flue gas desulfurization. Shifts in steelmaking technology, the closure or relocation of customer plants, and customers' substitution of alternative materials could permanently reduce demand for our products in particular regions. Because kilns and processing plants are capital intensive and located near specific deposits or customers, we may not be able to redeploy idled capacity, and we could be required to record impairment charges or incur closure costs.

We face significant competition, and pricing pressure could further compress our already narrow margins.

We compete with national, regional and local producers of aggregates, construction materials, lime and industrial minerals, as well as with transportation and terminal operators. In many local markets, competition depends on proximity to customers, product quality, service, reliability of supply and price. New entrants, the expansion of permitted reserves by competitors, imported materials delivered by water, and recycled or substitute materials could increase competitive pressure. Public construction work is generally awarded through competitive bidding, which can limit our ability to pass through cost increases on fixed-price contracts. Our gross margin is narrow relative to our revenue, so even modest declines in pricing or modest increases in costs that we cannot recover could have a disproportionate effect on our operating results.

Weather conditions and seasonality cause our results to fluctuate and can reduce our profitability in any given period.

Construction and many industrial activities slow during periods of cold, snow, heavy rainfall, flooding or extreme heat, and our sales volumes are typically lower in the winter months in many of our markets. Prolonged adverse weather during the peak construction season can defer or cancel shipments and projects. Because a large portion of our production costs, including depreciation, labor and maintenance, is fixed or semi-fixed, lower volumes reduce our margins. Drought conditions can restrict the water available for washing aggregates and for certain processing operations. Low water levels on inland waterways can limit barge loading capacity. Results for any quarter are therefore not necessarily indicative of results for a full year.

Our acquisition strategy exposes us to integration, valuation and execution risks.

Acquisitions are an important part of our strategy, and during fiscal 2024 we completed acquisitions that broadened our presence in several regional markets. Acquisitions involve risks that could prevent us from realizing the anticipated benefits, including:

difficulties integrating operations, safety programs, information systems, internal controls and personnel; the diversion of management attention from existing operations; the loss of key employees or customers of acquired businesses; reserve quantities, permit status or asset condition that differ from our expectations; environmental, reclamation, tax or other liabilities that were not identified during due diligence or are not covered by indemnities; and conditions imposed by antitrust authorities, including required divestitures.

We have funded recent acquisitions with a combination of cash on hand, borrowings and equity issuances. Future acquisitions may require us to incur additional debt or issue additional shares. If acquired businesses do not perform as expected, we may not generate returns sufficient to justify the price paid or to service the related financing, and we could be required to record impairment charges against the goodwill and other assets we recorded.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Increases in the cost of energy, fuel and other inputs could reduce our profitability, particularly given our narrow margins.

Our operations consume large quantities of diesel fuel, electricity, natural gas, coal, petroleum coke, explosives, liquid asphalt, cement, steel, tires and replacement parts. Lime kilns in particular are energy intensive, and fuel represents a major component of lime production cost. Transportation by truck, rail and barge also depends on fuel prices. Prices for these inputs can be volatile and are affected by global commodity markets, geopolitical events, supply disruptions, regulatory changes and inflation. Many of our industrial supply contracts include price adjustment provisions linked to energy and other input costs, but these provisions may operate with a lag, may not cover all cost categories and may not fully offset cost increases. In our construction businesses, fixed-price bids and contracts limit our ability to recover cost increases during the term of a project. Our cost of revenue is high relative to our revenue, so unrecovered input cost inflation could quickly erode our gross profit.

Our business depends on our ability to maintain and replenish permitted mineral reserves, and our reserve estimates may prove inaccurate.

Our long-term viability depends on access to economically recoverable reserves of suitable quality at locations near our markets. Obtaining zoning approvals and permits for new quarries, mines and expansions has become increasingly difficult, time consuming and costly, especially near population centers. Local opposition, environmental review requirements, water management restrictions and land use changes may delay or prevent the development of new reserves or the expansion of existing sites. Some of our reserves are held under leases that may expire, may not be renewed on acceptable terms or may require increasing royalty payments.

Our reserve estimates rest on geological data, engineering assumptions and economic factors, including prices, costs and expected recovery rates. These estimates involve inherent uncertainty. Actual mineral quality, quantity, recoverability or economic viability may differ materially from our estimates. Downward revisions could shorten the useful lives of our properties, increase depletion expense, trigger impairment charges and require us to acquire replacement reserves sooner and at greater cost than planned.

We rely on third-party rail and marine carriers and on our own logistics assets, and disruptions to transportation could impair our ability to serve customers.

Our integrated logistics network, managed largely through our Logistics and Services segment, depends on third-party railroads and marine carriers to move products from our production sites to terminals and customers. Rail service disruptions, labor disputes at carriers, congestion, car shortages, rate increases, infrastructure failures, changes in carrier operating practices and adverse waterway conditions could delay shipments, increase our costs and cause us to lose sales. In many locations we rely on a single rail carrier, which limits our negotiating leverage. We also own or lease rail car fleets, terminals and port facilities that require ongoing maintenance and capital investment. Any of these assets may become underutilized if volumes decline. Shortages of qualified commercial truck drivers could limit our delivery capacity and increase our costs.

Unplanned outages, equipment failures and catastrophic events could interrupt our operations and adversely affect our results.

Our operations depend on the continued performance of crushing and screening plants, kilns, mills, hydrating plants, mobile mining equipment, terminals and related infrastructure. Mechanical failures, fires, explosions, slope or pit wall failures, flooding, dam or impoundment failures, power outages and similar events could halt production, damage assets and injure people. Hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, wildfires, earthquakes, pandemics, acts of terrorism or war, and other catastrophic events could disrupt our facilities, those of our customers and suppliers, and the transportation networks we use. Some of our products are produced at a limited number of facilities, and an extended outage at one of those facilities could prevent us from meeting contractual supply commitments. Our insurance may not cover all losses, may be subject to substantial deductibles and may become more expensive or unavailable on acceptable terms.

Accidents and health and safety incidents could result in injuries, fatalities, liabilities and reputational harm.

Mining, blasting, processing, heavy equipment operation, kiln operations and transportation are inherently hazardous. Despite our safety programs, serious injuries or fatalities may occur among our employees, contractors or members of the public. Such incidents could result in regulatory citations, fines, orders to close all or part of a site, litigation, higher insurance and workers' compensation costs, and damage to our reputation with customers, regulators, employees and communities. Integrating acquired operations into our safety standards takes time, and safety performance at newly acquired sites may initially fall short of our standards.

We depend on a large, skilled workforce, and labor shortages, rising labor costs or work stoppages could disrupt our operations.

We employ about 14,000 people, many in skilled trades such as equipment operation, maintenance, electrical work and kiln operation. Competition for skilled labor in many of our markets is intense, and an aging workforce in the mining and construction industries may make qualified personnel harder to recruit and retain. Wage inflation and rising benefit costs increase our operating expenses. A portion of our workforce is covered by collective bargaining agreements. We may be unable to renegotiate those agreements on acceptable terms, and strikes, lockouts or other work stoppages could disrupt production. Union organizing activity at non-union locations could also increase our costs or reduce our operating flexibility.

Cybersecurity incidents and failures of our information and operational technology systems could disrupt our business and expose us to liability.

We rely on information technology systems to manage production, dispatch, logistics, billing, payroll, procurement and financial reporting. We also rely on operational technology to control kilns, plants and terminals. Our increasing use of automation, remote monitoring and connected equipment expands our exposure. These systems, and those of our vendors and service providers, are subject to cyberattacks, ransomware, malware, social engineering, employee error, and hardware or software failures. A significant incident could interrupt operations, compromise sensitive employee, customer or company information, result in regulatory penalties and litigation, and require significant expenditure to remediate. Integrating the systems of acquired businesses can create additional vulnerabilities during transition periods.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

We have incurred net losses in each of the last two fiscal years and may not achieve or sustain profitability.

We reported an operating loss and a net loss in both fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023. Although our revenue grew and our operating loss narrowed in fiscal 2024, our gross profit is not sufficient to cover our operating expenses and interest expense. Our ability to return to profitability depends on achieving higher pricing and volumes, realizing efficiency gains from our operating initiatives, successfully integrating acquired businesses and reducing our financing costs. We may not achieve these objectives within the time frame we expect, or at all. Continued losses would reduce our stockholders' equity and could limit our access to financing. They could also cause rating agencies, lenders and investors to view our credit profile less favorably, which could in turn adversely affect the trading price of our common stock.

Our substantial indebtedness could adversely affect our financial flexibility and our ability to operate our business.

We have a significant amount of long-term debt, which increased during fiscal 2024 primarily to fund acquisitions. Our interest expense also increased and exceeds our operating results. Our level of indebtedness could:

require us to dedicate a substantial portion of our operating cash flow to interest and principal payments, reducing funds available for capital expenditures, acquisitions and other purposes; increase our vulnerability to economic downturns, construction cycles and adverse industry conditions; limit our ability to obtain additional financing on acceptable terms; place us at a disadvantage relative to competitors that are less leveraged; and limit our flexibility in planning for, or reacting to, changes in our business and markets.

Our ability to make scheduled payments on and to refinance our indebtedness depends on our future performance, which is subject to economic, financial, competitive and other factors beyond our control. If we cannot generate sufficient cash flow or refinance maturing debt on acceptable terms, we may be required to sell assets, reduce or delay capital investment, or seek additional equity capital. We may not be able to take any of these actions on favorable terms, or at all.

Our operating cash flow has not been sufficient to fund our investing activities, and we depend on access to capital markets.

In fiscal 2024, net cash provided by operating activities covered only a portion of our capital expenditures and acquisition spending. We funded the balance through additional borrowings and the issuance of equity. Our business requires substantial ongoing capital investment to maintain plants and mobile equipment, develop reserves, comply with environmental requirements and pursue our efficiency initiatives. If our operating cash flow does not improve and we cannot access debt or equity markets on acceptable terms, we may have to defer maintenance or growth investments. Deferred investment could impair the reliability, safety and competitiveness of our operations. Volatility in credit and equity markets, rising interest rates or a deterioration in our financial results could restrict our access to capital when we need it.

Changes in interest rates and in our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs.

A portion of our indebtedness bears interest at variable rates, and we will need to refinance fixed-rate debt as it matures. Higher interest rates would increase our interest expense and reduce the cash available for other purposes. Rating agencies evaluate us on measures such as leverage, interest coverage and cash flow generation. Our net losses and increased debt levels could lead to a downgrade of our credit ratings, which could increase our borrowing costs, reduce the availability of financing, and require us to provide letters of credit or other collateral to counterparties, sureties and regulators.

The agreements governing our indebtedness contain restrictive covenants that could limit our operating flexibility.

Our credit facility and debt instruments contain covenants that restrict our ability to incur additional debt, grant liens, sell assets, make certain investments and acquisitions, and engage in mergers. Some of these instruments require us to maintain specified financial ratios. Our ability to comply with these covenants may be affected by events beyond our control, including weaker operating results or higher interest rates. A breach could result in an event of default. A default could permit lenders to accelerate repayment of outstanding amounts, terminate commitments and, through cross-default provisions, trigger defaults under other instruments.

We have a significant amount of goodwill, and an impairment of goodwill or other long-lived assets could result in material charges to our results of operations.

Goodwill represents a large portion of our total assets and increased during fiscal 2024 as a result of acquisitions. We also carry substantial property, plant and equipment, including mineral reserves and processing facilities. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually and test other long-lived assets when events or circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. Sustained operating losses, lower than expected performance of acquired businesses, declines in demand in particular markets, higher discount rates, reductions in reserve estimates, plant closures and declines in our market capitalization could require us to record impairment charges. Any such charge would be non-cash, but it could materially reduce our reported results and stockholders' equity and could affect our compliance with financial covenants.

Future issuances of equity could dilute existing stockholders, and we do not currently pay dividends.

We have issued equity to help fund our growth, and we may issue additional shares of common stock or securities convertible into common stock to finance acquisitions, reduce indebtedness or fund our operations. Such issuances would dilute the ownership interests of existing stockholders and could adversely affect the market price of our common stock. We did not pay dividends in fiscal 2024. We currently intend to use available cash to fund our operations, invest in our business and manage our indebtedness. Our debt agreements may also restrict our ability to pay dividends. Investors therefore should not expect to receive a return on their investment through dividends in the foreseeable future.

Our ability to use tax attributes to offset future taxable income may be limited.

We have recorded no income tax benefit on our recent pre-tax losses. Our ability to realize the value of net operating loss carryforwards and other tax attributes depends on generating sufficient future taxable income. Ownership changes under applicable tax rules, including changes that could result from equity issuances or transactions in our stock, could limit our ability to use these attributes. Changes in tax law, including changes to the treatment of interest deductions, percentage depletion or capital cost recovery, could increase our future tax obligations.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

We are subject to extensive environmental laws and permitting requirements that could increase our costs, restrict our operations or expose us to liability.

Our operations are subject to federal, state and local laws and regulations governing air emissions, water discharges and use, wetlands, endangered species, waste management, blasting, noise, dust, land use and the remediation of contaminated sites. We must obtain and maintain numerous permits to operate our quarries, mines, kilns, plants and terminals. Permits may be subject to renewal, modification, challenge or revocation, and new requirements may be imposed as a condition of renewal. Violations could result in fines, penalties, injunctions and suspension of operations. We may also be liable for the investigation and cleanup of contamination at properties we currently or formerly owned or operated, or at third-party sites, regardless of fault. Environmental requirements tend to become more stringent over time, and compliance may require significant capital expenditures and higher operating costs.

We have significant reclamation and closure obligations, and the cost of meeting them may exceed our estimates.

We are required to reclaim land disturbed by our mining and processing activities and to close facilities in accordance with permit conditions and applicable law. We record liabilities for these obligations based on estimates of future costs, timing and discount rates, which require significant judgment. Changes in regulatory requirements, closure plans, site conditions or cost assumptions could increase these obligations. Regulators often require financial assurance in the form of surety bonds, letters of credit or other instruments. The cost and availability of such assurance could be adversely affected by our financial condition or credit ratings.

Climate change and laws regulating greenhouse gas emissions could increase our costs and reduce demand for some of our products.

Lime production releases carbon dioxide both from fuel combustion and from the chemical conversion of limestone into quicklime, and our mobile equipment and transportation activities consume significant quantities of fossil fuels. Existing and future laws, regulations, emissions reporting requirements, carbon pricing mechanisms and permitting conditions related to greenhouse gas emissions could increase our operating and capital costs. Such measures could also require us to invest in emissions reduction technologies that may not be commercially proven or economically viable. Climate-related policies affecting our customers, such as measures that accelerate the retirement of coal-fired power plants or change steelmaking processes, could reduce demand for our lime and limestone products. Customers, investors and lenders increasingly consider climate-related factors in their decisions, which could affect demand for our products and our access to capital. Physical effects of climate change, such as more frequent severe weather, flooding, drought and extreme heat, could disrupt our operations and transportation networks.

We are subject to mine safety and occupational health regulations that could increase our costs and expose us to penalties.

Our mining and many of our processing operations are subject to the Federal Mine Safety and Health Act of 1977 and related regulations, which impose detailed safety and health standards and provide for frequent inspections, citations and penalties. Regulators can issue orders requiring the withdrawal of personnel or the closure of all or part of an operation. Regulations limiting worker exposure to respirable crystalline silica and other airborne contaminants have become more stringent. They may require additional engineering controls, monitoring, medical surveillance and personal protective equipment. Our non-mining operations are subject to occupational safety and health requirements. Compliance with these requirements, and with any future changes to them, could increase our costs and limit our operations.

We are, and may in the future be, subject to litigation and claims that could result in significant costs.

In the ordinary course of business we are involved in legal proceedings involving contract disputes, personal injury, property damage, environmental matters, employment matters, product quality and regulatory compliance. Because some of our products contain crystalline silica, we may be subject to claims alleging injury from exposure to silica-containing products. Construction projects can give rise to claims regarding defective materials or delays. The outcome of litigation is inherently uncertain. Adverse judgments, settlements or defense costs could be material, and insurance may not cover all such losses.

Our participation in public construction projects subjects us to additional regulatory requirements.

Our construction-related businesses perform work under contracts with federal, state and local governmental agencies. These contracts are subject to specific procurement rules, prevailing wage and labor requirements, small business participation goals, audit rights and termination-for-convenience provisions. Violations of these requirements could result in fines, contract terminations, or suspension or debarment from future government work. Debarment in one jurisdiction could affect our eligibility in others. Changes in procurement rules, including domestic content requirements, could increase our compliance costs.

General Risks

Inflation and broader economic conditions could adversely affect our costs and our customers' demand.

General inflation increases our costs for labor, energy, equipment, parts and services. It may also reduce the volume of construction that public and private customers can fund within fixed budgets. Recessions, financial market disruptions, tariffs and trade restrictions affecting equipment or materials, and changes in government fiscal policy could reduce demand for our products, impair our customers' ability to pay amounts owed to us, and increase the risk of uncollectible receivables.

The loss of key members of management could disrupt our business.

Our success depends on the experience and leadership of our senior management and the managers of our regional operations, who have specialized knowledge of local markets, permitting processes, operations and customer relationships. The loss of key personnel, or our failure to plan effectively for succession, could impair our ability to execute our strategy, including the integration of acquired businesses and the implementation of our efficiency initiatives.

If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, investors could lose confidence in our financial statements.

We must maintain effective internal control over financial reporting and disclosure controls and procedures. Our acquisitions require us to extend our controls to businesses that may have used different systems and processes, and integration may take time. If we identify material weaknesses, or fail to remediate them promptly, we could be unable to report our financial results accurately or on time. Such a failure could subject us to regulatory scrutiny, result in a loss of investor confidence and adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to our operating results, changes in our indebtedness or credit ratings, acquisition announcements, equity issuances, construction and industrial market trends, changes in government infrastructure funding, analyst expectations and general market conditions. Many of these factors are beyond our control. Stockholders may be unable to sell their shares at or above the price at which they purchased them.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could discourage or delay a change in control.

We are incorporated in Delaware. Provisions of Delaware law and of our certificate of incorporation and bylaws could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us without the consent of our board of directors, even if a transaction might be beneficial to stockholders. These provisions include the ability of our board to issue preferred stock without stockholder approval, advance notice requirements for stockholder proposals and nominations, and restrictions on business combinations with interested stockholders. These provisions could limit the price that investors are willing to pay for our common stock.
