Item 1A. Risk Factors

The risks and uncertainties described below could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows and the trading price of our common stock. Readers should evaluate these risks together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8. The risks described below are not the only ones we face. Other risks that we do not currently consider material, or that are not presently known to us, could also harm us. Some of the risks discussed below have occurred in the past, and the fact that a risk has occurred does not mean that it will not occur again or that its effects will be no greater than before.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

We face intense competition across each of our markets, which could reduce our market share, pricing and profitability.

Each of our segments operates in a highly competitive environment. In wireless, we compete with other national facilities-based carriers, regional carriers, cable operators that offer mobile service through mobile virtual network arrangements, and resellers. These competitors frequently use aggressive device promotions, multi-line discounts, unlimited data plans, bundled streaming content and price guarantees to attract our customers. In fixed broadband, we compete with cable operators that have extensive hybrid fiber-coaxial networks, with other fiber builders, with fixed wireless access providers and with satellite-based broadband services, including low-earth-orbit constellations that are expanding coverage in rural and remote areas. In business and wholesale markets, we compete with other carriers, systems integrators, cloud and software providers, and large technology companies that are building their own network infrastructure.

Some of our competitors have lower cost structures, fewer legacy obligations, less regulatory burden or greater flexibility to price aggressively. Others may be acquired by, or form alliances with, companies that have significant financial and technological resources. To respond to competitive offers, we may need to increase promotional spending, lower prices, enhance service features or increase marketing expenditures, any of which could reduce our revenue, margins and cash flows. If we fail to respond effectively, we could experience higher customer churn and lose market share.

Our revenue decreased in fiscal 2024, and we may be unable to stabilize or grow revenue if declines in legacy services continue to outpace growth in newer offerings.

Our total revenue decreased in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023, reflecting continued declines in legacy wireline voice and data services, lower equipment revenue and competitive pricing pressure, which were only partially offset by growth in fiber broadband, fixed wireless access and wireless data. Revenue from traditional landline voice, copper-based broadband, private-line and circuit-based business services, and certain wholesale transport products has declined for many years. We expect these declines to continue and possibly accelerate as customers migrate to wireless, internet-based and software-defined alternatives.

Our strategy depends on growing fiber, fixed wireless access, premium wireless plans, and managed, security and edge computing services quickly enough to replace legacy revenue. These newer offerings may carry lower margins, require significant upfront investment, take longer to scale than we anticipate, or fail to gain market acceptance. If we cannot offset legacy declines, our revenue, operating income and cash flows could continue to decrease. Because many of our network, labor and facilities costs are fixed in the near term, revenue declines could reduce our margins disproportionately.

Our significant investments in network technology may not generate the returns we expect.

We invest heavily each year to expand and upgrade our networks, including deploying 5G spectrum, densifying our wireless network, extending our fiber footprint and building capacity for fixed wireless access. These investments are made over multi-year periods on the basis of assumptions about customer demand, adoption rates, pricing, competitive dynamics, construction costs and regulatory conditions. Many of these assumptions are outside our control and may prove inaccurate.

The returns on our 5G investments, in particular, depend on monetizing new capabilities, such as private wireless networks, mobile edge computing and network slicing, for which demand remains uncertain. Fiber deployment requires substantial capital before subscribers are connected, and returns depend on achieving adequate penetration in areas where competitors may also be building. If customer demand, pricing or penetration falls short of our expectations, or if construction and equipment costs rise, we may not recover our investments, and we could be required to record impairment charges. Rapid technological change could also render portions of our network obsolete before the end of their expected useful lives.

Our fixed wireless access business depends on available network capacity and could be constrained as data usage grows.

Our fixed wireless access service shares wireless network capacity with our mobile customers. Its continued growth depends on our ability to manage network traffic so that both fixed and mobile customers receive an acceptable experience. Data consumption per customer continues to increase, and fixed wireless customers typically use significantly more data than mobile customers. If demand exceeds the capacity of our network in particular areas, we may need to limit sales, manage traffic in ways that degrade service, or make additional spectrum and network investments sooner than planned. Any of these responses could slow growth in this business, increase churn or raise costs.

We depend on access to sufficient spectrum, and we may be unable to obtain additional spectrum on acceptable terms.

Our wireless business depends on our ability to secure and maintain licenses to use radio frequency spectrum. Spectrum is a limited resource, and the availability and cost of additional spectrum depend on government auctions, secondary market transactions and regulatory decisions that we do not control. The Federal Communications Commission (the "FCC") has limited authority to conduct spectrum auctions, and delays in renewing that authority or in identifying new spectrum for commercial use could constrain our ability to meet growing demand. Competitors with greater spectrum holdings in particular bands could gain a capacity or performance advantage.

Acquiring spectrum can require substantial expenditures, which may increase our indebtedness. Our spectrum licenses are also subject to renewal and to build-out and other conditions. A failure to satisfy those conditions, or the imposition of new conditions or restrictions on the use of spectrum, could result in the loss of licenses or reduce their value.

We depend on a limited number of suppliers for devices, network equipment and other critical inputs.

We purchase smartphones and other devices from a small number of manufacturers, and a limited number of suppliers provide our core radio access network, fiber, optical transport and switching equipment. Our ability to attract and retain wireless customers depends in part on offering popular devices at competitive prices, and device manufacturers have significant bargaining power over pricing, allocation and launch timing. A disruption in supply, a supplier's financial difficulty, a dispute with a key supplier, or a supplier's decision to favor competitors could impair our ability to sell devices, deploy our network or serve customers.

Global supply chains for semiconductors, network electronics, fiber-optic cable and other components have experienced shortages, delays and price increases in the past and may do so again. Trade restrictions, tariffs, export controls and geopolitical tensions could increase our costs or limit our access to equipment. Government restrictions on the use of equipment from certain foreign suppliers could also narrow the pool of vendors available to us and increase our costs.

Our Business segment is exposed to the decline of legacy products, lengthy sales cycles and risks specific to government customers.

Our Business segment continues to experience declines as enterprise and government customers replace legacy voice, private-line and circuit-based data services with internet-based, software-defined and cloud-delivered solutions, some of which are offered by companies that are not traditional carriers. Large enterprise contracts are often awarded through competitive bidding with significant pricing pressure at renewal, and sales cycles for complex managed and security solutions can be long. Implementing these solutions may require significant upfront costs, and we may fail to realize expected margins.

Government agencies purchase services from us under contracts that typically permit termination for convenience, are subject to audit and appropriations risk, and impose extensive compliance requirements. Changes in government spending priorities, budget delays, government shutdowns, or the loss of significant contract vehicles could reduce our revenue. Failure to comply with government contracting requirements could result in penalties, contract termination or suspension from future government work.

Our Wholesale and Network Services segment depends on a concentrated group of large customers.

Our Wholesale and Network Services segment derives a significant portion of its revenue from a relatively small number of large telecommunications carriers, cable operators, mobile virtual network operators and hyperscale cloud operators. Some of these customers are also our competitors, and some are building or acquiring their own networks, which could reduce their demand for our services. The loss of a significant wholesale customer, a reduction in the volume of services purchased, or unfavorable pricing at contract renewal could adversely affect the segment's results. Industry consolidation among our wholesale customers could also increase their bargaining power or lead them to move traffic to their own facilities.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Network failures, natural disasters and other disruptive events could interrupt our services and damage our reputation.

Our business depends on the continuous operation of our wireless and wireline networks, data centers, billing systems and other information technology infrastructure. These systems are vulnerable to damage or interruption from hurricanes, floods, wildfires, earthquakes, severe winter storms, power outages, equipment failures, software errors, fiber cuts, vandalism, terrorism and other events. Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of extreme weather events affecting our facilities, and some of our network assets are located in areas that are especially exposed to such events.

Service outages, including those caused by software updates or configuration errors, can affect large numbers of customers at once, including emergency services and public safety users. An extended or widespread outage could lead to customer churn, service credits, regulatory investigations, fines, litigation and harm to our reputation. Our insurance may not cover all losses resulting from such events, and the cost of restoring and hardening our networks could be substantial.

Cyberattacks and other security breaches could compromise our networks and customer data and expose us to significant liability.

As a provider of critical communications infrastructure that holds large volumes of sensitive personal and business information, we are a frequent target of cyberattacks by criminal groups, nation-state actors and others. These attacks include attempts to gain unauthorized access to our networks and systems, ransomware, denial-of-service attacks, account takeovers and fraudulent number-porting schemes, and social engineering directed at our employees, contractors and customers. Threat actors are increasingly using advanced tools, including artificial intelligence, to make attacks more sophisticated. We also rely on vendors and other third parties that have access to our systems or data and that may themselves be compromised.

Although we invest significantly in cybersecurity, we cannot prevent all incidents, and some may go undetected for extended periods. A significant breach could disrupt our services, result in the theft or exposure of customer information, and lead to regulatory investigations, fines, class action litigation, remediation costs and lasting reputational harm. The costs of investigating, responding to and defending against such incidents could be material and may not be fully covered by insurance.

We may not achieve the cost reductions we expect, and our cost-reduction efforts could disrupt our operations.

Our operating results depend in part on our ability to reduce costs to offset revenue declines in legacy businesses. We are pursuing savings through process automation, digital self-service, workforce reductions, real estate consolidation, retirement of legacy network elements and procurement initiatives. Selling, general and administrative expense increased as a share of revenue in fiscal 2024 because these costs did not decline as quickly as our revenue. Many of our network, facilities and labor costs are fixed or difficult to reduce in the short term.

Retiring copper networks and other legacy facilities requires regulatory approval in many cases, and migrating customers to new technologies can be slow and costly. Workforce reductions may result in severance and related charges, the loss of institutional knowledge, lower employee morale and operational disruptions. If we are unable to achieve planned cost reductions, or if achieving them harms service quality or customer experience, our margins and cash flows could suffer.

Our large workforce and labor relations could affect our costs and operational flexibility.

We employ a large workforce, a substantial number of whom are covered by collective bargaining agreements. These agreements govern wages, benefits, work rules and other matters and may limit our ability to restructure operations or reduce costs. Negotiations to renew these agreements could result in higher labor costs, and labor disputes, strikes or work stoppages could disrupt our network operations, installation and repair activities and customer service. In addition, competition for employees with skills in network engineering, software, cybersecurity and data science is intense, and we may be unable to attract or retain the talent needed to execute our strategy.

Legacy network infrastructure may expose us to environmental, health and safety liabilities.

Portions of our wireline network were constructed many decades ago and include materials and equipment that are subject to increasing environmental and public health scrutiny, such as aerial and buried cables, backup power systems, fuel storage facilities and treated utility poles. We may be subject to investigations, litigation, remediation obligations or regulatory requirements related to these legacy assets, and the costs of addressing them, including removing or replacing infrastructure, could be significant. Changes in environmental laws or in the way existing laws are interpreted or enforced could increase these costs.

We depend on third parties for distribution, content, network access and other services.

We rely on national retailers, independent dealers and other third parties to sell our products and services, and on third-party content providers for the entertainment subscriptions bundled with our plans. We also depend on other carriers for roaming, interconnection and last-mile access in areas outside our footprint, on tower companies for a substantial portion of our wireless cell sites, and on contractors for network construction and customer installation. Increases in the cost of these services, the termination of key agreements, or a third party's failure to perform could raise our costs, reduce sales or impair service quality.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

Our substantial indebtedness could limit our financial flexibility and adversely affect our results.

We have a substantial amount of long-term debt, and we incur significant interest expense each year. Although we reduced our long-term debt during fiscal 2024, our indebtedness remains large relative to our cash balances. This level of indebtedness requires us to dedicate a significant portion of our cash flows from operations to interest and principal payments, which reduces funds available for capital expenditures, spectrum acquisitions, share repurchases and other purposes. It could also make us more vulnerable to economic downturns and competitive pressures, and limit our ability to respond to changes in our industry.

Our debt instruments contain covenants that could restrict our activities. If we fail to comply with these covenants, our lenders or noteholders could accelerate repayment. We may also incur additional debt in the future, including to fund spectrum purchases or acquisitions, which would intensify these risks.

Changes in interest rates, credit market conditions or our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to capital.

We regularly access the debt capital markets to refinance maturing obligations and to fund our operations and investments. Interest rates rose significantly in recent years, and refinancing maturing debt that was issued when rates were lower could increase our interest expense. Disruptions in credit markets could limit our ability to refinance on acceptable terms, or at all.

Our borrowing costs and access to capital depend significantly on our credit ratings. Rating agencies consider factors such as our leverage, cash flows, revenue trends, capital allocation decisions and industry conditions. A downgrade could increase our borrowing costs, reduce the pool of investors willing to purchase our debt, and require us to provide collateral or other credit support under certain agreements. Continued revenue declines or capital allocation decisions that increase leverage could put pressure on our ratings.

Our capital allocation decisions, including share repurchases, may not produce the intended results and could reduce our financial flexibility.

We must allocate cash flows among capital expenditures, debt reduction, spectrum and other investments, and returns to stockholders. In fiscal 2024, we repurchased a significant amount of our common stock, and the amount we returned to stockholders through repurchases exceeded our cash flows from operations less capital expenditures. These repurchases were funded in part by proceeds from other investing activities and by existing cash, and they contributed to decreases in both our cash balance and our total stockholders' equity during the year.

Proceeds from asset sales and other non-recurring investing activities may not be available in future periods. If our operating cash flows decline, or if we need to increase capital spending to remain competitive, we may need to reduce or suspend share repurchases, borrow additional funds, or defer network investments. Each of these actions could have adverse consequences, and repurchases made at prices that later prove to be high would not create the value for stockholders that we expected. Continued reductions in stockholders' equity could also affect how lenders and rating agencies view our capital structure.

Our pension and other postretirement benefit obligations could require significant cash contributions and increase our expenses.

We sponsor defined benefit pension plans and postretirement health care and life insurance plans covering a large number of current and former employees, and these obligations make up a substantial part of our non-current liabilities. The funded status of these plans and our related expenses depend on factors such as discount rates, investment returns on plan assets, mortality assumptions, health care cost trends and legislative changes. Declines in interest rates or poor performance in financial markets could increase our unfunded obligations, require us to make larger cash contributions and increase reported expense. Workforce reductions may also trigger settlement or curtailment charges.

We could be required to record significant impairment charges related to goodwill, spectrum licenses and other long-lived assets.

A substantial portion of our total assets consists of goodwill, wireless spectrum licenses, other intangible assets and property, plant and equipment. We test goodwill and indefinite-lived intangible assets, including spectrum licenses, for impairment at least annually, and test other long-lived assets when events indicate that their carrying values may not be recoverable. Continued declines in revenue or cash flows in a reporting unit, increases in discount rates, changes in market valuations, decisions to retire legacy networks, or unfavorable regulatory developments affecting the value of spectrum could require us to record material non-cash impairment charges. Such charges would reduce our net income and stockholders' equity and could affect how investors and rating agencies view our financial position.

We are exposed to credit risk from customers, including on device installment plans.

We extend credit to a large number of consumer and business customers, including through device installment plans under which customers pay for equipment over time. Weak economic conditions, rising consumer debt, higher unemployment or inflation could increase delinquencies and bad debt expense. We also face credit risk from wholesale and enterprise customers, some of which may experience financial distress. If our collection experience deteriorates, we may need to tighten credit standards, which could reduce sales, or record higher provisions for credit losses.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

We are subject to extensive and evolving regulation, and changes in regulation could adversely affect our business.

Our operations are subject to regulation by the FCC, state public utility commissions, and other federal, state and local authorities. These regulations govern matters such as spectrum licensing, network interconnection, service obligations, pricing for certain wholesale services, pole attachments, rights-of-way, emergency services, accessibility and the discontinuance of legacy services. Requirements to maintain copper networks or legacy services, or delays in obtaining approval to retire them, could prevent us from reducing costs. Regulators may also impose rules on broadband internet access, including requirements related to network management, pricing transparency or service quality, that limit how we manage and price our services.

Federal and state programs that fund broadband deployment and support service in high-cost areas are also subject to legal challenges and policy changes. Changes in the universal service contribution system, in broadband subsidy programs, or in eligibility for funding could increase our costs, reduce our revenue or make network investments in certain areas uneconomical. Changes in administration, legislation and court decisions can significantly alter the regulatory landscape in ways that are difficult to predict.

Laws and regulations concerning privacy, data protection and consumer protection could increase our costs and expose us to liability.

We collect, store and use large amounts of personal information, including customer proprietary network information and location data, that is subject to federal and state laws governing privacy, data security and data breach notification. A growing number of states have enacted comprehensive privacy laws with differing requirements, and federal regulators have taken enforcement actions against communications providers concerning the use and protection of customer data. Complying with these laws requires significant resources and may limit our ability to use data for marketing, advertising and product development. Violations could result in fines, consent decrees, litigation and reputational harm. We are also subject to consumer protection laws governing billing, advertising, telemarketing and robocall mitigation, and noncompliance could result in penalties.

We are subject to litigation and investigations that could result in significant costs and liabilities.

We are involved in a range of legal proceedings, including consumer class actions, intellectual property claims, commercial disputes, employment claims, environmental matters and regulatory investigations. Communications providers are frequent targets of patent infringement claims, often brought by entities that do not themselves produce products. The outcome of litigation is inherently uncertain, and an adverse judgment, settlement or regulatory order could require significant payments, changes to our business practices or restrictions on our operations. Even where claims lack merit, defending them can be costly and distract management.

Changes in tax laws or the resolution of tax audits could adversely affect our results.

We are subject to income and other taxes at the federal, state and local levels, and our effective tax rate depends on the mix of our earnings, the availability of deductions and credits, and the interpretation of complex tax laws. Changes in tax legislation, including changes to corporate tax rates, the deductibility of interest, depreciation and expensing rules, or taxes on share repurchases, could increase our tax expense and reduce cash flows. We are also subject to regulatory fees, surcharges and non-income taxes on communications services, which may increase. Tax authorities regularly audit our returns, and the resolution of audits could result in additional tax liabilities.

Concerns about radio frequency emissions and the siting of network facilities could affect our operations.

Allegations that radio frequency emissions from wireless devices and cell sites pose health risks could lead to litigation, regulation or reduced customer use of wireless services. Local opposition to the placement of towers, small cells and other network facilities, together with permitting, zoning and right-of-way requirements, could delay network deployment and increase costs, particularly for the dense networks needed to support 5G and fixed wireless access.

General Risks

Adverse economic conditions could reduce demand for our services and increase our costs.

Our business is affected by general economic conditions in the United States. An economic downturn, high inflation, elevated interest rates or rising unemployment could cause consumers to reduce spending on premium plans and new devices, switch to lower-priced services, or fail to pay their bills, and could lead businesses and government customers to reduce or delay purchases. Inflation has increased, and could continue to increase, our costs for labor, energy, network equipment, construction and services, and competitive conditions may prevent us from passing these increases on to customers.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile, and our share repurchase program may be modified or suspended.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly due to our operating results, changes in revenue trends, competitive developments, regulatory actions, changes in our credit ratings, analyst expectations and broader market conditions. We do not currently pay a dividend, and our repurchases of common stock are made at the discretion of our board of directors. The board may modify, suspend or terminate repurchases at any time based on our cash flows, capital needs, debt levels and other factors, and any such change could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

Our ability to use artificial intelligence effectively, and the risks associated with its use, could affect our competitiveness.

We are deploying artificial intelligence and machine learning tools in network operations, customer care, sales and software development to improve efficiency and service quality. These technologies are evolving rapidly, and competitors may use them more effectively or earlier than we do. Our use of artificial intelligence may also produce inaccurate or biased results, create cybersecurity, privacy or intellectual property risks, and become subject to new regulation, any of which could result in reputational harm, liability or additional compliance costs.

Climate change and related regulatory and stakeholder expectations could increase our costs.

In addition to the physical risks posed by extreme weather, we may face increased costs from climate-related regulation, disclosure requirements, carbon pricing and higher energy prices, given that our network consumes significant amounts of electricity. Stakeholder expectations regarding environmental and other sustainability matters are evolving, and our failure to meet those expectations, or to achieve any sustainability goals we announce, could harm our reputation. At the same time, our sustainability practices may draw criticism from stakeholders with opposing views.

Provisions of our governing documents and Delaware law could delay or prevent a change in control.

Our certificate of incorporation, bylaws and Delaware law contain provisions that could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us without the consent of our board of directors, even if a transaction would benefit our stockholders. In addition, transactions involving a change in control of our FCC and state licenses require prior regulatory approval, which could delay or deter potential acquirers.
