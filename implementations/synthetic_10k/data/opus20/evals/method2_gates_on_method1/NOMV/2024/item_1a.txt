Item 1A. Risk Factors

Our business, financial condition, results of operations and cash flows are subject to a number of risks and uncertainties, many of which are beyond our control. Investors should consider the risks described below together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including our consolidated financial statements and the related notes. These are not the only risks we face. Additional risks that we do not currently know of, or that we currently consider immaterial, could also adversely affect us. If any of these risks occurs, the trading price of our common stock could decline.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Demand for our products is cyclical and depends on construction activity, which can decline sharply during economic downturns.

Most of our revenue comes from materials and products used in public infrastructure, non-residential construction and residential construction. Activity in each of these end markets rises and falls with broader economic conditions. Construction spending has historically fallen significantly during recessions, during periods of tight credit and when business and consumer confidence weakens. A slowdown in any major end market or region we serve could reduce our shipment volumes and pricing. Because a large share of our cost base is fixed, lower volumes reduce plant utilization and absorption of fixed costs, so profitability can decline faster than revenue.

We cannot predict when the construction cycle will turn, how long a downturn will last or how deep it will be. Our operating margin is already thin. As a result, a decline in construction demand of even moderate size could materially increase our net losses and reduce the cash we generate from operations.

A reduction or delay in government funding for infrastructure could reduce demand for our construction materials and engineered products.

A significant portion of our Construction Materials and Engineered Products sales supports highway, bridge, airport, transit, water and wastewater projects funded by federal, state and local governments. Public infrastructure spending depends on legislative appropriations, multi-year federal transportation authorizations, state fuel tax and other dedicated revenues, municipal bond markets and the budget condition of the agencies that award work. Funding programs may expire, be extended only temporarily, be reduced or be redirected to other priorities. State and local budget shortfalls, political disagreements, government shutdowns or changes in administration could delay project lettings or cancel planned work.

Even when funds are appropriated, the timing of contract awards and construction starts can lag for extended periods because of planning, permitting, environmental review and labor or contractor capacity constraints. Any reduction, delay or uncertainty in public funding could reduce our volumes in affected markets and adversely affect our results of operations.

Higher interest rates, tighter credit and weaker housing conditions could reduce private construction activity.

Residential construction, including new single-family and multi-family housing and repair and remodeling, depends heavily on mortgage rates, home affordability, household formation and consumer confidence. Non-residential projects such as warehouses, manufacturing plants, data centers, office and retail buildings depend on the availability and cost of financing, occupancy and rental trends, and the expected return on new investment. When interest rates rise or lenders tighten standards, developers and homebuilders may postpone or cancel projects. Changes in the pace of investment in specific sectors, such as data centers, energy facilities or advanced manufacturing, could also cause sudden shifts in regional demand. Any sustained weakness in private construction could adversely affect sales in our Construction Materials and Engineered Products segments.

We operate in highly competitive local markets, and competitive pressure could limit our ability to maintain or raise prices.

Construction materials are generally sold in local markets, and in each of them we compete with national and regional building materials producers, vertically integrated contractors and smaller independent operators. In cement, we also compete with imported product, which can enter coastal and river-served markets at prices that reflect excess capacity, currency movements or subsidies abroad. In engineered building products, we compete with manufacturers of alternative materials such as plastic, steel and composite pipe and structures, and with producers that may have lower cost structures in certain regions. In specialty minerals, we compete on quality, technical service and delivered cost with domestic and foreign producers.

New capacity, aggressive pricing by competitors, consolidation among our customers or the entry of new participants into a local market could reduce our market share or force us to lower prices. Because our margins are narrow, our ability to recover cost increases through pricing is especially important to our results. If we cannot do so, our operating income could decline further.

Our results are affected by weather and seasonality, which can cause significant variation in quarterly and annual performance.

Construction activity in most of our markets is lower in winter months and during periods of heavy rain, flooding, extreme heat or other severe weather. Prolonged periods of adverse weather can delay or cancel projects, reduce shipments and increase operating costs. They can also shift volumes between periods in ways that make our results difficult to predict. Because our plants and equipment carry substantial fixed costs throughout the year, weather-driven volume shortfalls can have a disproportionate effect on our profitability and liquidity in a given quarter or year.

Increases in the cost or disruptions in the supply of energy, fuel and other inputs could reduce our margins.

Our operations consume large quantities of energy. Cement kilns and lime kilns require significant amounts of coal, natural gas, petroleum coke and alternative fuels, as well as electricity. Our quarries, plants and delivery fleets consume diesel fuel. We also purchase raw materials and consumables, including liquid asphalt, cement, admixtures, steel reinforcement, resins, explosives, repair parts and tires. Prices for many of these inputs are volatile and are influenced by global commodity markets, geopolitical events, weather, regulatory changes and supply disruptions.

Cost of revenue represents the large majority of our revenue, and it grew faster than revenue in fiscal 2024. Our gross margin therefore narrowed. In much of our Construction Materials and Engineered Products business, prices are quoted in advance or fixed for the duration of a project, and we may be unable to pass through cost increases on a timely basis. Although some Specialty Minerals supply agreements include energy or input cost adjustment provisions, these provisions may not fully or promptly offset our cost increases. Any significant increase in input costs, or any interruption in supply, could materially and adversely affect our results of operations.

Our growth strategy depends in part on acquisitions, which involve significant risks and may not deliver the benefits we expect.

We have made substantial investments in acquisitions and other investing activities to broaden our geographic footprint and product offering, and we expect to continue to pursue acquisitions that complement our existing operations. Acquisitions involve numerous risks, including:

Paying more than an acquired business is ultimately worth, or overestimating the reserves, demand or synergies associated with it. Difficulties integrating operations, information systems, safety practices, personnel and customer relationships. Assuming unknown or contingent liabilities, including environmental, reclamation, product liability or tax liabilities. Diversion of management attention from existing operations. Regulatory review that may delay a transaction, impose conditions or require divestitures. Increased indebtedness or dilution to stockholders used to fund the transaction.

We may not be able to identify suitable acquisition candidates or complete transactions on acceptable terms. Acquisitions we complete may not perform as anticipated. If acquired businesses fail to meet our expectations, we may record impairment charges and our results of operations and financial condition could be adversely affected.

Demand for our specialty minerals depends on the performance of a limited number of industrial end markets.

Our Specialty Minerals segment sells lime, industrial sand, calcium carbonate and other mineral products to customers in steelmaking, environmental services, water treatment, glass, paper and packaging, plastics and agriculture. Some of these industries are themselves cyclical or are undergoing structural change. Examples include declining demand for certain paper grades, changes in steelmaking technology, and shifts in power generation that affect demand for flue gas treatment products. The closure, idling or relocation of a customer facility that we supply from a nearby plant could leave that plant without a comparable alternative customer. If demand in these end markets weakens, or if key customers reduce their purchases or do not renew supply agreements, our Specialty Minerals results could decline.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Our business depends on our ability to secure, permit and maintain sufficient mineral reserves near our markets.

Our Construction Materials and Specialty Minerals segments depend on access to economically recoverable reserves of aggregates, limestone, industrial sand and other minerals located close to customers. Because these materials are heavy and costly to transport, reserves that are far from demand may have limited economic value. Obtaining zoning approvals and permits for new quarries, or for expansion of existing sites, is frequently time-consuming, expensive and uncertain. It may also face opposition from local communities, environmental organizations and governmental authorities.

Over time, urban growth, changes in land use regulation and restrictions on mining near residential areas, waterways or protected habitats may limit our ability to replenish reserves. Estimates of reserve quantity and quality are based on geological interpretation and assumptions that may prove inaccurate. If we cannot replace depleted reserves on acceptable terms, or if our reserves prove smaller or of lower quality than estimated, our ability to serve certain markets could be impaired.

Our business is capital intensive, and we may be unable to fund the investments needed to maintain and grow our operations.

We operate a large base of quarries, kilns, plants, terminals, mobile equipment and delivery fleets that require continual maintenance, replacement and upgrades. Property, plant and equipment is the largest asset on our balance sheet, and we continued to invest in it during fiscal 2024. We also invest to comply with environmental and safety requirements, to improve energy efficiency and to expand capacity in growing markets.

Our cash from operations was not sufficient in fiscal 2024 to fund our capital expenditures and investing activities, and we relied on external financing, including borrowings and equity issuances, to fund the difference. If our operating cash flow declines, or if external financing becomes more costly or unavailable, we may have to defer maintenance or growth projects. Deferring these investments could reduce efficiency, increase the risk of equipment failure and weaken our competitive position.

Our operations involve inherent hazards that could result in injuries, property damage, production interruptions and liability.

Our operations involve blasting, heavy mobile equipment, high-temperature kilns, crushing and conveying equipment, the handling of hazardous materials and the operation of large truck fleets on public roads. These activities expose our employees, contractors and the public to risks of serious injury or death. They also expose our facilities to fires, explosions, structural failures, slope failures, equipment breakdowns and releases of hazardous substances. A serious incident could result in fines, litigation, regulatory orders that suspend operations, reputational harm and significant repair costs.

Many of our facilities, particularly cement and lime plants, are large single-site operations. An extended unplanned outage at one of these facilities could force us to purchase product from third parties at higher cost or to forgo sales. Although we maintain insurance, our coverage may be insufficient, may be subject to large deductibles or exclusions, or may not be available on acceptable terms in the future.

Disruptions in transportation and logistics could increase our costs and impair our ability to serve customers.

We rely on company-operated and third-party trucks, railroads and barges to move raw materials to our plants and finished products to customers and distribution terminals. Rail service disruptions, rate increases, barge restrictions caused by low or high river levels, shortages of qualified commercial drivers, and congestion or closures of key routes could increase our delivered costs or prevent timely deliveries. Because freight represents a meaningful portion of the delivered cost of many of our products, transportation cost increases that we cannot pass on to customers could reduce our margins and competitiveness in distant markets.

We may be unable to attract, retain and develop a qualified workforce, and labor costs may increase.

We employ tens of thousands of people across numerous operating locations, many in skilled trades such as equipment operation, maintenance, electrical work and commercial driving. Competition for these workers is intense in many of our markets. Labor shortages could limit our ability to staff plants, operate fleets and meet customer demand. Wage inflation, rising healthcare and benefit costs, and changes in labor laws could increase our costs. A portion of our workforce is represented by labor unions, and strikes, work stoppages or difficult contract negotiations could disrupt operations at affected facilities.

Cybersecurity incidents and failures of our information technology systems could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.

We depend on information technology and operational technology systems to manage plant processes, dispatch and deliver products, process orders and payments, manage our supply chain and maintain financial records. These systems, and those of our vendors and acquired businesses, are exposed to cyberattacks, ransomware, malicious insiders, software defects, power outages and other disruptions. The integration of acquired businesses can introduce additional vulnerabilities. A significant incident could interrupt production and deliveries, compromise confidential or personal information, result in regulatory penalties and litigation, and require substantial remediation costs.

Fixed-price commitments on construction projects expose us to the risk of cost overruns.

In our Construction Materials and Engineered Products segments, we frequently quote prices to contractors who incorporate those prices into competitive bids for multi-month or multi-year projects. Where we also perform paving and related construction services, we may enter into fixed-price or unit-price contracts. If our costs for energy, materials, labor or freight rise after we quote a price, or if a project's schedule or scope changes, we may be unable to recover the additional costs. Project delays, design errors, adverse site conditions, warranty claims and disputes with customers over performance or change orders could also reduce the profitability of these projects.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

We have incurred net losses, and our operating income does not currently cover our interest expense.

We reported net losses in both fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023, and our net loss increased in fiscal 2024. Although our revenue grew, our operating income declined. Selling, general and administrative expenses and other operating expenses increased faster than revenue, and our gross margin narrowed. In both years, our interest expense exceeded our operating income.

We cannot assure you that we will achieve or sustain profitability. Achieving profitability will depend on our ability to improve pricing, control costs, realize expected returns from our capital investments and acquisitions, and reduce our financing costs. If we continue to incur losses, our stockholders' equity will decline. Our ability to obtain financing on acceptable terms could also be impaired.

Our substantial indebtedness could adversely affect our financial flexibility and our ability to operate our business.

We have a substantial amount of long-term debt, and our borrowings increased during fiscal 2024. Our level of indebtedness could:

Require us to dedicate a significant portion of our operating cash flow to interest and principal payments, reducing funds available for capital expenditures, acquisitions and other purposes. Increase our vulnerability to downturns in construction activity and to rising interest rates. Limit our ability to obtain additional financing for working capital, capital expenditures, acquisitions or general corporate purposes. Place us at a competitive disadvantage relative to competitors with less leverage. Restrict our flexibility to respond to changes in our business and industry.

We may incur additional indebtedness in the future, including to fund acquisitions, which would intensify these risks.

We may be unable to refinance maturing debt on favorable terms, and rising interest rates could increase our borrowing costs.

Our ability to repay or refinance our debt as it matures depends on our future operating performance and on conditions in the credit and capital markets, which are beyond our control. Given our history of net losses and the relationship between our operating income and interest expense, lenders and rating agencies may view our credit profile less favorably. That view could increase our borrowing costs, restrict our access to certain markets or result in more restrictive terms. Any portion of our debt that bears interest at floating rates exposes us to increases in market interest rates. If we refinance fixed-rate debt in a higher rate environment, our interest expense would rise. A downgrade in our credit ratings could further increase our cost of capital and adversely affect our relationships with suppliers and other counterparties.

Our debt agreements contain covenants that restrict our activities, and a failure to comply could result in acceleration of our debt.

The agreements governing our indebtedness contain covenants that may limit our ability to incur additional debt, grant liens, sell assets, make certain investments and acquisitions, and engage in mergers and similar transactions. Some agreements may require us to maintain specified financial ratios. Continued weakness in our earnings could make it more difficult to comply with these requirements. A breach of a covenant could result in an event of default. Lenders could then accelerate repayment, terminate commitments or exercise remedies. Cross-default provisions could cause other indebtedness to become due. We might not have sufficient liquidity to repay accelerated debt.

We have a significant amount of goodwill and long-lived assets that could become impaired.

Goodwill represents a significant portion of our total assets and of our stockholders' equity, largely as a result of past acquisitions. We also carry substantial property, plant and equipment and mineral reserves. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually, and we test long-lived assets whenever events indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. Declines in construction activity, lower margins, higher discount rates, a decline in our market capitalization or underperformance by acquired businesses could require us to record impairment charges. Such charges would not affect our cash, but they could be material to our reported results and stockholders' equity. They could also affect our compliance with certain financial covenants.

We may be unable to realize the benefit of our deferred tax assets.

We did not record a provision for income taxes in fiscal 2024 or fiscal 2023, reflecting our pre-tax losses and our assessment of the realizability of deferred tax assets. Our ability to use net operating loss carryforwards and other tax attributes depends on generating sufficient future taxable income, and may be limited by tax rules, including limitations that apply after certain ownership changes. If we cannot generate sufficient taxable income, these attributes may expire unused. Changes in our assessment of the need for valuation allowances could also cause significant fluctuations in our effective tax rate and reported results.

Future issuances of equity securities may dilute the ownership interests of our existing stockholders.

During fiscal 2024, our share issuances exceeded our share repurchases, providing net cash from financing activities. We may issue additional shares of common stock or securities convertible into common stock in the future. Such issuances could fund acquisitions, reduce indebtedness, support capital investment or serve as consideration in business combinations. We may also issue shares under employee compensation plans. Any such issuance could dilute existing stockholders and could cause the market price of our common stock to decline.

We do not currently pay dividends, and we may not do so in the future.

We did not pay dividends on our common stock in fiscal 2024. Any future decision to pay dividends will be at the discretion of our Board of Directors and will depend on our results of operations, financial condition, capital requirements, contractual restrictions in our debt agreements and other factors. As a result, investors may need to rely on appreciation in the price of our common stock, which may not occur, to realize a return on their investment.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

We are subject to extensive environmental, health and safety laws, and compliance costs and liabilities could be significant.

Our operations are subject to federal, state and local laws and regulations governing air emissions, water discharges, stormwater management, waste handling and disposal, the use of hazardous substances, the protection of wetlands and endangered species, noise, vibration, dust and the remediation of contaminated sites. Cement and lime kilns in particular are subject to stringent air emission standards for particulate matter, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide, mercury and other pollutants. Compliance requires significant capital and operating expenditures. These requirements tend to become more stringent over time. We may be held liable for contamination at sites we currently or formerly owned or operated, at sites acquired through acquisitions, or at third-party disposal sites, regardless of fault. Violations could result in fines, permit revocation, orders to curtail operations and litigation.

Laws and regulations addressing climate change and greenhouse gas emissions could increase our costs and reduce demand for some of our products.

Cement and lime manufacturing emits significant quantities of carbon dioxide. Most of these emissions come from the chemical conversion of limestone, which cannot be eliminated through fuel switching alone. Governmental bodies have adopted or are considering carbon pricing, emissions caps, reporting requirements, procurement standards that favor lower-carbon materials, and border adjustment mechanisms. These measures could require us to make substantial investments in emissions reduction technologies, purchase allowances or offsets, or modify our products and processes. Some of the technologies needed to reduce emissions significantly, such as carbon capture, are costly and not yet proven at commercial scale. Customers, governmental purchasers and investors are also increasingly focused on the carbon intensity of building materials. If we cannot meet their expectations, demand for our products could decline or our cost of capital could rise.

Our mining operations are subject to mine safety and health regulation and to reclamation obligations.

Our quarries, pits and mines are subject to federal and state mine safety and health laws, which impose detailed requirements and provide for inspections, citations, penalties and orders that can close all or part of an operation. Changes to these regulations, including more restrictive exposure limits for respirable crystalline silica, could require additional engineering controls, monitoring and medical surveillance. We are also required to reclaim land disturbed by our mining activities and to provide financial assurance for these obligations. Our estimates of reclamation and asset retirement costs are based on assumptions that may change. Actual costs could exceed the amounts we have recognized.

We are subject to litigation and claims that could result in significant liabilities.

In the ordinary course of business, we are involved in lawsuits, claims and governmental proceedings. These include matters relating to:

Personal injury and vehicle accidents. Product defects and warranties. Contract disputes. Employment matters. Environmental conditions and permitting. Competition and pricing practices.

Certain claims, such as those alleging exposure to silica or other substances, can involve many plaintiffs and lengthy proceedings. The outcome of litigation is inherently uncertain. Adverse judgments or settlements, or the cost of defending claims, could materially affect our results of operations and cash flows in the period in which they are recognized.

Changes in trade policy and tax laws could adversely affect our business.

Tariffs, anti-dumping and countervailing duties, and other trade measures affect the cost of imported equipment, parts and raw materials we purchase. They also affect the price at which imported cement and other competing products enter our markets. Changes in these measures could raise our costs or intensify import competition. Changes in federal, state or local tax laws, including the treatment of interest deductions, depreciation, depletion and net operating losses, could increase our tax burden or reduce the value of our tax attributes.

General Risks

Inflation and broader macroeconomic and geopolitical conditions could adversely affect our costs and the demand for our products.

Inflationary pressures on labor, energy, materials, equipment and services can raise our costs faster than we can adjust prices. These pressures can also prompt monetary policy actions that slow construction activity. Geopolitical conflicts, pandemics, financial market disruptions and other events could disrupt supply chains, reduce construction investment and limit our access to capital.

Physical effects of climate change and extreme weather could damage our facilities and disrupt operations.

Many of our operations are located in areas exposed to hurricanes, flooding, wildfires, drought, extreme heat and severe winter storms. These events could damage our quarries, plants and terminals, interrupt power and water supplies, impair river and rail transportation, and delay construction projects. More frequent or severe events could increase our operating and insurance costs and reduce the availability of insurance coverage.

The loss of key personnel or the failure to plan effectively for succession could harm our business.

Our success depends on the experience and judgment of our senior leadership team and of the managers who run our local operations and maintain relationships with customers, public agencies and communities. The loss of key individuals, or difficulty attracting and retaining qualified managers, could impair our ability to execute our strategy and integrate acquired businesses.

Our reported results depend on estimates and judgments, and changes in accounting standards could affect our financial statements.

The preparation of our consolidated financial statements requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect reported amounts. These include estimates relating to goodwill and long-lived asset impairment, mineral reserves, asset retirement obligations, income tax valuation allowances, self-insurance reserves, pension obligations and the fair value of assets acquired in business combinations. Actual results may differ materially from these estimates. New accounting standards or changes in interpretation could also require us to change our accounting policies, which could affect our reported results.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors, including:

Variations in our quarterly results. Changes in construction activity and public funding. Announcements of acquisitions or financing transactions. Changes in analysts' estimates or credit ratings. Conditions affecting the building materials industry. General market volatility.

Some of these factors are unrelated to our operating performance.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could discourage or delay a change in control.

We are incorporated in Delaware. Certain provisions of Delaware law and of our certificate of incorporation and bylaws could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us, even if a change in control would benefit our stockholders. These include provisions governing stockholder proposals, director nominations and the ability of our Board of Directors to issue preferred stock. Such provisions could limit the price that investors are willing to pay for our common stock.
