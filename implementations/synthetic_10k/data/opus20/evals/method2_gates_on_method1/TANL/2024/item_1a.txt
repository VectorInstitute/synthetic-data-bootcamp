Item 1A. Risk Factors

Investing in our common stock involves risk. Before making an investment decision, you should consider carefully the risks described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations and our consolidated financial statements and related notes. The risks below are those we currently consider material. Risks we have not yet identified, or that we currently regard as less significant, could also harm us. If any of these risks materializes, our revenue, profitability, cash flows, financial condition and the trading price of our common stock could decline, possibly significantly. The order in which these risks appear does not indicate their relative likelihood or the magnitude of their potential effect.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Government and private-sector efforts to reduce drug prices and health care spending could reduce the revenue and profitability of each of our segments.

Federal and state governments, commercial payers, pharmacy benefit managers and employers continue to seek ways to lower prescription drug prices and overall health care costs. Federal law now permits the government to negotiate prices for certain high-expenditure drugs reimbursed under Medicare. It also requires manufacturers to pay rebates when prices for certain drugs rise faster than inflation and redesigns the Medicare Part D benefit in ways that shift greater financial responsibility to manufacturers. Several states have enacted or proposed drug price transparency requirements, prescription drug affordability boards, upper payment limits and importation programs.

These measures could reduce the net prices we realize for products in our Therapeutics segment, particularly for our established branded medicines, which may become eligible for negotiation. They could also compress the margins we earn on pharmaceutical products that we distribute and dispense, and reduce the reimbursement we receive for services in our Care Delivery and Health Services segment. Because these policies continue to evolve and are subject to rulemaking and litigation, we cannot predict their ultimate scope or financial effect.

Our results depend on coverage and reimbursement decisions by third-party payers over which we have limited control.

Demand for our branded medicines, and the economics of the products we dispense and the services we provide, depend heavily on whether government programs, commercial insurers and pharmacy benefit managers cover those products and services, and on what terms. Payers increasingly use formulary exclusions, prior authorization, step therapy, utilization management and specialty tiering to steer patients toward lower-cost alternatives. They also negotiate larger rebates and price concessions as a condition of favorable formulary placement.

An unfavorable coverage decision for one of our significant products could sharply reduce its utilization. So could a reduction in reimbursement rates for dispensing fees, infusion services or care management services. Changes in the mix of patients covered by government programs relative to commercial insurance, including changes resulting from Medicaid eligibility redeterminations or shifts in employer-sponsored coverage, could also reduce our average reimbursement.

The loss of, or a significant reduction in business from, one or more large customers could adversely affect us.

A substantial portion of the revenue in our Pharmacy and Distribution Services segment is derived from a relatively small number of large customers, including national retail pharmacy chains, large health systems and group purchasing organizations. Our agreements with these customers generally have fixed terms. On renewal, customers may seek lower pricing, more favorable payment terms or expanded service commitments, and they may solicit bids from our competitors.

Consolidation among retail pharmacies, health systems and physician groups has increased the purchasing power of these customers. If a significant customer does not renew its agreement, renews on materially less favorable terms, reduces its purchases, experiences financial distress or decides to source products directly from manufacturers, our revenue and operating income could decline. We might not be able to reduce related costs quickly enough to offset that decline.

Our distribution economics depend on arrangements with pharmaceutical manufacturers and on trends in branded and generic drug pricing.

The profitability of our distribution business depends in significant part on the terms of our agreements with branded, generic and specialty manufacturers. These terms include distribution service fees, purchase discounts, inventory management provisions and the timing and extent of manufacturer price changes. Manufacturers may seek to reduce fees, shift distribution to limited networks or exclusive arrangements, or sell directly to dispensing sites.

Our margins on generic pharmaceuticals depend on our ability to source products at competitive cost. Sustained deflation in generic drug prices, or reduced opportunities to obtain favorable purchasing terms, could reduce our margins. Similarly, a slowdown in branded price increases could reduce the compensation we earn under arrangements that are tied to manufacturer list prices.

The loss of patent protection or regulatory exclusivity for our branded products could significantly reduce revenue in our Therapeutics segment.

Products in our Therapeutics segment contribute a disproportionately large share of our gross profit relative to their share of our revenue. Many of these products rely on patents and regulatory exclusivity to deter competition. When that protection expires, or is successfully challenged, competitors may introduce generic or biosimilar versions. Prices and volumes for the original product typically decline rapidly once that happens.

Generic and biosimilar manufacturers may challenge our patents before they expire, and they may launch products at risk while litigation is pending. A loss of exclusivity for one or more of our significant products earlier than expected, or a faster-than-expected erosion of sales following loss of exclusivity, could materially reduce our consolidated gross margin and operating income.

Our substantial investment in research and development may not result in commercially successful products.

We devote significant resources to discovering and developing new medicines and to expanding indications for existing products. Our research and development expense increased in fiscal 2024 in absolute terms and remained a meaningful share of our revenue. Drug development is lengthy, expensive and uncertain, and most product candidates do not reach commercialization. A candidate may fail to demonstrate safety or efficacy in clinical trials, may produce results that regulators or payers do not consider sufficient, or may be delayed by difficulties with patient enrollment, manufacturing or regulatory review.

Even when we obtain approval, a product may fail to achieve market acceptance or adequate reimbursement, or it may face competition from therapies that are more effective, more convenient or less expensive. If our pipeline does not produce new products in time to offset declines in our existing portfolio, our long-term growth and profitability could suffer. Any significant write-off of acquired or licensed research programs could also adversely affect our results of operations.

We face intense competition in each of our segments.

Our Therapeutics segment competes with large multinational pharmaceutical companies, biotechnology companies and generic and biosimilar manufacturers. Many of these competitors have greater resources in particular therapeutic areas. Our Pharmacy and Distribution Services segment competes with other national and regional wholesale distributors, specialty distributors, pharmacy benefit manager-affiliated specialty and mail-order pharmacies, and manufacturers that sell directly to providers. Our Care Delivery and Health Services segment competes with health systems, physician groups, payer-owned care delivery organizations, retail clinic operators and technology-enabled care companies.

Competitors may offer lower prices, broader service offerings or more advanced technology. Some competitors are vertically integrated with payers in ways that give them preferential access to patients. Competitive pressure could force us to reduce prices, increase spending on service enhancements or forgo business. Any of these outcomes could reduce our revenue or margins.

Value-based and risk-sharing arrangements expose us to the risk that medical costs or outcomes differ from our expectations.

In our Care Delivery and Health Services segment, we increasingly enter into contracts under which a portion of our compensation depends on achieving quality, utilization or total cost of care targets. Some of these contracts require us to bear financial responsibility for costs that exceed agreed benchmarks.

Our ability to succeed under these arrangements depends on accurately estimating the health status and expected costs of attributed patient populations. It also depends on effectively managing care and receiving timely and accurate data from payers. Benchmark methodologies, risk adjustment models and program rules, particularly in government programs, may change in ways that reduce the payments we receive. If actual medical costs exceed our estimates, or if we fail to meet quality targets, we could incur losses on these contracts. We may also be required to record significant adjustments to previously reported revenue.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Because most of our revenue is generated at relatively low margins, small changes in costs or pricing can have a disproportionate effect on our operating income.

Cost of revenue represents the large majority of our revenue, reflecting the scale of our Pharmacy and Distribution Services segment. As a result, our consolidated gross margin and operating margin are narrow relative to our revenue base. Our gross margin declined modestly in fiscal 2024. During the same period, selling, general and administrative expenses increased as a share of revenue and operating income grew more slowly than revenue.

A modest increase in product acquisition costs, labor, freight, fuel, facility or technology costs could materially reduce our operating income if we cannot pass those increases through to customers. The same is true of a modest decline in the prices or fees we realize. Our ability to sustain profitability therefore depends on continued operating discipline, productivity improvements and favorable pricing and product mix, none of which can be assured.

Disruptions in our supply chain or manufacturing operations could prevent us from meeting customer and patient demand.

We rely on a complex network of third-party manufacturers, active pharmaceutical ingredient suppliers, contract manufacturers, packaging vendors and logistics providers. Some of these suppliers are located outside the United States or are the sole qualified source of a particular material or product. Disruptions at any point in this network could cause product shortages or delays. Such disruptions could result from quality failures, regulatory actions, natural disasters, geopolitical conflicts, trade restrictions, labor disputes or financial difficulties at a supplier.

Our own manufacturing facilities and distribution centers are also subject to operational risks, including equipment failure, contamination, fire, severe weather and power outages. Many of the products we handle require temperature control or are subject to controlled substance requirements. If our facilities or those of our suppliers fail to meet current Good Manufacturing Practice or other applicable standards, we could face product recalls, regulatory sanctions, lost sales and reputational harm.

Our significant investment in inventories and accounts receivable exposes us to obsolescence and customer credit risk.

To serve our customers reliably, we maintain substantial inventories of pharmaceutical products, and we extend credit to a broad range of customers. Both inventories and accounts receivable increased in fiscal 2024, and together they represent a substantial portion of our current assets.

Inventories are subject to the risk of expiration, damage, recall, theft and decline in value, including when manufacturers reduce list prices or generic competition emerges. Accounts receivable are exposed to the risk that customers fail to pay amounts when due. That risk is heightened for independent pharmacies, physician practices and smaller health care providers that may be affected by reimbursement pressure or economic conditions. The bankruptcy or financial distress of a significant customer could require us to write off substantial receivables and could disrupt the related revenue stream.

Our large workforce makes us vulnerable to labor shortages, wage inflation and changes in employment law.

We employ approximately 852,000 people, including pharmacists, pharmacy technicians, nurses, physicians, scientists, warehouse personnel, drivers and corporate staff. The health care industry continues to experience shortages of qualified clinical professionals, particularly pharmacists, nurses and primary care clinicians. Competition for scientific and technical talent remains intense.

Shortages could require us to increase wages and benefits, rely on more costly temporary staffing, or reduce hours or services at certain locations. Because labor represents a significant portion of our operating expenses, sustained wage inflation could pressure our margins. Union organizing activity, work stoppages and changes in laws governing wages, overtime, scheduling, benefits and worker classification could also increase our costs or disrupt operations. The loss of key executives or scientific leaders could impair our ability to execute our strategy.

Cybersecurity incidents or failures of our information technology systems could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.

Our operations depend on information technology systems to process orders, manage inventory, adjudicate pharmacy claims, maintain clinical records, conduct research and support financial reporting. We collect and store large volumes of protected health information, personal information and proprietary research data. Our systems, and those of the vendors and partners on which we rely, are subject to ransomware, phishing, insider threats, software vulnerabilities and other cyber threats. Health care organizations have become frequent targets of these threats.

A successful attack or significant system failure could interrupt product distribution, pharmacy dispensing and patient care. It could also compromise sensitive data, result in regulatory investigations, litigation and notification obligations, and damage our reputation with customers, patients and partners. The costs of preventing, detecting and responding to such incidents are substantial and continue to grow. Our insurance coverage may not be sufficient to cover all resulting losses.

Acquisitions, licensing transactions and other strategic investments may not achieve their intended benefits.

We regularly pursue acquisitions, in-licensing arrangements, collaborations and other investments to expand our pipeline, service offerings and geographic reach. Our investing activities in fiscal 2024 included significant outflows beyond capital expenditures. These transactions involve risks, including:

overpaying for an acquired business or asset; failing to integrate operations, systems, personnel and compliance programs effectively; inheriting undisclosed liabilities, including regulatory, litigation and tax exposures; diverting management attention from existing operations; and failing to realize expected revenue growth, cost synergies or clinical results.

If an acquisition or investment underperforms, we may be required to record impairment charges or restructuring costs, and our return on invested capital could decline.

Our international operations expose us to additional economic, political and regulatory risks.

Although most of our revenue is generated in the United States, we conduct research, manufacturing, sourcing and commercial activities in selected international markets. These activities are subject to risks that include foreign currency fluctuations, differing regulatory and pricing regimes, tariffs and trade restrictions, export controls and sanctions, political instability, and anti-corruption laws. They are also subject to the challenge of enforcing intellectual property rights in certain jurisdictions. Changes in trade policy affecting pharmaceutical ingredients or finished products could increase our costs or limit our ability to source critical materials.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

Our indebtedness could limit our flexibility and increase our vulnerability to adverse developments.

We have a substantial amount of long-term debt, which increased in fiscal 2024, and we incur meaningful interest expense each year. Our debt obligations require us to dedicate a portion of our operating cash flow to interest and principal payments. That portion is not available for research and development, capital investment, acquisitions, dividends or share repurchases.

We may incur additional debt to fund acquisitions, capital expenditures or other corporate purposes. When existing debt matures, we may need to refinance it at higher interest rates or on less favorable terms, particularly if credit markets tighten or our financial performance weakens. Our debt agreements contain covenants that restrict certain transactions, and a breach could result in the acceleration of amounts outstanding. A higher level of indebtedness would also leave us more exposed to economic downturns and competitive pressure than competitors with less leverage.

A downgrade in our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs and affect our relationships with suppliers and customers.

Our access to the commercial paper market, bank credit facilities and long-term debt markets, and the cost of that financing, depend in part on our credit ratings. Rating agencies evaluate our leverage, cash flow, business mix and financial policies. Additional borrowing to fund acquisitions or share repurchases, a significant litigation outcome, or a deterioration in operating results could lead to a downgrade. A downgrade could raise our interest costs, reduce our access to short-term funding and cause suppliers to tighten payment terms. Because our distribution business depends on favorable trade credit from manufacturers, any such tightening could significantly increase our working capital requirements.

We could be required to record significant impairment charges related to goodwill and other intangible assets.

As a result of past acquisitions, goodwill is the largest single asset on our consolidated balance sheet. It represents a substantial portion of our total stockholders' equity, and it increased during fiscal 2024. We also carry significant other intangible and long-lived assets.

We test goodwill for impairment at least annually, and more frequently if events indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. Several developments could reduce the estimated fair value of one or more reporting units, including:

adverse changes in reimbursement or drug pricing; loss of significant customers; failure of acquired research programs; underperformance of acquired care delivery businesses; and increases in discount rates.

Any resulting non-cash impairment charge could be material to our results of operations and stockholders' equity.

We depend on operating cash flow to fund our obligations and capital allocation priorities, and we may change or suspend dividends and share repurchases.

We maintain a relatively modest cash balance compared with the scale of our operations. We rely primarily on cash generated from operations, supplemented by access to credit markets, to meet working capital needs, service our debt, fund capital expenditures and acquisitions, and return capital to stockholders. In fiscal 2024, cash returned through dividends and share repurchases represented a substantial portion of our operating cash flow.

Our operating cash flow can fluctuate significantly from period to period. These fluctuations may result from the timing of customer collections, manufacturer payments, inventory purchases, rebate settlements and tax payments. A sustained decline in operating cash flow could require us to borrow more, reduce investment or curtail capital returns. Our board of directors may change, reduce or suspend our dividend or share repurchase program at any time, and any such action could adversely affect the price of our common stock.

Our substantial non-current liabilities could require significant future cash outlays.

In addition to long-term debt, we carry substantial other non-current liabilities. These may include obligations related to leases, litigation and settlement accruals, self-insured risks, deferred taxes and employee benefits. The amount and timing of the related cash payments depend on estimates and assumptions that may prove inaccurate. Unfavorable litigation developments, changes in actuarial assumptions, rising claims experience or changes in tax positions could increase these liabilities or accelerate the related payments, reducing the cash available for other purposes.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

We are subject to extensive health care laws and regulations, and violations could result in substantial penalties and exclusion from government programs.

Our businesses are subject to federal and state laws governing fraud and abuse, including anti-kickback statutes, physician self-referral laws, false claims acts, civil monetary penalty laws and beneficiary inducement prohibitions. They are also subject to rules governing drug pricing reporting, Medicaid drug rebates, the 340B drug pricing program and government contracting. These laws are complex, are subject to varying interpretation and are actively enforced by government agencies and private whistleblowers.

Our interactions with prescribers, pharmacies, payers and patients through patient support programs, hub services, specialty pharmacy operations and value-based arrangements are subject to particular scrutiny. If we are found to have violated these laws, we could face significant civil and criminal penalties, treble damages, repayment obligations, corporate integrity agreements and exclusion from federal health care programs. Any of these outcomes could have a material adverse effect on our business.

Our distribution and dispensing of controlled substances subjects us to regulatory oversight, litigation and potential liability.

We distribute and dispense controlled substances, including opioid medications. We are therefore required to maintain registrations with federal and state authorities, implement systems to identify and report suspicious orders, and comply with strict recordkeeping and security requirements. Pharmaceutical distributors and pharmacies throughout the industry have faced governmental investigations and extensive litigation alleging failure to adequately monitor and report suspicious orders of prescription opioids.

We may become subject to claims of this kind, or to new claims related to other controlled substances. Regulators may also impose additional compliance obligations that increase our costs. Adverse outcomes, settlements or regulatory actions, including suspension or revocation of a registration at one of our facilities, could result in substantial monetary obligations and operational disruption.

Our products are subject to rigorous regulatory requirements before and after approval.

The development, manufacturing, labeling, promotion and distribution of our medicines are regulated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and comparable authorities in other jurisdictions. Regulatory review can be lengthy and unpredictable, and regulators may require additional clinical data, impose restrictive labeling or decline approval altogether.

After approval, we remain subject to post-marketing study obligations, adverse event reporting, manufacturing inspections and restrictions on promotional activities. Newly identified safety signals could lead to label changes, risk evaluation and mitigation strategies, restrictions on use or product withdrawal. Our distribution operations must also comply with federal requirements for product tracing and verification throughout the pharmaceutical supply chain, and failure to meet those requirements could disrupt our ability to ship products.

We face exposure to product liability and professional liability claims.

The development, manufacture and sale of pharmaceutical products, the dispensing of medications and the delivery of clinical care each carry an inherent risk of injury to patients. We may be subject to claims alleging that our products caused harm, that our pharmacies made dispensing errors or that our clinicians provided negligent care. Such claims may be brought individually or as mass tort or class actions. We maintain insurance and self-insurance reserves, but coverage may be insufficient or unavailable for certain claims, and significant claims could exceed our reserves. Adverse publicity from such claims could reduce demand for our products and services.

Failure to comply with privacy and data protection laws could result in penalties and reputational harm.

We are subject to federal and state laws governing the privacy and security of health and personal information. These include the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 and its implementing regulations, state consumer privacy and health data laws, and, for our international activities, foreign data protection regimes. These laws are becoming more stringent and more varied, increasing our compliance costs and limiting how we may use data in research, analytics and commercial activities. Violations could result in significant fines, litigation, mandated corrective action and damage to the trust of patients and customers.

We may be unable to adequately protect our intellectual property or may be found to infringe the rights of others.

Our success in the Therapeutics segment depends on our ability to obtain and enforce patents, maintain trade secrets and protect other proprietary information. Patent applications may not result in issued patents, and issued patents may be challenged, narrowed or invalidated. Third parties may assert that our products or processes infringe their intellectual property. Litigation of this kind is costly, and an adverse outcome could require us to pay substantial damages or royalties, obtain licenses on unfavorable terms or stop selling a product.

Laws governing the corporate practice of medicine and pharmacy, and licensure requirements, could limit how we structure our care delivery business.

Many states prohibit or restrict business corporations from practicing medicine, employing physicians or sharing professional fees. In some states, we therefore provide clinical services through affiliated professional entities under management and administrative services arrangements. Regulators or courts could determine that these arrangements violate applicable law, which could require us to restructure our operations or affect our ability to consolidate those entities. Our pharmacies, distribution centers and care sites are also subject to state licensure, accreditation and certification requirements, and the loss or suspension of a license could disrupt operations at the affected site.

Changes in tax laws or adverse outcomes in tax examinations could increase our tax obligations.

Our effective tax rate has varied from year to year and depends on our geographic mix of earnings, the availability of tax credits, including credits for research activities, and the resolution of uncertain tax positions. Changes in U.S. federal or state tax law could increase our effective tax rate. So could the adoption by other countries of a global minimum tax framework or changes in the tax treatment of research expenditures or intellectual property. We are regularly examined by taxing authorities, and adverse determinations could require us to pay additional taxes, interest and penalties.

General Risks

Adverse economic conditions could reduce demand for our products and services and increase our costs.

Inflation, high interest rates, unemployment and economic uncertainty can affect us in several ways. Patients may defer care or discontinue therapies because of out-of-pocket costs. Employers may reduce health benefits, and government budgets may tighten, leading to reductions in health program spending. At the same time, inflation in labor, transportation, energy and materials costs can raise our operating expenses faster than we can raise prices, which is particularly significant given our narrow margins.

Public health emergencies, natural disasters and climate-related events could disrupt our operations.

Pandemics, severe weather, wildfires, floods and other catastrophic events could damage our facilities or interrupt transportation networks. They could also disrupt clinical trials, reduce elective care volumes or cause sudden shifts in demand for particular medicines. Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of such events. Our distribution network and temperature-controlled operations are particularly vulnerable to prolonged power outages and transportation disruptions.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to factors such as:

clinical trial results and regulatory decisions; changes in drug pricing policy; litigation developments; quarterly variations in our operating results; and changes in analyst expectations and broader market conditions.

Many of these factors are outside our control. Volatility in our stock price could also affect our ability to use our equity in acquisitions and as a component of employee compensation.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could delay or prevent a change in control.

Certain provisions of the Delaware General Corporation Law, our certificate of incorporation and our bylaws could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us, even if a transaction would benefit our stockholders. These include the ability of our board of directors to issue preferred stock without stockholder approval and advance notice requirements for stockholder proposals. These provisions could limit the price that investors might be willing to pay for our common stock.
