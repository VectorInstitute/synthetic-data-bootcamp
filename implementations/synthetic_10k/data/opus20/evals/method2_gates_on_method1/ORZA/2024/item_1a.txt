Item 1A. Risk Factors

An investment in our securities involves risk. The risks described below are those that we currently believe could, individually or together, have a material adverse effect on our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows, liquidity or the trading price of our common stock. Additional risks that we do not presently know of, or that we currently regard as immaterial, may also impair our business. The order in which the risks appear, and the captions under which they are grouped, do not reflect their relative likelihood or the magnitude of their potential effects. You should read these risks together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 7, Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations, and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8.

RISKS RELATED TO OUR BUSINESS AND INDUSTRY

Our results depend on the volumes of hydrocarbons that producers, refiners and shippers deliver to our systems, and those volumes are influenced by factors we do not control.

The throughput on our gathering systems, processing plants, pipelines, storage facilities and terminals depends on drilling and completion activity in the basins we serve, refinery utilization, petrochemical demand and export flows. Producers set their capital budgets based on commodity prices, access to financing, investor expectations about capital discipline, well productivity and the cost of oilfield services. A sustained decline in crude oil, natural gas or NGL prices, or a shift by producers toward returning capital rather than drilling, could reduce new well connections and allow natural production declines to outpace new supply. As a result, volumes on our systems could fall. Even where our contracts include minimum volume commitments or acreage dedications, these protections expire over time, may be renegotiated in a downturn and do not apply to all of our assets.

Lower volumes would reduce fee revenue in our Gathering and Processing segment. Because much of the gas we process flows onward into our own transportation, fractionation and storage assets, a volume shortfall at the wellhead can also lower utilization and revenue in our Transportation and Storage and Terminals and Marketing Services segments. Our cost of revenue and other operating expenses include a substantial fixed component, such as pipeline integrity, compression, labor and property taxes, that does not decline in proportion to throughput. Lower volumes could therefore reduce our margins by more than they reduce our revenue.

A portion of our earnings is directly exposed to commodity prices, and our hedging activities may not fully protect us.

Although we structure most of our agreements on a fee basis, certain percent-of-proceeds processing arrangements allow us to retain a share of the residue gas and NGLs we process. As a result, our earnings under those arrangements fall when commodity prices or processing spreads decline. Our marketing activities purchase and resell crude oil, NGLs, natural gas and refined products, and their results depend on location and time spreads that can narrow or reverse quickly. Because marketing revenues are recorded on a gross basis, changes in commodity prices can cause large swings in reported revenue and cost of revenue. Those swings can make our results harder to compare from period to period.

We use derivative instruments to reduce some of this exposure. However, our hedges cover only a portion of our expected volumes and periods, and they may be ineffective because of basis differences between the hedged index and our actual sales points. Hedges can also require us to post collateral when prices move against our positions, which could strain liquidity during periods of market volatility. In addition, our counterparties may fail to perform.

We depend on a limited number of large customers in certain segments, and their financial difficulty or loss of their business could materially affect our results.

Several large producers, refiners, utilities and exporters account for a meaningful share of revenue in individual segments. If one of these customers reduced its volumes, did not renew its contracts, sought to reject its agreements in a bankruptcy proceeding or failed to pay amounts owed, the affected segment's results could decline materially. Producer bankruptcies in prior industry downturns resulted in attempts to reject gathering and processing agreements, and courts have reached differing conclusions on whether acreage dedications survive such proceedings. Our credit support requirements, such as letters of credit, guarantees and prepayments, may be insufficient to cover our losses. Our accounts receivable have grown along with our revenue, which increases the amount at risk if a customer defaults.

We may be unable to renew or replace expiring contracts on comparable terms.

Our firm transportation, storage, gathering and terminalling agreements have finite terms. When they expire, customers may seek lower rates, shorter terms, reduced commitments or more flexible service, particularly where competing infrastructure has been built or where supply and demand patterns have shifted. If we cannot recontract our capacity at rates and terms similar to those we receive today, or if capacity remains unsubscribed, our revenue and cash flows could decline. Expiring reservation charges would also reduce the predictability of our cash flows that our contract structure is designed to provide.

We face substantial competition across our segments.

We compete with other midstream companies, integrated energy companies, producer-owned gathering systems, private equity-backed developers and, in some markets, rail and truck transportation. Competitors may have greater financial resources, lower costs of capital, more favorable asset locations or the willingness to accept lower returns to secure volumes. New pipeline or processing capacity built by competitors in basins or corridors we serve could divert volumes from our systems and put pressure on our rates when contracts come up for renewal. Customers increasingly can choose among several service providers. If we cannot keep our unit costs competitive, we may lose new business to those competitors.

Long-term changes in demand for hydrocarbons could reduce the use and value of our assets.

Our assets are long-lived and were sized and constructed in anticipation of decades of service. Policies that favor electrification, renewable power, energy efficiency and alternative fuels, together with technological advances and changing consumer preferences, could reduce long-term demand for crude oil, refined products, natural gas and NGLs in the markets we serve. Lower demand could lead to reduced throughput, shorter contract terms, lower rates or the early retirement of assets. It could also impair the carrying value of our property, plant and equipment and goodwill. Some investors and lenders have reduced their exposure to the fossil fuel sector, and further such actions could raise our cost of capital. The timing and extent of the energy transition are uncertain, and our planning assumptions may prove incorrect.

Our investments in lower-carbon businesses may not achieve expected returns.

We are evaluating and pursuing opportunities in carbon dioxide transportation and sequestration, renewable feedstock and fuel logistics, and hydrogen handling. These businesses depend heavily on government incentives, evolving regulatory frameworks, unproven commercial structures and technologies that have not been deployed at scale. Carbon dioxide pipelines in particular have faced significant public opposition and permitting challenges in several states. If incentives are reduced or repealed, if customer demand develops more slowly than expected, or if we cannot obtain permits and rights-of-way, we may be unable to recover our investments in these opportunities.

Acquisitions may not perform as expected and could result in goodwill or asset impairments.

We have grown in part through acquisitions, and goodwill represents a significant portion of our total assets. Acquired businesses may not achieve the volumes, cost savings or commercial integration we anticipated. We may assume liabilities, including environmental, safety, tax or litigation liabilities, that we did not identify during due diligence. Integrating acquired assets, systems and personnel also requires significant management attention and may disrupt our existing operations. If the performance of an acquired business or a reporting unit deteriorates, or if market conditions or discount rates change unfavorably, we may be required to record impairment charges. Such charges could be material to our results of operations and stockholders' equity.

Our growth projects are subject to construction, cost and schedule risks.

We invest substantial capital in expansions, new pipelines, processing plants, fractionators and terminals. These projects require federal, state and local permits, rights-of-way, specialized labor and long-lead equipment such as compressors, pipe and cryogenic components. Delays in permitting, litigation by project opponents, adverse weather, contractor performance problems, supply chain disruptions, tariffs on imported steel and inflation in labor and materials costs could cause cost overruns or delays. Such problems could also cause us to abandon projects after significant expenditures have been made. In many cases, our commercial agreements fix the rates we may charge before construction is complete. As a result, cost overruns may reduce our returns, and delays may defer revenue while our financing costs continue to accrue.

RISKS RELATED TO OPERATIONS

Our operations involve hazards that could result in injury, loss of life, environmental damage and significant liabilities, and our insurance may not cover all losses.

Gathering, processing, fractionating, transporting, storing and handling hydrocarbons involves inherent risks. These include pipeline ruptures, leaks, fires, explosions, releases of toxic gases such as hydrogen sulfide, cavern instability, equipment failure, vessel incidents at our docks and third-party damage from excavation. Any of these events could cause personal injury or death, property damage, environmental contamination, evacuation of nearby communities and the suspension of operations. They could also expose us to government investigations, fines, remediation obligations and litigation. Incidents involving our assets or the assets of other operators may also lead to more stringent regulation and damage our reputation with regulators, communities and customers.

We maintain insurance at levels we believe are customary for our industry. However, our policies contain deductibles, self-insured retentions, sublimits and exclusions, and certain risks, such as some environmental and cyber-related losses, may be uninsurable or available only at prohibitive cost. Conditions in the insurance market have at times reduced available capacity and raised premiums for energy infrastructure operators. An uninsured or underinsured loss could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition and results of operations.

Severe weather and changing climate conditions could damage our assets and disrupt our operations.

Many of our assets are located in regions exposed to hurricanes, flooding, extreme heat, winter storms, wildfires and drought, including Gulf Coast fractionation, storage and marine terminal facilities. Severe weather events can damage facilities and interrupt power supplies, and they can force shutdowns of producer, refinery and petrochemical operations that supply or receive our volumes. Extreme cold can cause wellhead freeze-offs that sharply reduce gathered volumes, while elevated temperatures can reduce the capacity of compression and processing equipment. Over time, more frequent or intense weather events could raise our repair, adaptation and insurance costs and reduce our revenue.

A cyberattack or failure of our technology systems could disrupt our operations and harm our business.

We rely on industrial control systems, supervisory control and data acquisition systems, centralized control rooms and enterprise information technology to operate our assets, schedule shipments, measure volumes and bill customers. Our growing use of centralized operations and predictive maintenance increases our dependence on these systems. Energy infrastructure has been targeted by nation-state actors, criminal organizations and ransomware groups. A successful attack, or a failure of systems maintained by us or our vendors, could disrupt pipeline operations, cause physical damage or safety incidents, compromise confidential information and delay invoicing and collections. Such an event could also subject us to regulatory penalties and litigation. Federal security directives applicable to pipeline operators impose additional compliance requirements, and our security measures may not prevent or detect every intrusion.

Aging infrastructure and expanding integrity requirements could increase our costs.

Portions of our pipeline and facility network were constructed decades ago. Maintaining the integrity of older assets requires ongoing inline inspection, repair, replacement and cathodic protection. Inspections may reveal conditions that require unplanned capital expenditures, pressure reductions or temporary shutdowns. If our maintenance costs rise faster than we can recover them through rates or contract escalators, our margins would decline.

We depend on third-party infrastructure, suppliers and service providers.

Our operations rely on interconnecting pipelines, fractionators, refineries, export terminals, rail carriers and electric utilities owned by others. Outages, capacity constraints or curtailments at these facilities could prevent us from receiving or delivering volumes. We also depend on a limited number of suppliers for key equipment and on contractors for construction and maintenance services. Supply chain disruptions, trade restrictions, tariffs or the financial failure of a key supplier could delay projects, raise costs or impair our ability to maintain safe operations.

Our success depends on attracting and retaining a skilled workforce.

We employ a large workforce that includes engineers, control room operators, field technicians and commercial personnel with specialized knowledge. Competition for qualified personnel, the retirement of experienced employees, wage inflation and changes in labor relations could increase our costs or impair our operations. The loss of key members of senior management could also adversely affect the execution of our strategy.

RISKS RELATED TO FINANCING AND CAPITAL STRUCTURE

Our substantial indebtedness could limit our flexibility and adversely affect our financial condition.

We have a significant amount of long-term debt, which exceeds our total stockholders' equity, and our borrowings increased during the year to fund acquisitions, capital projects and returns of capital. Interest expense absorbs a meaningful portion of our operating income. Our level of indebtedness could:

require us to dedicate a substantial portion of operating cash flow to interest and principal payments; reduce our flexibility to respond to industry downturns, competitive pressures or investment opportunities; make it more difficult or more costly to obtain additional financing; place us at a disadvantage relative to less leveraged competitors; and increase our vulnerability to rising interest rates.

If our cash flows decline, we may be unable to service our debt without reducing capital spending, selling assets, issuing equity or reducing dividends and share repurchases.

Changes in interest rates and conditions in credit markets could increase our borrowing costs and affect our ability to refinance maturing debt.

We regularly access the debt capital markets to refinance maturities and fund growth. Higher benchmark interest rates or wider credit spreads for energy issuers would raise the cost of new borrowings and of any floating-rate debt, including amounts drawn under our credit facilities and commercial paper program. Disruptions in the financial markets, or reduced investor appetite for midstream or fossil fuel-related debt, could restrict our access to capital when we need it. Higher interest rates may also reduce the valuation multiples that investors assign to infrastructure assets, which could affect the market price of our common stock.

A downgrade of our credit ratings could increase our costs and reduce our access to capital.

Our credit ratings reflect the rating agencies' assessment of our leverage, business risk, contract quality and financial policies. Increases in our debt, debt-funded acquisitions, weaker cash flows or a more aggressive approach to shareholder returns could lead the rating agencies to lower our ratings. A downgrade could increase our interest costs, limit our access to the commercial paper market and trigger collateral requirements under commercial and derivative agreements. It could also cause some counterparties to require additional credit support before transacting with us.

Our current liabilities exceed our current assets, which increases our reliance on operating cash flow and external financing.

We operate with a working capital deficit, in part because current maturities of long-term debt, commercial paper and commodity purchase obligations in our marketing business are classified as current liabilities. We depend on continued operating cash flow, access to our revolving credit facilities and the capital markets to meet these obligations as they come due. If our cash flows weaken or our access to financing becomes constrained at a time when significant obligations mature, our liquidity could be materially impaired.

Our capital spending and shareholder returns have exceeded our operating cash flow, and we may need to reduce one or more of them.

During the year, the combined amount of our capital expenditures, acquisitions and other investing activities, dividends and share repurchases exceeded our cash flow from operations, and we financed the difference largely with additional debt. This approach cannot continue indefinitely without increasing our leverage. If our operating cash flows fall short of expectations, or if borrowing becomes more expensive or less available, our board of directors may reduce or suspend dividends or share repurchases, or we may defer growth projects. Any of these actions could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

Our debt agreements contain covenants that restrict our activities.

Our credit facilities and indentures contain covenants that limit, among other things, our leverage, the incurrence of liens and our ability to enter into certain mergers or sales of assets. These restrictions may prevent us from pursuing opportunities that we believe would benefit our business. A breach of these covenants could result in an event of default, allowing lenders to accelerate our indebtedness. Cross-default provisions could then accelerate other debt.

RISKS RELATED TO LEGAL AND REGULATORY MATTERS

Rate regulation of our pipelines and storage facilities could limit the revenue we earn.

Our interstate natural gas pipelines and storage facilities are subject to regulation by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, and our interstate liquids pipelines are subject to its oversight of tariffs and terms of service. Certain of our intrastate assets are regulated by state commissions. Regulators may challenge our rates, change the methodologies used to set allowable returns or the treatment of income taxes in cost-of-service rates, or revise the index used to adjust liquids pipeline rates. Shippers may also file complaints seeking rate reductions or refunds. Adverse regulatory outcomes could reduce our revenue, require refunds and limit our ability to recover costs. Gathering assets that are not currently subject to rate regulation could become regulated in the future.

Pipeline safety laws and regulations could impose significant compliance costs and operating restrictions.

The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration and state agencies regulate the design, construction, operation, inspection and maintenance of our pipelines and storage facilities. Recent and proposed rules have expanded requirements for gas gathering lines, valve installation, leak detection, repair criteria and integrity management. Additional requirements could raise our operating and capital costs. They could also require pressure reductions or replacements that limit throughput, and violations could result in substantial civil penalties or orders to shut down affected facilities.

Environmental laws and regulations, including those addressing greenhouse gas emissions, could increase our costs and reduce demand for our services.

Our operations are subject to extensive federal, state and local laws governing air emissions, water discharges, waste management, the protection of endangered species and the remediation of contaminated properties. Some of these laws impose strict, joint and several liability without regard to fault. Regulation of methane and other greenhouse gas emissions from oil and gas facilities has expanded, including leak detection and repair requirements, equipment standards and federal charges on certain methane emissions. Additional measures, such as carbon pricing, emissions caps or restrictions on the use of natural gas in buildings and power generation, could raise our compliance costs. They could also reduce the volumes produced and consumed in the markets we serve. Future changes in administration and policy priorities could alter these requirements in ways that are difficult to predict.

We may be unable to obtain or maintain permits, rights-of-way and land access on acceptable terms.

Constructing and operating our assets requires permits from numerous agencies and easements from landowners, tribal nations and government entities. Project opponents increasingly use litigation, administrative challenges and public campaigns to delay or block energy infrastructure. Courts have invalidated permits for projects that were already under construction or in service. Some of our rights-of-way are subject to renewal or expiration, and certain states have limited the use of eminent domain for pipelines. The loss of permits or rights-of-way, or the inability to obtain them, could prevent us from completing projects, require us to reroute or remove existing facilities and result in significant costs or lost revenue.

We are subject to litigation and regulatory proceedings that could result in significant liabilities.

In the ordinary course of business, we are involved in lawsuits, regulatory investigations and enforcement actions involving contract disputes, personal injury, property damage, environmental matters, royalty and landowner claims, and employment matters. Climate-related lawsuits brought by governmental entities and private parties against energy companies have increased. The outcome of these matters is inherently uncertain, and adverse judgments, settlements or penalties could be material.

Changes in tax laws or their interpretation could increase our tax burden.

Our effective tax rate depends on federal and state tax laws, the geographic distribution of our income and the availability of credits and deductions. Legislative changes to corporate tax rates, the corporate alternative minimum tax, bonus depreciation, interest deductibility or energy-related tax credits, or increases in state income or property taxes, could raise our tax expense and reduce our cash flows. Changes to tax incentives for carbon capture, clean hydrogen or renewable fuels could also affect the economics of our energy transition investments.

Evolving sustainability disclosure requirements and stakeholder expectations could increase our costs and expose us to reputational and legal risks.

Federal, state and international authorities have adopted or proposed rules requiring disclosure of greenhouse gas emissions and climate-related risks, and some of these rules are subject to ongoing legal challenges. Complying with these requirements may require substantial resources. At the same time, we face opposing pressures from stakeholders who want us to accelerate decarbonization and from those who oppose such efforts. Statements we make about sustainability goals depend on assumptions and data that may change. Allegations that our disclosures are inaccurate or misleading could lead to litigation, enforcement actions or reputational harm.

Regulation of derivatives and commodity markets could increase the cost of our hedging and marketing activities.

Federal regulation of over-the-counter derivatives, position limits and market conduct applies to our hedging and marketing activities. Changes to margin, clearing or reporting requirements could increase our costs, reduce the availability of hedging instruments or require us to post additional collateral. Alleged violations of market manipulation rules could result in significant penalties.

GENERAL RISKS

Adverse economic conditions, inflation and geopolitical events could reduce demand for our services and increase our costs.

A recession, financial crisis, pandemic, armed conflict, trade dispute or sanctions regime could reduce energy demand, disrupt export markets, raise commodity price volatility and impair the financial condition of our customers. Inflation in labor, materials, power and services could increase our operating and capital costs faster than contractual escalators allow us to recover them. Changes in trade policy affecting exports of liquefied natural gas, NGLs or crude oil could reduce the volumes moving through our export-oriented assets.

Impairments of long-lived assets or goodwill could reduce our earnings and stockholders' equity.

Property, plant and equipment and goodwill together account for the substantial majority of our total assets. We evaluate these assets for impairment when events or circumstances indicate their carrying amounts may not be recoverable, and we test goodwill at least annually. These evaluations rely on estimates of future volumes, rates, commodity prices, costs, asset lives and discount rates. Declines in basin activity, contract losses, regulatory changes or higher interest rates could cause us to record non-cash impairment charges, which could be material.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile, and future dividends and share repurchases are not assured.

The price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to commodity prices, interest rates, our operating results, changes in our credit ratings, investor sentiment toward the energy sector and broader market conditions. Our board of directors determines dividends and share repurchases in its discretion. It may reduce or eliminate them in light of our financial condition, capital requirements, debt covenants and other factors.

Provisions of Delaware law and our organizational documents could delay or prevent a change in control.

Our certificate of incorporation, bylaws and Delaware law contain provisions that could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us without the approval of our board of directors. These provisions include the board's ability to issue preferred stock and restrictions on certain business combinations with interested stockholders. They could discourage transactions that some stockholders may consider favorable and could limit the price that investors are willing to pay for our common stock.

Our estimates and judgments in applying accounting policies may prove incorrect.

The preparation of our financial statements requires estimates and assumptions concerning revenue recognition under complex contracts, asset retirement obligations, environmental liabilities, the fair value of derivatives and acquired assets, and the useful lives of our assets. Actual results may differ from these estimates. Changes in accounting standards or in the interpretation of existing standards could also require us to restate or change the presentation of our results.
