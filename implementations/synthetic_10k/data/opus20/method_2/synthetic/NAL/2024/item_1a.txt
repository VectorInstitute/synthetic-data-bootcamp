# ITEM 1A. RISK FACTORS

An investment in our securities involves a high degree of risk. You should carefully consider the risks and uncertainties described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. That information includes Item 1, "Business," Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," Item 7A, "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk," and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8.

The risks described below are not the only risks we face. Risks and uncertainties that we do not currently know about, or that we currently believe are immaterial, may also materially and adversely affect us. If any of the following risks actually occurs, it could materially and adversely affect any or all of the following:

- our business;
- our financial condition;
- our results of operations;
- our cash flows;
- our prospects.

In that event, the trading price of our common stock could decline, and you could lose all or part of your investment. Many of the risks described below are interrelated, and the occurrence of one risk may increase the likelihood or severity of others. The order in which these risks are presented does not necessarily reflect their relative likelihood or the magnitude of their potential effects.

Unless the context indicates otherwise, the terms "Amberwood," the "Company," "we," "us," and "our" refer to Amberwood Partners Co. and its consolidated subsidiaries.

## Summary of Risk Factors

The following is a summary of the principal risks that could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations. This summary is qualified in its entirety by the more complete discussion of each risk that follows.

**Macroeconomic and Consumer Demand Risks**

- Unfavorable economic conditions, reduced consumer discretionary spending, and changes in consumer confidence could reduce guest traffic and check average across our brands.
- Our fine dining brand, Ironbark Chophouse, is particularly sensitive to corporate entertainment spending and to the spending of higher-income households.
- We may be unable to offset inflation in our cost structure through menu pricing without reducing guest traffic or harming perceptions of value.
- Changes in consumer preferences, dining patterns, and health and wellness trends could reduce demand for our menu offerings.
- We face intense competition for guests, team members, and restaurant sites.
- Our significant presence in Florida and the southeastern United States exposes us to regional economic, regulatory, tourism, and weather-related risks.

**Operational Risks**

- Food safety incidents, foodborne illness, or concerns about food quality could harm our reputation and reduce sales.
- Increases in labor costs, including those driven by minimum wage, tip credit, benefit, and scheduling laws, could reduce our margins.
- Our ability to recruit, train, and retain qualified team members and restaurant leaders is critical to our operations.
- Volatility in commodity prices could adversely affect our margins. We generally do not hedge commodity prices with financial instruments.
- Disruptions in our supply chain or distribution network, or the loss of key suppliers, could interrupt restaurant operations.
- Hurricanes, tropical storms, flooding, and other severe weather events, which may become more frequent or severe as a result of climate change, could cause restaurant closures, property damage, and supply disruptions.
- Increases in insurance costs, limits on coverage availability, and our retention of significant self-insured losses could adversely affect our results.
- Our reliance on third-party delivery service providers exposes us to commission costs, service quality risks, and loss of guest relationships.

**Brand, Strategy, and Growth Risks**

- Damage to the reputation of any of our brands, including through social media, could reduce sales across our portfolio.
- Managing a multi-brand portfolio is complex, and one or more brands may underperform.
- New restaurant development, remodels, and relocations involve substantial risks and may not achieve expected returns.
- Our emerging brands, including Daybreak Porch, may not achieve the operating performance necessary to support continued investment.
- We may be required to record impairment charges related to long-lived assets, operating lease right-of-use assets, goodwill, or other intangible assets.

**Franchising, Licensing, and International Risks**

- Our franchising and licensing activities depend on the performance and conduct of independent third parties over whom we have limited control.
- Our international franchise operations expose us to foreign political, economic, legal, and currency risks.

**Technology, Cybersecurity, and Privacy Risks**

- A cybersecurity incident or data breach could result in liability, regulatory penalties, operational disruption, and reputational harm.
- Failures or interruptions in our information technology systems, or those of our third-party vendors, could disrupt our operations.
- Evolving privacy and data protection laws could increase our compliance costs and restrict our marketing and loyalty activities.

**Legal and Regulatory Risks**

- We are subject to extensive and evolving laws and regulations, and compliance failures could result in penalties, closures, or loss of licenses.
- We are subject to litigation, including wage-and-hour and other employment-related class and collective actions, premises liability claims, and consumer claims.
- Our sale of alcoholic beverages subjects us to licensing requirements and to "dram shop" liability.

**Financial Condition and Capital Structure Risks**

- Our liabilities are substantial relative to our assets and stockholders' equity, which could limit our financial flexibility and increase our vulnerability to adverse developments.
- The agreements governing our indebtedness may restrict our operations, and changes in interest rates or credit market conditions could increase our borrowing costs.
- Our long-term lease obligations are largely fixed and could become burdensome if restaurant performance declines.

**Risks Related to Our Common Stock**

- The market price of our common stock may be volatile, and provisions of our governing documents and Florida law could discourage a change in control.

## Risks Related to Macroeconomic Conditions and Consumer Demand

**Unfavorable economic conditions and reduced consumer discretionary spending could adversely affect guest traffic, check average, and our results of operations.**

The restaurant industry is part of the Consumer Discretionary sector. Dining away from home is a discretionary purchase that consumers can reduce, defer, or replace with lower-cost alternatives when their financial circumstances or outlook deteriorate. Our sales depend on the willingness and ability of guests to spend on restaurant meals. Many factors outside our control influence that spending, including:

- general economic conditions and the risk of recession;
- employment levels and wage growth;
- consumer confidence;
- inflation, particularly in food, housing, energy, and other essentials;
- interest rates and the cost and availability of consumer credit;
- household debt levels;
- the value of homes, investments, and retirement savings;
- tax rates and government stimulus or benefit programs;
- fuel prices.

During periods of economic weakness or uncertainty, guests may visit restaurants less frequently, choose less expensive restaurants or menu items, order fewer appetizers, desserts, and alcoholic beverages, or substitute meals prepared at home. These behaviors could reduce both traffic and check average at our restaurants. Our full-service brands, which generally carry higher price points than quick-service and many fast-casual competitors, may be more exposed to trade-down behavior than lower-priced concepts.

Because a substantial portion of our restaurant-level costs, including occupancy, management salaries, depreciation, and a portion of hourly labor, are fixed or semi-fixed in the short term, declines in sales can have a disproportionately negative effect on our operating margins. A prolonged economic downturn could also impair the financial condition of our franchisees, landlords, suppliers, and distributors, which could further disrupt our operations. In addition, weak economic conditions could require us to slow new restaurant development, defer remodels, close underperforming restaurants, or record impairment charges, any of which could adversely affect our results of operations and growth prospects.

**Ironbark Chophouse is particularly sensitive to corporate entertainment spending and to the discretionary spending of higher-income households, which may be volatile.**

Ironbark Chophouse, which comprises our Fine Dining segment, targets special-occasion dining, business entertaining, and guests who seek an elevated experience. Its private dining business, which is an important component of the brand's sales, depends heavily on corporate events, client entertainment, and holiday party activity. Corporate entertainment budgets are often among the first expenses that businesses reduce when they face economic uncertainty, declining profits, or changes in their policies on travel and entertainment. Changes in tax laws affecting the deductibility of business meals, shifts toward remote or hybrid work that reduce in-person business meetings, and heightened corporate cost discipline could each reduce demand for Ironbark's dining rooms and private dining spaces.

Ironbark's results also depend on the spending of higher-income households. Their spending can be affected by declines in equity markets, real estate values, and other asset prices, even when broader economic indicators remain stable. Because Ironbark's check average is substantially higher than that of our other brands, reductions in its traffic can have an outsized effect on its segment profitability. Ironbark also requires a more specialized workforce, including experienced culinary and wine professionals, and carries significant wine inventories. Each of these characteristics makes it more difficult to reduce costs quickly in response to declining sales. Any significant or sustained decline in Ironbark's performance could adversely affect our consolidated results of operations.

**We may be unable to offset inflation in our costs through menu price increases without reducing guest traffic or damaging guest perceptions of value.**

We have experienced, and may continue to experience, inflation in many components of our cost structure, including food and beverage costs, wages and benefits, occupancy costs, utilities, insurance, packaging, construction, and technology. Our ability to maintain operating margins depends in part on our ability to raise menu prices to offset these increases.

Menu price increases involve significant risks. As described in Item 1, our pricing strategy considers guest sensitivity to price changes and the importance of protecting traffic over the long term. Guests who perceive that our prices have risen faster than the quality of the experience may visit less frequently, shift to lower-priced menu items, or choose competitors. Competitors may hold prices steady or engage in aggressive discounting, making our price increases more visible. Guests increasingly compare prices across restaurants and channels through digital platforms, and menu prices on third-party delivery marketplaces, which may be higher than in-restaurant prices, could contribute to perceptions that our brands are expensive.

If we raise prices too aggressively, we risk losing traffic and eroding the brand equity that supports our long-term pricing power. If we raise prices too slowly or too little, we may experience margin compression. In either case, our results of operations could be adversely affected. In addition, we generally favor everyday value over deep discounting. If competitive conditions require us to increase promotional activity, our margins could decline and guests could become conditioned to wait for promotions, as discussed in Item 1.

**Changes in consumer preferences, dining patterns, and health and wellness trends could reduce demand for our offerings.**

Our success depends on our ability to anticipate, identify, and respond to changing consumer preferences, tastes, and eating habits. Consumer preferences can shift quickly and unpredictably in response to many factors, including:

- health and nutrition concerns, including concerns about sodium, sugar, fat, red meat, alcohol, or processed foods;
- the growing use of medications and other treatments that suppress appetite or alter food and beverage consumption;
- dietary trends, including plant-forward, low-carbohydrate, and allergen-free diets;
- concerns about animal welfare, sustainability, and the environmental footprint of food production;
- generational differences in dining habits and in the consumption of alcoholic beverages;
- demand for convenience, speed, and off-premise options;
- publicity concerning the health effects of particular foods or ingredients.

Several of our brands are built around menu categories that could be affected by these trends. Amberwood Grill and Ironbark Chophouse feature steaks and other red meat. Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen depends on consumer confidence in seafood quality and sustainability. Our full-service brands generate meaningful revenue from alcoholic beverages, and a decline in alcohol consumption, whether driven by changing social attitudes, health guidance, or other factors, could reduce check average and beverage margins. Although we offer an expanding selection of non-alcoholic and low-alcohol beverages, those products may not fully offset lost alcohol sales.

Shifts in dining patterns could also reduce demand for the dine-in experiences that define our full-service brands. Off-premise sales generally carry different margin profiles than dine-in sales and may not fully replace lost dine-in revenue. If we fail to adapt our menus, service models, restaurant formats, and marketing to changing preferences, or if we adapt in ways that do not resonate with guests, our sales and results of operations could decline.

**We face intense competition, which could reduce our sales, margins, and market share.**

The restaurant industry is intensely competitive, fragmented, and characterized by relatively low barriers to entry. As described in Item 1, our brands compete with national and regional restaurant chains, locally owned independent restaurants, fast-casual and quick-service restaurants, delivery-only and virtual restaurant concepts, grocery and convenience stores, meal-kit providers, and other providers of food away from home. We compete on the basis of food quality, variety, and value; service; atmosphere; convenience; location; brand recognition; and digital capabilities.

Many of our competitors have greater financial resources, longer operating histories, or greater brand recognition in particular markets. Some competitors may be willing to accept lower margins or operate with lower cost structures, allowing them to offer lower prices or engage in more aggressive promotions. Competitors with superior digital capabilities, loyalty programs, or data analytics may be able to attract and retain guests more effectively than we can. Grocery and convenience retailers continue to expand their prepared-food offerings, increasingly competing for meal occasions that historically went to restaurants.

Competition also extends to team members and real estate. We compete with restaurants, retailers, and employers in other industries for hourly workers and experienced managers, and with other retailers and developers for attractive restaurant sites. Increased competition in any of these areas could require us to increase wages, marketing expenditures, or occupancy costs, or to reduce prices, any of which could adversely affect our margins. If we are unable to compete effectively, our sales, market share, and results of operations could decline.

**Our significant presence in Florida and the southeastern United States exposes us to regional economic, regulatory, tourism, and weather-related risks.**

Our headquarters is located in Florida, and we maintain a significant concentration of restaurants in the southeastern United States. As a result, our results of operations are more sensitive than those of a more geographically diversified company to conditions in that region, including:

- regional economic conditions and housing markets;
- population and migration trends;
- the cost and availability of property insurance;
- changes in state and local laws, including minimum wage, tax, and employment laws;
- competitive conditions in key markets;
- hurricanes, tropical storms, flooding, and extreme heat.

Certain of our restaurants are located in markets with significant tourist activity. Sales in those markets may fluctuate with seasonal travel patterns and can be affected by factors that reduce tourism, including economic downturns, changes in airfare or fuel prices, public health concerns, environmental events affecting coastal areas, adverse publicity about a destination, and the aftermath of severe weather. Rising homeowners' and commercial insurance costs in the region could also reduce the disposable income of local residents and increase our own occupancy and insurance costs. A significant adverse development affecting Florida or the broader Southeast could have a disproportionate effect on our consolidated results.

## Risks Related to Our Operations

**Food safety incidents, foodborne illness, or concerns about food quality could harm our reputation, reduce sales, and expose us to liability.**

Food safety is a top priority for Amberwood, and we maintain extensive food safety and quality assurance programs, as described in Item 1. Nonetheless, foodborne illness and food safety incidents are an inherent risk of the restaurant business, and we cannot guarantee that our programs will prevent all such incidents. Food safety issues can arise from many sources, including:

- contamination of ingredients by bacteria, viruses, parasites, or other pathogens at any point in the supply chain;
- improper food handling, storage, or preparation by team members;
- team members or guests who are ill;
- equipment failures, including refrigeration failures;
- cross-contact with allergens;
- tampering or other intentional acts.

Several of our menu categories carry heightened food safety risk. Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen serves raw oysters and other shellfish, which can carry naturally occurring pathogens even when properly handled. Our brands also serve fresh produce, proteins cooked to guest-requested temperatures, and scratch or near-scratch preparations involving significant hand handling. Because our kitchens handle common allergens, we cannot guarantee that any menu item is free of allergens, and allergic reactions could result in serious harm to guests and in claims against us.

A food safety incident at one restaurant, or a series of incidents, could result in temporary restaurant closures, product holds and withdrawals, regulatory investigations, fines, litigation, and significant remediation costs. Adverse publicity about an incident, whether or not it is accurate, could spread rapidly through social media and news outlets and could reduce sales not only at the affected restaurant but across the affected brand and potentially across our portfolio, given our shared supply chain and corporate identity. Incidents at franchised restaurants, at our licensees' consumer products manufacturers, or at competitors' restaurants, or industry-wide concerns about a particular ingredient, could also reduce guest confidence and demand. Recovering from a significant food safety incident could take an extended period and require substantial marketing investment. Our insurance may not cover, or may not fully cover, the losses associated with such an incident.

**Increases in labor costs could adversely affect our margins and results of operations.**

Labor is one of our largest operating costs. A substantial majority of our team members are hourly restaurant employees, and many are paid at rates related to applicable minimum wages. Our labor costs are affected by many factors, including:

- increases in federal, state, or local minimum wages, including scheduled increases in several states and localities where we operate;
- changes to or the elimination of tip credits, or changes to rules governing tip pooling and service charges;
- competitive wage pressure in tight labor markets;
- predictive scheduling and fair workweek laws;
- paid sick leave, family leave, and other mandated benefits;
- health care costs, including costs under our self-insured health plans;
- workers' compensation costs;
- overtime rules and changes to the classification of employees as exempt or non-exempt;
- payroll taxes;
- the costs of training new team members as a result of turnover.

Changes to tip credit rules are of particular concern to our full-service brands, where tipped positions such as servers and bartenders make up a meaningful portion of the workforce. The elimination or reduction of tip credits in jurisdictions where we operate could significantly increase our labor costs. Increases in the minimum wage also tend to create upward pressure on wages for team members paid above the minimum, as we seek to maintain pay differentials based on experience and responsibility.

Our managing partner program provides eligible general managers with a share of restaurant cash flow or profits. While we believe this program aligns incentives and promotes tenure, it also represents a significant compensation cost that may not decline proportionately with restaurant performance in all circumstances. If we cannot offset increases in labor costs through menu pricing, productivity improvements, or other operating efficiencies, our margins could decline. If we raise menu prices to offset labor cost increases, guest traffic could decline, as described above.

**Our success depends on our ability to recruit, train, motivate, and retain qualified team members and restaurant leaders.**

The guest experience in our restaurants is delivered by people. Our success depends on our ability to attract, develop, and retain sufficient numbers of qualified hourly team members, restaurant managers, chefs, and field leaders. Team member turnover is inherently high in the restaurant industry, particularly among hourly team members. Shortages of qualified workers, whether caused by low unemployment, demographic trends, changes in immigration policy and enforcement, competition from other industries, or other factors, could require us to increase wages and benefits, increase recruiting and training expenditures, or operate with reduced staffing.

Insufficient staffing can lead to longer wait times, reduced operating hours, closed sections of dining rooms, inconsistent food quality, and diminished hospitality, any of which could harm guest satisfaction and sales. Ironbark Chophouse depends on a specialized workforce, including experienced culinary professionals, sommeliers, and wine stewards, whose skills are in limited supply. New restaurant openings require us to draw experienced managers and trainers from existing restaurants. A shortage of qualified leaders could delay openings or weaken operations at the restaurants from which they are drawn.

Our managing partner program and other incentive programs are designed to promote tenure among our most important field leaders. However, these programs may not be sufficient to retain high-performing managers, particularly if competitors offer more attractive compensation or if restaurant performance declines and reduces the value of profit-sharing arrangements. In addition, the multi-year commitments and other conditions associated with certain managing partner arrangements may, in some circumstances, give rise to disputes. If we are unable to recruit, develop, and retain qualified team members and leaders, our operations, growth, and results of operations could be adversely affected.

**Union organizing activity or changes in labor law could increase our costs and disrupt our operations.**

Our team members are not covered by collective bargaining agreements, and we believe that our relationships with our team members are good. However, union organizing activity in the restaurant and retail industries has increased in recent years, and our team members may seek to organize in the future. Changes in federal labor law, or in the interpretation and enforcement of existing law by the National Labor Relations Board or the courts, could make it easier for unions to organize. Examples include changes affecting election procedures, employer communications, or standards for recognizing bargaining units.

If some or all of our team members were to become unionized, we could experience higher labor costs, more restrictive work rules, reduced flexibility in scheduling and staffing, and work stoppages or other disruptions. Responding to organizing efforts could divert management attention and result in increased legal and other costs. Any of these developments could adversely affect our business and results of operations. In addition, unionization or labor disputes at our suppliers, distributors, or delivery partners could disrupt our supply chain or off-premise business.

**The loss of key personnel or ineffective succession planning could adversely affect our business.**

Our success depends significantly on the contributions of our executive officers, our brand presidents and brand leadership teams, and other key personnel. Our operating model gives brand leaders substantial autonomy over culinary direction, guest experience, and local marketing. As a result, the performance of each brand may depend heavily on the judgment and capabilities of a relatively small number of individuals. The loss of one or more key executives or brand leaders, whether through resignation, retirement, illness, or other causes, could disrupt our operations, delay the execution of our strategy, and result in the loss of institutional knowledge.

Competition for experienced restaurant executives is intense. We may not be able to identify, recruit, or integrate suitable replacements on a timely basis or on acceptable terms. Although our Compensation and Human Capital Committee periodically reviews succession planning with management, our succession plans may not be effective in ensuring a smooth transition. Leadership transitions can also create uncertainty among team members, franchisees, and other stakeholders. We do not maintain key-person life insurance on all of our executives.

**Volatility in the prices of food, beverages, and other commodities could adversely affect our margins, and we generally do not hedge commodity prices with financial instruments.**

Many of the products we purchase, including beef, poultry, pork, seafood, fresh produce, dairy products, grains, cooking oils, wine, spirits, and beer, are subject to significant price volatility. As described in Item 1, price changes may result from weather, crop yields, animal disease, feed costs, fuel and transportation costs, trade policies and tariffs, labor availability, and changes in supply and demand. Packaging, paper goods, cleaning supplies, and smallwares are also subject to price fluctuations.

Several of our brands have significant exposure to particular commodities. Amberwood Grill and Ironbark Chophouse purchase substantial quantities of beef, including premium and prime grades, whose prices can be highly volatile and are affected by cattle herd cycles, drought, and export demand. Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen depends on seafood, whose supply and price can be affected by fishery management decisions, harvest restrictions, water temperature changes, red tide and other environmental events, import tariffs, and international trade conditions. Our use of hardwood for cooking at Amberwood Grill also exposes us to the cost and availability of that input.

We seek to manage commodity cost risk through fixed-price and formula-based purchase contracts, forward purchase commitments, menu engineering, specification adjustments, and pricing actions. However, as described in Item 1, we generally do not use financial instruments to hedge commodity prices. Our purchase contracts typically cover limited periods, and fixed-price contracts may cause us to pay more than prevailing market prices if prices decline. We may be unable to anticipate or react effectively to commodity price changes, and we may be unable to pass increases on to guests through menu pricing without reducing traffic. Significant increases in commodity costs could adversely affect our operating margins and results of operations.

**Disruptions in our supply chain or distribution network, or the loss of key suppliers or distributors, could adversely affect our operations.**

Our restaurants depend on frequent deliveries of fresh ingredients and other products that meet our specifications. We rely primarily on broadline and specialty foodservice distributors to deliver products to our restaurants. In many jurisdictions, alcoholic beverages must be purchased through licensed wholesalers. For certain items, particularly proprietary sauces, seasonings, and specialty products, we rely on a limited number of suppliers that produce items to our proprietary specifications.

Our supply chain could be disrupted by many factors, including:

- financial difficulties, insolvency, or consolidation of suppliers or distributors;
- labor shortages, strikes, or work stoppages;
- transportation constraints and fuel shortages;
- severe weather, including hurricanes and tropical storms affecting our southeastern markets;
- food safety recalls or regulatory actions affecting suppliers;
- animal disease outbreaks or crop failures;
- trade disputes, tariffs, and import restrictions;
- public health crises;
- cyberattacks on suppliers or distributors;
- geopolitical events.

The loss of a significant distributor, or a disruption in its operations, could adversely affect our restaurants until alternative arrangements are made. Although we believe that alternative sources of supply are generally available for our principal products, changing suppliers could involve additional cost, time, and product qualification, and replacement products may not meet our quality standards or proprietary specifications. Shortages or interruptions could require us to remove menu items, substitute ingredients, pay higher prices, or temporarily close restaurants, any of which could harm guest satisfaction and our results of operations. In addition, our responsible sourcing priorities may limit the number of qualified suppliers for certain products and increase our costs.

**Hurricanes, tropical storms, flooding, and other severe weather events, which may become more frequent or severe as a result of climate change, could adversely affect our business.**

Our headquarters and many of our restaurants are located in Florida and other coastal and southeastern markets exposed to hurricanes, tropical storms, storm surge, flooding, and extreme heat. These events are more common during the Atlantic hurricane season in the late summer and fall. A significant storm or other severe weather event could:

- force the temporary or extended closure of affected restaurants;
- cause property damage to restaurants and their contents;
- disrupt the delivery of food and supplies;
- cause loss of perishable inventory due to power outages;
- reduce guest traffic before, during, and after the event;
- displace team members or prevent them from reporting to work;
- disrupt the operations of our Restaurant Support Center and the shared services it provides to all of our brands;
- damage communications, utility, and transportation infrastructure.

Although we maintain business continuity plans and have experience operating through severe weather, these plans may not be adequate to address a major storm or a series of storms. Recovery from a significant event could take an extended period, particularly if local infrastructure, housing, and tourism are severely affected. Our business interruption and property insurance may not fully cover our losses, and insurance for wind and flood damage in coastal and hurricane-prone regions has become more costly and, in some cases, more limited in availability.

Climate change may increase the frequency, severity, and unpredictability of hurricanes, flooding, heat waves, droughts, and other extreme weather events. It may also affect the availability and cost of agricultural commodities and seafood, increase energy costs, and require capital expenditures to harden our restaurants against severe weather. Severe weather in other regions where we operate, including winter storms, can also reduce guest traffic and cause temporary closures. The occurrence of one or more severe weather events could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

**Increases in insurance costs, reduced availability of coverage, and our retention of significant self-insured losses could adversely affect our results of operations.**

We maintain insurance for a variety of risks, including property damage, general liability, liquor liability, workers' compensation, employment practices liability, automobile liability, cyber liability, directors' and officers' liability, and business interruption. The cost of insurance has risen significantly in recent years, particularly for property coverage in coastal and hurricane-prone regions, including Florida. We expect that these costs may continue to increase, and in some cases coverage may become unavailable on acceptable terms or at all. Higher deductibles, lower limits, and broader exclusions could increase the portion of losses that we bear.

As described in Item 1, we retain a significant portion of expected losses under certain of our programs, including workers' compensation, general liability, and team member health benefits, through self-insurance or high-deductible arrangements. Our accrued liabilities for self-insured risks are based on actuarial estimates that incorporate assumptions about claim frequency, severity, development patterns, and medical cost trends. Actual losses may differ materially from these estimates. Unexpected increases in the number or severity of claims, adverse developments in pending claims, rising medical costs, or "social inflation" in jury verdicts and settlements could require us to increase our reserves and could adversely affect our results of operations. In addition, our stop-loss and excess coverage may not be sufficient to cover very large or catastrophic claims, and insurers may dispute coverage for particular losses.

**Increases in energy and utility costs could adversely affect our margins.**

Our restaurants consume significant amounts of electricity, natural gas, and water to operate cooking equipment, refrigeration, dishwashing, lighting, and heating and air conditioning systems. Air conditioning loads are particularly significant in our Florida and southeastern restaurants. Energy prices can fluctuate significantly as a result of weather, geopolitical events, changes in supply and demand, regulatory changes, and infrastructure constraints. Rising fuel costs also increase the delivery charges we pay to distributors and the costs of our suppliers.

In addition, some jurisdictions have adopted, or may adopt, restrictions on the use of natural gas in new construction. Such restrictions could require us to redesign kitchen equipment and cooking methods for new restaurants and could be particularly disruptive to concepts, such as Amberwood Grill and Ventana Trattoria, whose culinary identities depend on wood-fire cooking or wood-oven preparations. Regulations governing air emissions from cooking equipment could also increase our costs or limit our ability to use certain cooking methods in some markets. If we are unable to offset increases in energy and utility costs, our margins could decline.

**Our reliance on third-party delivery service providers exposes us to risks that could adversely affect our off-premise business and margins.**

Off-premise dining has become a significant and durable part of our business, and delivery orders through our brand-owned channels are, in many markets, fulfilled by third-party delivery service providers integrated with our platforms. We also participate in third-party delivery marketplaces. These arrangements expose us to a number of risks, including:

- commissions and fees that reduce the profitability of delivery orders;
- the potential for providers to increase fees or change terms in ways that are unfavorable to us;
- limited control over the quality, timeliness, and condition of food delivered to guests, which could harm guest satisfaction and our brands;
- loss of direct relationships with guests who order through marketplaces, and limited access to guest data;
- the risk that providers promote competing restaurants, including their own virtual or delivery-only concepts, over our brands;
- the financial stability and operational reliability of providers;
- data security and privacy risks associated with sharing information with providers;
- regulatory changes affecting providers, including changes in the classification of delivery drivers, fee caps, and restrictions on the delivery of alcoholic beverages;
- potential liability for incidents involving delivery drivers.

Greenhollow Bowls, which has a high proportion of digital and off-premise sales, is particularly exposed to these risks. If delivery providers increase their fees, experience service disruptions, or exit markets, or if consumer use of delivery declines, our off-premise sales and margins could be adversely affected. If delivery orders cannibalize higher-margin dine-in or carryout sales, our overall profitability could decline.

**Our results fluctuate from quarter to quarter due to seasonality and other factors, which could cause our results to fall short of expectations.**

As described in Item 1, our sales and operating results fluctuate from quarter to quarter due to seasonal patterns, holidays, weather, and the timing of restaurant openings and closures. Hurricanes and tropical storms are more common during the late summer and fall. Gift card sales are concentrated in the holiday season, and the redemption of those cards typically benefits sales in subsequent months. Fine dining sales are influenced by the timing of corporate entertainment activity, including the holiday party season. Sales in markets with significant tourist activity fluctuate with seasonal travel patterns.

Other factors that could cause our quarterly results to fluctuate include:

- changes in commodity and labor costs;
- pre-opening costs and initial operating inefficiencies of new restaurants;
- impairment charges and restaurant closure costs;
- changes in self-insurance reserves;
- the timing of marketing and promotional activities;
- changes in the calendar, including shifts in the timing of holidays;
- legal settlements and other non-recurring items.

As a result, results for any interim period are not necessarily indicative of results for a full fiscal year. Fluctuations in our quarterly results could cause us to fall short of the expectations of securities analysts or investors, which could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

## Risks Related to Our Brands, Strategy, and Growth

**Damage to the reputation of our brands, including through social media and adverse publicity, could reduce sales across our portfolio.**

Our success depends on the value and reputation of our brands. Brand value is based in large part on guest perceptions of food quality, safety, value, hospitality, and our conduct as a corporate citizen. These perceptions can be damaged by many types of events, including:

- food safety incidents or concerns about ingredient sourcing;
- service failures or inconsistent execution;
- allegations of discrimination, harassment, or mistreatment of guests or team members;
- workplace accidents or violent incidents at our restaurants;
- data breaches or misuse of guest information;
- labor disputes or allegations of unfair employment practices;
- concerns about animal welfare, environmental practices, or responsible sourcing;
- the conduct of franchisees, licensees, suppliers, delivery partners, or celebrity or influencer partners;
- positions we take, or decline to take, on social, political, or environmental issues.

The widespread use of social media and online review platforms allows individuals to share opinions and information, whether accurate or not, with large audiences almost instantly. A single negative incident captured on video, a viral post, or a coordinated online campaign could rapidly damage a brand's reputation before we have an opportunity to respond. We also use social media in our marketing, and missteps in our own communications could cause reputational harm. Because our brands share a common corporate platform, adverse publicity affecting one brand or our corporate identity could affect other brands in our portfolio. Restoring a damaged reputation can take substantial time and investment. Any significant damage to the reputation of our brands could reduce guest traffic and adversely affect our results of operations.

**Managing a multi-brand portfolio is complex, and the underperformance of one or more brands could adversely affect our results.**

We operate six distinct brands across three reportable segments, spanning casual dining, fine dining, fast-casual, and daytime dining. Managing a diverse portfolio presents challenges that a single-brand company may not face, including:

- allocating capital, management attention, and shared-service resources among brands with different growth prospects and return profiles;
- maintaining clear differentiation among brands and avoiding competition among our own brands for the same guest occasion;
- balancing the autonomy of brand leadership with the efficiencies of centralized services;
- maintaining distinct supply chain specifications, technology configurations, and training programs within a common platform;
- realizing the anticipated benefits of scale without diluting brand identities.

Our brands are at different stages of maturity and face different competitive dynamics. The performance of any brand could decline for reasons specific to that brand, such as loss of relevance, menu fatigue, increased competition in its category, or ineffective leadership. If we misallocate capital to brands or markets that do not generate expected returns, or underinvest in brands that require reinvestment, our overall returns could suffer. We may also decide to sell, close, or reposition a brand, which could result in impairment charges, disruption, and costs. Our segment structure could change in the future, which could affect the comparability of our reported results.

**Our new restaurant development, remodel, and relocation activities involve substantial risks and may not achieve expected returns.**

Our growth strategy depends in part on opening new restaurants and reinvesting in existing restaurants through remodels, refreshes, kitchen upgrades, technology enhancements, and relocations. These activities require significant capital and involve numerous risks, including:

- difficulty identifying and securing suitable sites on acceptable terms;
- increases in construction, equipment, and labor costs;
- delays or difficulties in obtaining zoning, permitting, environmental, utility, and design approvals;
- shortages of contractors, materials, and equipment, including as a result of tariffs or supply chain disruptions;
- weather-related construction delays, particularly during the hurricane season;
- difficulty hiring and training qualified team members for new restaurants;
- inaccurate forecasts of sales volumes, including because of flaws in our site selection models;
- cannibalization of sales at our existing restaurants in the same trade area;
- changes in the trade area after a restaurant opens, such as new competition, road construction, or declines in nearby traffic generators.

New restaurants typically incur higher operating costs during their opening period and may take time to reach the sales volumes and margins of our more mature restaurants. Some new restaurants may never reach those levels. Restaurants in new markets, where we have limited operating experience and brand awareness, may be particularly vulnerable. Remodels may disrupt operations during construction and may not generate sales increases sufficient to justify the investment. If our development and reinvestment activities do not perform as expected, we may need to slow our development pace, close restaurants, or record impairment charges, and our growth and returns on invested capital could be adversely affected.

**Our emerging brands, including Daybreak Porch, may not achieve the performance necessary to support continued investment.**

Daybreak Porch is our newest full-service brand and is in a development phase in which we are refining its operating model and prototype design. Greenhollow Bowls is also at an earlier stage of growth than our established casual dining brands. Emerging brands present heightened risks, including:

- unproven unit economics and limited operating history on which to base development decisions;
- limited brand awareness in new markets;
- operating models that may require significant refinement before they can be replicated at scale;
- daytime-only operating hours at Daybreak Porch, which concentrate sales into a narrower window and limit the ability to absorb fixed occupancy costs;
- the possibility that guest enthusiasm for a new concept will not be sustained.

We may invest substantial capital and management resources in emerging brands that ultimately do not achieve acceptable returns. If an emerging brand fails to perform as expected, we may slow or halt its expansion, close restaurants, or exit the concept, which could result in impairment charges, lease termination costs, and other expenses. Even if an emerging brand succeeds, its growth may divert resources from our established brands.

**We may be required to record impairment charges, which could adversely affect our results of operations and reduce our stockholders' equity.**

We periodically review our long-lived assets, including property and equipment and operating lease right-of-use assets, for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a restaurant or group of restaurants may not be recoverable. We also test goodwill and indefinite-lived intangible assets, including trademarks associated with brands we have acquired, for impairment at least annually and more frequently if indicators of impairment arise.

Factors that could lead to impairment include:

- declines in restaurant sales or cash flows;
- adverse changes in the business climate or in the markets in which our restaurants operate;
- decisions to close or relocate restaurants;
- declines in the performance or outlook of a brand;
- increases in discount rates or changes in other valuation assumptions;
- declines in our market capitalization.

Impairment analyses require significant judgment, including estimates of future sales, margins, and cash flows, and the selection of appropriate discount rates. Changes in these estimates could result in material impairment charges. Impairment charges are non-cash, but they reduce our reported earnings and our stockholders' equity. As discussed below under "Risks Related to Our Financial Condition and Capital Structure," our stockholders' equity of $2,098,428,814 at the end of fiscal 2024 represented approximately 19.6% of our total assets of $10,730,227,552. As a result, significant impairment charges could have a proportionately larger effect on our equity than they would for a company with a higher ratio of equity to assets. Restaurant closures can also result in lease termination costs, severance, and other expenses.

**Acquisitions, dispositions, and other strategic transactions may not achieve their intended benefits and may disrupt our business.**

Our history includes the selective acquisition of brands that we believed had durable guest appeal and room to grow, and we may pursue acquisitions, dispositions, joint ventures, or other strategic transactions in the future. These transactions involve numerous risks, including:

- difficulties integrating acquired brands, team members, systems, and supply chains;
- the potential loss of key personnel of acquired businesses;
- overpaying for acquired businesses or failing to achieve anticipated synergies;
- unanticipated liabilities, including liabilities for employment, tax, environmental, or food safety matters;
- the diversion of management attention from existing operations;
- dilution of our brand identities or culture;
- the incurrence of additional indebtedness or the issuance of equity securities;
- the recording of goodwill and intangible assets that may later become impaired.

Dispositions may result in losses, ongoing indemnification obligations, and the loss of revenues and cash flows. We may also be unable to complete desirable transactions on acceptable terms or at all. Any strategic transaction that does not achieve its intended benefits could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

**Our marketing programs, loyalty programs, and gift card programs may not be successful and could expose us to additional risks.**

We invest significantly in marketing, including digital and social media advertising, streaming and connected television, email and text-message communications, local store marketing, and partnerships. We increasingly rely on digital and data-driven channels to target relevant audiences and measure return on marketing investment. These efforts may not succeed in attracting or retaining guests. Changes in technology, platform policies, and privacy regulations could reduce the effectiveness of targeted advertising and our ability to measure results. Competitors may outspend us, and media costs may increase.

Several of our brands operate loyalty programs, and we operate a cross-brand recognition program. These programs may not generate incremental visits sufficient to justify their cost, and changes to program terms could alienate members. Loyalty programs also involve the collection and use of personal information, creating privacy and data security risks, and are subject to consumer protection laws governing program terms and communications.

We sell gift cards for each of our brands and a portfolio-wide gift card that can be redeemed across participating brands. Gift card sales are seasonal and depend on third-party retail and digital distribution channels, whose fees and terms may change. Gift cards are subject to laws governing expiration dates, fees, and unclaimed property, and changes in those laws or their interpretation could affect our recognition of breakage revenue. Gift card fraud could result in losses. If our marketing, loyalty, or gift card programs do not perform as expected, our sales and results of operations could be adversely affected.

**We may be unable to protect our intellectual property adequately, and we could be subject to claims of infringement.**

Our trademarks, service marks, trade names, trade dress, logos, domain names, proprietary recipes, and operating procedures are important to the marketing and recognition of our brands. We rely on trademark registrations, trade secret protection, and confidentiality agreements to protect our intellectual property. These measures may not be adequate. Third parties may infringe or misappropriate our intellectual property, and policing unauthorized use can be difficult and expensive. Trade secrets, including proprietary recipes, could be disclosed by current or former team members, suppliers, franchisees, or licensees despite confidentiality obligations. Intellectual property protection in certain foreign jurisdictions where our franchisees operate may be weaker than in the United States.

We may also face claims that our marks, menu item names, trade dress, or business practices infringe the rights of others. Defending such claims could be costly and could require us to change brand names, rebrand restaurants, modify menus, or pay damages or royalties. Any of these outcomes could adversely affect our brands and results of operations.

## Risks Related to Franchising, Licensing, and International Operations

**Our franchising activities depend on the performance and conduct of independent franchisees over whom we have limited control.**

We supplement our company-operated restaurant base with franchised restaurants in certain domestic markets, international markets, and non-traditional venues such as airports, travel centers, universities, and entertainment venues. Our franchise revenues, including royalties and fees, depend on the sales and financial health of our franchisees. Franchisees are independent businesses responsible for their own employment decisions, financing, and day-to-day operations. Although we require franchisees to operate in accordance with our brand standards and conduct periodic evaluations of franchised restaurants, we have limited control over their operations.

Franchisees may not operate their restaurants in a manner consistent with our standards for food safety, quality, service, and restaurant design. Their failures could damage the reputation of our brands and harm sales at company-operated restaurants. Franchisees may experience financial difficulties that cause them to underinvest in their restaurants, fall behind on royalty or other payments, close restaurants, or fail to meet development commitments. Franchisee financial difficulties could result in lower revenues and bad debt expense for us, and, in some cases, we may be subject to contingent liabilities related to franchisee obligations.

Disputes with franchisees could result in litigation, damage our relationships with other franchisees, and divert management attention. We may also be unable to attract qualified franchisees for new markets or venues, or to renew franchise agreements on acceptable terms. Changes in franchise laws, including laws governing terminations, non-renewals, and the franchise relationship, could limit our ability to manage our franchise system effectively.

**We could be held liable for the acts or obligations of our franchisees, including under joint employer theories.**

Franchisees independently employ the team members at franchised restaurants, and those individuals are not our employees. However, franchisors have increasingly been named as defendants in lawsuits and regulatory proceedings involving franchisees, including claims based on joint employer, vicarious liability, or agency theories. The standards for determining joint employer status under federal and state labor laws have changed several times in recent years and remain subject to change through rulemaking, agency decisions, and litigation.

If we were found to be a joint employer of franchisee employees, we could be held liable for franchisee violations of wage-and-hour, discrimination, and other employment laws. We could also be required to participate in collective bargaining with franchisee employees. Even unsuccessful claims could result in significant legal costs and adverse publicity. We could also be subject to claims arising from food safety incidents, premises accidents, or other events at franchised restaurants. Any of these developments could adversely affect our business and results of operations, and could cause us to change how we support franchisees in ways that reduce the effectiveness of our franchise system.

**Our international franchise operations expose us to political, economic, legal, and currency risks.**

Our international franchising activities are typically structured as area development or master franchise agreements with partners in their respective markets. Our international operations are subject to risks that differ from, or are greater than, those we face in the United States, including:

- political instability, civil unrest, terrorism, and armed conflict;
- economic volatility and currency fluctuations that affect the U.S. dollar value of royalties;
- restrictions on the transfer of funds, the import of products, or foreign investment;
- differing consumer preferences, which may require menu adaptations;
- difficulties in sourcing products that meet our specifications;
- weaker intellectual property protection;
- differing franchise, employment, food safety, and tax laws;
- compliance with U.S. laws governing foreign operations, including anti-corruption, sanctions, and export control laws;
- adverse perceptions of U.S. brands in certain markets.

Violations of anti-corruption or sanctions laws by our international partners, even without our knowledge, could expose us to penalties and reputational harm. If our international franchise partners do not perform as expected, or if conditions in their markets deteriorate, our international revenues and growth could be adversely affected.

**Our licensing of branded consumer products exposes us to product quality, recall, and reputational risks.**

We license certain of our trademarks and proprietary recipes to third parties for the manufacture and sale of branded consumer products, such as sauces, dressings, and seasonings, sold through retail grocery channels. Although our licensing arrangements are subject to our product quality standards and approval rights, we do not control the manufacturing, packaging, distribution, or marketing activities of our licensees. A product quality issue, contamination, mislabeling of allergens, or recall involving a licensed product could harm the reputation of the associated brand and reduce sales at our restaurants. We could also be named in product liability claims. Licensees may fail to meet sales expectations, fail to pay royalties, or experience financial difficulties. Our licensing revenues depend on retail grocery dynamics, including shelf space, private-label competition, and retailer relationships, over which we have little influence.

## Risks Related to Information Technology, Cybersecurity, and Data Privacy

**A cybersecurity incident or other breach of our information systems could result in liability, regulatory penalties, operational disruption, and reputational harm.**

We collect, process, and store personal information about guests, team members, and other parties, as well as payment card data. Our systems, and those of our third-party vendors, are subject to cybersecurity threats, including:

- ransomware and other malware;
- phishing and social engineering attacks;
- credential theft and account takeover;
- denial-of-service attacks;
- attacks on point-of-sale systems designed to steal payment card data;
- vulnerabilities in software and hardware supplied by third parties;
- insider threats from current or former team members or contractors;
- attacks enabled or enhanced by artificial intelligence tools.

The techniques used in cyberattacks change frequently and are often difficult to detect for extended periods. Our large, distributed restaurant footprint and the high turnover of our hourly workforce may increase our exposure to certain threats, including social engineering and the misuse of credentials. We rely on numerous third-party vendors, including cloud service providers, payment processors, digital ordering and loyalty platforms, delivery providers, and payroll and benefits administrators. Security failures at these vendors could expose our data or disrupt our operations, and we have limited ability to control their security practices.

Although we maintain an information security program, as described in Item 1C, "Cybersecurity," we cannot guarantee that our measures will prevent or detect all security incidents. A significant incident could result in:

- the unauthorized disclosure or loss of personal information or payment card data;
- disruption of restaurant operations, digital ordering, or corporate functions;
- ransom payments or extended recovery efforts;
- litigation, including class actions;
- regulatory investigations, fines, and penalties under privacy and data breach laws;
- assessments, fines, and increased transaction fees under payment card industry rules;
- significant remediation and notification costs;
- damage to our reputation and loss of guest trust.

Our cyber liability insurance may not be sufficient to cover all losses from a significant incident, and such insurance may become more costly or less available. Any of these consequences could adversely affect our business, financial condition, and results of operations.

**Failures or interruptions in our information technology systems, or those of our vendors, could disrupt our operations.**

Information technology is integral to nearly every aspect of our business. As described in Item 1, our technology portfolio includes restaurant point-of-sale and kitchen systems, digital ordering and loyalty platforms, data and analytics platforms, enterprise resource planning and financial systems, human resources and payroll systems, supply chain systems, and network and communications infrastructure. Our restaurants use a common suite of operational technology, which creates efficiencies but also means that a failure in a shared system could affect many restaurants at once.

Our systems could be disrupted by power outages, telecommunications failures, hardware failures, software defects, natural disasters including hurricanes, cyberattacks, vendor failures, and human error. A disruption could prevent restaurants from processing orders and payments, impair our ability to fulfill digital and off-premise orders, interrupt payroll processing, and affect our ability to produce timely and accurate financial information. Because our Restaurant Support Center is located in Florida, a severe weather event affecting that facility could disrupt shared services that support all of our brands, despite our business continuity and disaster recovery plans.

We periodically upgrade or replace our systems, and implementing new systems involves risks of cost overruns, delays, data migration errors, and operational disruptions. If we fail to maintain, upgrade, or protect our systems effectively, our operations and results could be adversely affected.

**Our investments in digital capabilities and emerging technologies may not succeed.**

Guests increasingly expect to engage with restaurants through digital channels, including online and mobile ordering, digital reservations and waitlists, contactless payment, and personalized offers. We have invested, and expect to continue to invest, in technology to meet these expectations. We also evaluate emerging technologies, such as automated order-taking, kitchen automation, artificial intelligence, and data-driven personalization.

These investments involve significant costs and uncertainties. Technologies we adopt may not deliver the expected improvements in guest experience or operating efficiency. Guests may not adopt them, or competitors may develop superior capabilities. The use of artificial intelligence and automated tools may produce inaccurate or biased outputs, raise privacy and intellectual property concerns, attract regulatory scrutiny, or harm the hospitality-driven experience that defines our full-service brands. Our digital platforms must also be accessible to individuals with disabilities, and we have been, and may in the future be, subject to claims alleging that our websites and mobile applications are not fully accessible. If our digital investments do not succeed, we may not recover their costs, and our competitive position could suffer.

**Evolving privacy and data protection laws could increase our compliance costs and limit our marketing and loyalty activities.**

We are subject to an expanding body of federal, state, and foreign laws governing the collection, use, retention, security, and transfer of personal information. These include comprehensive state consumer privacy laws, laws governing marketing communications by email and text message, laws relating to the collection of biometric information, and data breach notification laws. Many of these laws grant consumers rights to access, delete, and opt out of the sale or sharing of their personal information, and some provide for private rights of action and statutory damages.

The regulatory landscape is changing rapidly, and new laws continue to be adopted at the state level, creating a patchwork of requirements that can be inconsistent and costly to implement. Laws governing text-message marketing and certain website tracking technologies have been the subject of significant class action litigation against consumer-facing companies. Compliance with these laws requires ongoing investment in our systems and processes and may limit our ability to collect and use guest data for loyalty, personalization, and targeted marketing. Failure to comply could result in regulatory enforcement, litigation, fines, and reputational harm. As a merchant that accepts payment cards, we are also subject to the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, and failure to comply could result in fines, increased fees, or the loss of our ability to accept payment cards.

## Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

**We are subject to extensive and evolving laws and regulations, and compliance failures or changes in these laws could adversely affect our business.**

As described in Item 1, we are subject to extensive federal, state, local, and foreign laws and regulations affecting the development, construction, and operation of our restaurants, our franchising activities, our employment practices, and other aspects of our business. These include laws relating to:

- food safety, health, and sanitation;
- menu labeling and nutrition disclosure;
- alcoholic beverage control;
- employment, wages, and working conditions;
- accessibility for individuals with disabilities;
- franchising;
- privacy and data protection;
- environmental protection;
- zoning, land use, and construction;
- taxes and unclaimed property;
- consumer protection and advertising.

Compliance with these laws is costly and requires significant management attention. Laws and regulations frequently change, and new requirements may be adopted with little notice. A failure to comply with applicable laws, whether by us, our franchisees, or our vendors, could result in fines, penalties, temporary or permanent restaurant closures, loss of operating permits or liquor licenses, litigation, and reputational harm. Changes in laws or regulations, or in their interpretation or enforcement, could increase our costs, require changes to our operations, or limit our ability to operate in certain jurisdictions. Difficulties or delays in obtaining permits and approvals could increase our costs and delay restaurant openings or remodels.

**We are subject to litigation and other claims that could result in significant costs and harm our reputation.**

In the ordinary course of business, we are subject to a variety of lawsuits, claims, and governmental investigations. These include:

- wage-and-hour class and collective actions, including claims involving overtime, meal and rest breaks, tip credits and tip pooling, off-the-clock work, and employee classification;
- claims of discrimination, harassment, retaliation, and wrongful termination;
- premises liability claims, including slip-and-fall injuries;
- claims by guests alleging illness or injury from food or allergic reactions;
- claims under accessibility laws relating to our restaurants, websites, and mobile applications;
- consumer protection claims relating to advertising, pricing, menu descriptions, service charges, and loyalty and gift card programs;
- privacy and data security claims;
- disputes with franchisees, landlords, suppliers, and contractors;
- claims relating to intellectual property.

The restaurant industry is a frequent target of class action litigation, and plaintiffs in these cases may seek substantial damages, which may not be covered by insurance. Regardless of their merit, claims can be costly to defend, divert management attention, and generate adverse publicity. Because many of these matters are inherently uncertain, we cannot predict their outcomes. An unfavorable resolution of one or more matters, or a settlement of significant amount, could adversely affect our financial condition and results of operations. Our accruals for legal matters are based on estimates that may prove to be inaccurate.

**Our sale of alcoholic beverages subjects us to licensing requirements and to liability under "dram shop" statutes.**

Alcoholic beverages are an important component of the guest experience and sales at our full-service brands. Each restaurant that sells alcoholic beverages must obtain and maintain licenses or permits from state and, in some cases, local authorities. These licenses are subject to periodic renewal and may be suspended or revoked for violations, such as serving minors or visibly intoxicated persons. The loss or suspension of a liquor license at a restaurant, or difficulty obtaining a license for a new restaurant, could significantly reduce that restaurant's sales or delay its opening. Alcoholic beverage regulations also govern many aspects of daily operations, including hours of operation, advertising, wholesale purchasing, and the sale of alcoholic beverages for off-premise consumption.

We are subject to "dram shop" statutes in many states, which generally provide that a person injured by an intoxicated individual may recover damages from the establishment that wrongfully served alcohol to that individual. Dram shop claims can result in substantial damages awards. Although we carry liquor liability insurance and train our team members in responsible alcohol service, our insurance may not be sufficient to cover all claims, and liquor liability insurance has become more expensive. A significant judgment against us could adversely affect our financial condition and results of operations and harm our reputation. In addition, increases in excise taxes on alcoholic beverages or changes in regulations governing their sale could reduce our beverage sales and margins.

**Regulations and public concern relating to nutrition, health, and menu content could increase our costs and reduce demand.**

Federal law requires restaurant chains above a certain size to disclose calorie information on menus and menu boards and to make additional nutrition information available. Certain state and local jurisdictions have adopted, or may adopt, additional requirements relating to sodium content, added sugars, allergens, and ingredients in children's menu offerings. Compliance with these requirements involves costs, and errors in nutrition or allergen disclosures could expose us to regulatory penalties, litigation, and harm to guests.

The disclosure of nutrition information may influence guest choices in ways that reduce demand for certain menu items. Governments may also adopt measures intended to discourage the consumption of particular foods or beverages, such as taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages, restrictions on marketing, or limits on certain ingredients. Public health campaigns and media attention focused on the health effects of restaurant food could reduce demand for our offerings. We may be required to reformulate recipes or modify menus, which could increase costs or alter the guest experience in ways that reduce demand.

**Environmental, climate, and sustainability-related laws and stakeholder expectations could increase our costs and expose us to reputational and legal risks.**

Our operations are subject to environmental laws relating to waste disposal, including grease and food waste, air emissions from cooking equipment, wastewater, refrigerants, and the remediation of contaminated properties. We may be liable for environmental contamination at properties we own or lease, including contamination caused by prior owners or tenants. Some jurisdictions have adopted restrictions on single-use plastics and packaging materials, organic waste diversion requirements, and restrictions on the use of natural gas in new construction. Additional requirements, including those relating to climate change, greenhouse gas emissions, refrigerants, packaging, and climate-related disclosures, could increase our costs.

Investors, guests, team members, and other stakeholders increasingly focus on corporate practices relating to sustainability, animal welfare, responsible sourcing, and social responsibility. Expectations in these areas are diverse, sometimes conflicting, and subject to change. Our responsible sourcing priorities and corporate responsibility efforts may not satisfy all stakeholders. If we fail to meet stakeholder expectations or our own stated objectives, or if we are perceived as having done so, our reputation could be harmed. Conversely, certain stakeholders and government officials have criticized corporate sustainability and social initiatives, which could expose us to scrutiny, legal challenges, or reputational harm. Disclosures about our sustainability efforts could also be subject to claims that they are inaccurate or misleading.

**Changes in tax laws, unfavorable tax audits, and unclaimed property obligations could adversely affect our results.**

We are subject to federal, state, local, and foreign tax laws, including income, sales and use, payroll, property, and excise taxes. Changes in tax laws or their interpretation, including changes in corporate income tax rates, the treatment of tip income and related employer tax credits, the deductibility of business meals and entertainment, and sales tax rules applicable to restaurant transactions and delivery orders, could increase our tax expense or reduce demand. Several of these matters, including the deductibility of business meals, could particularly affect Ironbark Chophouse.

We are subject to audits by tax authorities, and the outcome of audits could differ from the amounts we have recorded. We are also subject to unclaimed property laws, including laws applicable to gift cards. State unclaimed property audits can be lengthy and costly, and changes in unclaimed property laws or their application could affect our recognition of gift card breakage and require us to remit amounts to states. Any of these developments could adversely affect our financial condition and results of operations.

**Changes in immigration law and enforcement could adversely affect our workforce and operations.**

The restaurant industry relies on a diverse workforce. Although we require all team members to provide documentation of their eligibility to work in the United States and comply with employment eligibility verification requirements, some team members may, without our knowledge, be unauthorized workers. Changes in federal or state immigration laws or enforcement practices, including expanded workplace enforcement, mandatory use of electronic verification systems, or changes to temporary work authorization programs, could reduce the available labor pool, increase our labor costs, and disrupt operations in affected restaurants. If we were found to have employed unauthorized workers, we could be subject to fines, penalties, and adverse publicity. Immigration enforcement activity could also affect our suppliers, particularly in agriculture and food processing, and increase our food costs.

## Risks Related to Our Financial Condition and Capital Structure

**Our liabilities are substantial relative to our assets and stockholders' equity, which could limit our financial flexibility and increase our vulnerability to adverse developments.**

As of the end of fiscal 2024, we had total assets of $10,730,227,552, total liabilities of $8,631,798,738, and total stockholders' equity of $2,098,428,814. Our total liabilities represented approximately 80.4% of our total assets, and our ratio of total liabilities to total stockholders' equity was approximately 4.11 to 1. Our liabilities include, among other items:

- long-term operating and finance lease liabilities associated with the restaurant properties we lease, which represent the majority of our restaurant properties;
- indebtedness under the agreements governing our borrowings;
- accounts payable and accrued expenses, including accrued wages, managing partner compensation, and other payroll-related obligations;
- accrued liabilities for self-insured risks, including workers' compensation, general liability, and team member health benefits;
- deferred revenue associated with unredeemed gift cards and loyalty program obligations;
- income tax and other liabilities.

Our substantial liabilities could have important consequences, including:

- requiring us to dedicate a significant portion of our cash flows from operations to rent, interest, and principal payments, reducing cash available for restaurant development, remodels, technology investment, and the return of capital to shareholders;
- limiting our ability to obtain additional financing on acceptable terms for working capital, capital expenditures, acquisitions, or other purposes;
- increasing our vulnerability to economic downturns, industry conditions, and adverse developments in our business, because many of our obligations are fixed;
- limiting our flexibility to respond to competitive pressures and changes in consumer demand;
- placing us at a competitive disadvantage relative to competitors with stronger balance sheets.

Because our stockholders' equity represented approximately 19.6% of our total assets, a relatively modest decline in the value of our assets, whether resulting from impairment charges, operating losses, or other factors, would represent a proportionately larger reduction in our stockholders' equity. If our cash flows decline, we may be unable to meet our obligations as they come due without taking actions such as reducing capital expenditures, selling assets, closing restaurants, or seeking additional capital, which may not be available on favorable terms or at all.

**The agreements governing our indebtedness may restrict our operations, and a default could have serious consequences.**

The agreements governing our indebtedness may contain financial maintenance covenants, such as limits on leverage and requirements regarding coverage of fixed charges, as well as covenants that restrict our ability to:

- incur additional indebtedness or grant liens;
- make certain investments, acquisitions, or dispositions;
- pay dividends or repurchase shares;
- enter into certain transactions with affiliates;
- merge or consolidate with other entities.

These restrictions could limit our ability to pursue business opportunities and respond to changing conditions. Our ability to comply with financial covenants depends on our operating performance, which is subject to the many risks described in this Item 1A. A decline in our operating results, including as a result of reduced traffic, higher costs, or significant impairment or other charges, could cause us to breach these covenants.

A breach could result in an event of default. In that case, lenders could declare outstanding amounts immediately due and payable, terminate commitments to extend further credit, and, to the extent applicable, exercise remedies against any collateral. A default under one agreement could also trigger cross-default or cross-acceleration provisions in other agreements. We may not have sufficient liquidity to repay accelerated amounts, and we may be unable to obtain waivers or amendments on acceptable terms. Any of these events could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition.

**Changes in interest rates and conditions in the credit markets could increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to capital.**

To the extent our borrowings bear interest at variable rates, increases in interest rates would increase our interest expense and reduce cash available for other purposes. When we refinance or issue new debt, we may do so at higher interest rates than our existing obligations. Disruptions or tightening in the credit markets, adverse changes in our credit profile, or downgrades in any credit ratings assigned to us could increase our borrowing costs, limit our access to financing, or require us to accept more restrictive terms. Higher interest rates also affect the discount rates used in impairment analyses, the measurement of certain liabilities, and the cost of capital for our development activities, and they can reduce consumer spending by increasing the cost of consumer credit. In addition, the financial institutions that participate in our credit arrangements could experience difficulties that impair their ability to fund their commitments.

**Our long-term lease obligations are largely fixed and could become burdensome if restaurant performance declines.**

We lease the majority of our restaurant properties. Our leases typically have initial terms with multiple renewal options and provide for fixed minimum rent. Many also require us to pay property taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance costs, and some provide for contingent rent based on sales above specified levels. Our lease obligations are recognized as liabilities on our balance sheet and contribute meaningfully to our total liabilities.

Lease payments are generally fixed regardless of restaurant performance. If a leased restaurant performs poorly or the surrounding trade area declines, we generally remain obligated to pay rent for the remainder of the lease term, unless we can negotiate a termination, sublease the property, or assign the lease. These alternatives may not be available or may require significant payments. When we close restaurants, we may continue to incur occupancy costs and may record impairment charges on related right-of-use assets and leasehold improvements.

When leases expire, we may be unable to renew them on acceptable terms or at all, which could force us to relocate or close restaurants, including profitable ones. Rising rents in desirable markets could increase our occupancy costs. Increases in property taxes and insurance costs passed through by landlords, particularly in Florida and other coastal markets, could further increase occupancy costs. Restaurants that we own are subject to risks of changes in real estate values and illiquidity.

**Our results depend on critical accounting estimates, and changes in those estimates or in accounting standards could adversely affect our reported results.**

The preparation of our consolidated financial statements requires management to make estimates and judgments that affect reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses. Areas requiring significant judgment include:

- impairment of long-lived assets, right-of-use assets, goodwill, and intangible assets;
- accrued liabilities for self-insured risks;
- gift card breakage and loyalty program obligations;
- lease accounting, including the determination of lease terms and discount rates;
- income taxes;
- legal contingencies;
- managing partner and other incentive compensation.

Actual results could differ materially from our estimates. Changes in our estimates, or in accounting standards or their interpretation, could require us to restate prior results, record significant charges, or change our accounting policies in ways that adversely affect our reported results and financial position.

**If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, investors could lose confidence in our financial statements.**

We are required to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting and to report annually on its effectiveness. Our independent registered public accounting firm is required to attest to the effectiveness of our internal control over financial reporting. Our operations are complex and involve many restaurants, multiple brands, numerous systems, franchise and licensing arrangements, and significant estimates. Changes in our systems, processes, or personnel, including system implementations and the integration of acquired businesses, increase the risk that deficiencies could arise. If we identify material weaknesses or are unable to conclude that our internal control is effective, we could fail to report our financial results accurately or on a timely basis. Such a failure could result in restatements, regulatory investigations, loss of investor confidence, and a decline in the market price of our common stock.

**We may not be able to continue to return capital to shareholders at current levels or at all.**

Our capital allocation priorities include the return of capital to shareholders. Any decision to pay dividends or repurchase shares is at the discretion of our Board of Directors and depends on our results of operations, cash flows, financial condition, capital requirements, restrictions in the agreements governing our indebtedness, applicable law, and other factors. Our substantial liabilities relative to our stockholders' equity, our ongoing investment in restaurant development and reinvestment, and the potential need to preserve liquidity during periods of economic uncertainty or after significant weather events could limit our capacity to return capital. Our Board may reduce, suspend, or discontinue dividends or share repurchases at any time. Any such action could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

## Risks Related to Our Common Stock and Corporate Governance

**The market price of our common stock may be volatile, and you could lose all or part of your investment.**

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors, some of which are beyond our control, including:

- variations in our quarterly results, including comparable restaurant sales, traffic, and margins;
- differences between our results or guidance and the expectations of securities analysts or investors;
- announcements by us or our competitors of new brands, menu offerings, acquisitions, or strategic initiatives;
- food safety incidents or other events affecting our brands or the restaurant industry;
- severe weather events affecting our markets;
- changes in commodity or labor costs;
- changes in economic conditions, consumer spending, or interest rates;
- changes in laws or regulations affecting our industry;
- sales of our common stock by significant shareholders, directors, or officers;
- actions of activist investors;
- general volatility in the financial markets.

These fluctuations may be unrelated to our operating performance. In the past, securities class action litigation has often been brought against companies following periods of volatility in the market price of their securities. Such litigation could result in substantial costs and divert management attention.

**Provisions of our governing documents and Florida law could discourage, delay, or prevent a change in control.**

We are incorporated in Florida, and our articles of incorporation and bylaws, together with Florida law, contain provisions that could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us, even if a change in control would be beneficial to our shareholders. Our governing documents may include provisions that authorize our Board of Directors to issue preferred stock with rights and preferences determined by the Board without shareholder approval, establish advance notice requirements for shareholder proposals and director nominations, and limit the ability of shareholders to act by written consent or to call special meetings. In addition, the Florida Business Corporation Act contains provisions that may restrict certain business combinations with interested shareholders and limit the voting rights of shares acquired in certain control share acquisitions, unless specified conditions are met or the corporation has opted out of those provisions.

These provisions could discourage potential acquisition proposals, delay or prevent a change in control, and limit the price that investors might be willing to pay for our common stock. They could also make it more difficult for shareholders to replace members of our Board of Directors or management.

**Future issuances of equity securities could dilute the ownership of our existing shareholders.**

We may issue additional shares of common stock or securities convertible into common stock in the future, including in connection with acquisitions, financings, or our equity-based compensation and employee stock purchase plans. Any such issuance could dilute the ownership interests of existing shareholders and could adversely affect the market price of our common stock. Sales of substantial amounts of our common stock in the public market, or the perception that such sales could occur, could also depress the market price of our common stock.

## General Risk Factors

**Public health crises, pandemics, and outbreaks of contagious disease could adversely affect our business.**

Public health crises, including pandemics and outbreaks of influenza or other contagious diseases, could significantly disrupt our operations. Such events could result in government-mandated restrictions on dine-in service, capacity limitations, or temporary closures. They could also reduce guest willingness to dine in restaurants, cause staffing shortages due to illness or quarantine requirements, disrupt our supply chain, and increase costs for sanitation, protective equipment, and safety measures. Our full-service brands, which depend on dine-in traffic, private dining, and corporate entertainment, would be particularly vulnerable to restrictions on in-person dining. Public health crises could also adversely affect the economy, consumer confidence, tourism in our markets, and the financial condition of our franchisees, suppliers, and landlords. The extent and duration of any such effects would be difficult to predict.

**Geopolitical events, trade policies, and tariffs could increase our costs and disrupt our supply chain.**

Changes in trade policies, including the imposition of tariffs, quotas, or other trade restrictions, could increase the cost of imported products we purchase, such as certain seafood, wine, spirits, specialty ingredients, kitchen equipment, furniture, smallwares, packaging, and construction materials. Retaliatory tariffs imposed by other countries could affect the export markets for U.S. agricultural products, causing price volatility in domestic commodity markets. Geopolitical events, including armed conflicts and political instability, could disrupt global supply chains, increase energy and commodity prices, and adversely affect consumer confidence. These developments could also affect our international franchise partners. We may be unable to offset increased costs through menu pricing or sourcing changes.

**Acts of violence, terrorism, or civil unrest at or near our restaurants could adversely affect our business.**

Our restaurants are public places, and incidents of violence, crime, terrorism, or civil unrest at or near our restaurants could harm guests and team members, result in liability claims, damage property, and reduce guest traffic. Such incidents could also generate adverse publicity that harms our brands. Concerns about safety in particular areas, including urban locations where certain of our restaurants operate, could reduce traffic. We may be required to increase security expenditures. Our insurance may not cover all losses resulting from such incidents.

**Our business could be affected by the actions of activist shareholders.**

Activist shareholders may seek to influence our strategy, capital allocation, governance, or Board composition, including through proxy contests, public campaigns, or demands for strategic transactions. Responding to activist actions can be costly and time-consuming and can divert the attention of our Board and management from our business. Perceived uncertainty about our future direction could disrupt relationships with team members, franchisees, suppliers, and landlords, make it more difficult to attract and retain qualified personnel, and cause fluctuations in the market price of our common stock.

**Failure to meet stakeholder expectations regarding corporate governance and responsibility could harm our reputation.**

Investors, proxy advisory firms, guests, team members, and regulators increasingly evaluate companies based on their governance practices, human capital management, and corporate responsibility efforts. Standards and expectations in these areas continue to evolve and may vary among stakeholders. If our practices or disclosures do not meet the expectations of investors or other stakeholders, our reputation and our ability to attract investment, team members, and guests could be adversely affected. Meeting these expectations may also require additional investment and management attention. In addition, new or expanded disclosure requirements relating to these matters could increase our compliance costs and legal exposure.

**Changes in the legal and regulatory environment generally, and in the political climate, could create uncertainty and adversely affect our business.**

Our business is affected by the policies of federal, state, and local governments, which can change as a result of elections, legislative action, regulatory initiatives, and judicial decisions. Changes in policies affecting minimum wages, tip credits, health care, immigration, taxes, trade, labor relations, privacy, environmental protection, and alcoholic beverages could each have significant effects on our costs and operations. Uncertainty about the direction of policy in these areas can make planning difficult and may affect consumer confidence and spending. We cannot predict the nature or timing of future policy changes or their effects on our business, financial condition, or results of operations.
