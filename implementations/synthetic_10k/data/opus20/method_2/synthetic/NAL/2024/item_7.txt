## Overview

### Our Business

Amberwood Partners Co. is a multi-brand restaurant company headquartered in Florida. We own, operate, and franchise restaurants across the casual dining, fine dining, and fast-casual categories of the restaurant industry, which is part of the broader Consumer Discretionary sector. Our portfolio consists of six brands:

- **Amberwood Grill**, our flagship polished-casual brand, known for American grill classics prepared with a wood-fire emphasis;
- **Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen**, an approachable seafood destination that celebrates the coastal cooking traditions of the American South and the Gulf and Atlantic shores;
- **Ventana Trattoria**, a full-service Italian-inspired brand featuring house-made pastas, wood-oven pizzas, and a robust catering and large-format carryout program;
- **Ironbark Chophouse**, our fine dining brand, focused on prime and premium steaks, seafood, an extensive wine program, and private dining;
- **Greenhollow Bowls**, our fast-casual brand, offering customizable grain bowls, salads, and wraps, with a high proportion of digital and off-premise sales;
- **Daybreak Porch**, our newest full-service brand, which serves breakfast, brunch, and lunch and is in a development phase.

We manage and report our business through three reportable segments:

- **Casual Dining**, which includes Amberwood Grill, Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen, and Ventana Trattoria;
- **Fine Dining**, which includes Ironbark Chophouse;
- **Fast Casual and Emerging Brands**, which includes Greenhollow Bowls and Daybreak Porch.

Revenues from franchising and licensing activities are included within the segment of the brand to which they relate. Certain corporate costs, including those associated with our Restaurant Support Center in Florida, are not allocated to segments.

Our revenues consist primarily of food and beverage sales at company-operated restaurants. To a lesser extent, they also include:

- royalties, development fees, and initial franchise fees from franchised and licensed restaurants;
- revenue from gift cards, including breakage;
- royalties from branded consumer products sold under licensing arrangements;
- certain other ancillary sources.

Our operating model pairs brand-level autonomy over culinary direction, guest experience, and local marketing with a shared corporate platform. That platform provides purchasing scale, common technology, centralized supply chain management, and shared-service functions. We believe this combination allows each brand to maintain a distinct identity while benefiting from the efficiencies of a larger organization.

### Operating Environment

The restaurant industry is intensely competitive, fragmented, and sensitive to changes in consumer discretionary spending. During fiscal 2024, our business was shaped by several broad conditions that we expect to remain relevant in future periods.

**Consumer spending and value perceptions.** Dining away from home is a discretionary purchase. Guests in every economic environment are attentive to value, which we define as the relationship between the quality of the overall experience and the price paid. Our brands span a range of price points and occasions, from weekday fast-casual lunches at Greenhollow Bowls to special-occasion dinners at Ironbark Chophouse. We believe this diversity gives us some resilience when spending patterns shift among categories. However, our full-service brands generally carry higher price points than quick-service and many fast-casual competitors. As a result, they may be more exposed to trade-down behavior when consumers become cautious.

**Pricing discipline.** We continue to take a disciplined approach to menu pricing. We weigh inflation in our cost structure against competitive pricing in local markets, guest sensitivity to price changes, and the importance of protecting traffic over the long term. Across our brands, we generally favor everyday value and abundant portions over deep discounting. We use pricing analytics, menu-mix engineering, and targeted promotional offers to balance margin objectives with guest perceptions of value.

**Labor.** Labor is one of our largest operating costs. A substantial majority of our team members are hourly restaurant employees, and many are paid at rates related to applicable minimum wages. Scheduled minimum wage increases in several states and localities where we operate, together with competition for hourly workers and experienced managers, continue to create upward pressure on wages. We seek to offset these pressures through the following:

- labor scheduling tools that use forecasts of guest demand;
- menu simplification that improves kitchen efficiency;
- retention efforts that reduce recruiting and training costs;
- our managing partner program, which aligns restaurant leaders' compensation with restaurant performance.

**Commodities.** Many of the products we purchase are subject to price volatility. These include beef, poultry, pork, seafood, produce, dairy, grains, cooking oils, and beverages. Amberwood Grill and Ironbark Chophouse have significant exposure to beef, including premium and prime grades. Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen has significant exposure to seafood. We manage commodity cost risk through the following:

- fixed-price and formula-based purchase contracts;
- forward purchase commitments;
- menu engineering and specification adjustments;
- pricing actions, in some cases.

We generally do not use financial instruments to hedge commodity prices.

**Occupancy, insurance, and utilities.** Because we lease the majority of our restaurant properties, occupancy costs are a significant and largely fixed component of our cost structure. Insurance costs, particularly property coverage for wind and flood damage in Florida and other coastal markets, have become more costly and, in some cases, more limited in availability. Utility costs are also significant, especially air conditioning loads in our Florida and southeastern restaurants.

**Weather.** Our headquarters and many of our restaurants are located in Florida and other coastal and southeastern markets exposed to hurricanes, tropical storms, flooding, and extreme heat. These events are more common during the Atlantic hurricane season in the late summer and fall. They can cause restaurant closures, property damage, supply disruptions, and reduced guest traffic. We maintain business continuity plans designed to mitigate these effects, including pre-positioning critical supplies ahead of forecasted storms and coordinating recovery logistics with our distributors.

**Digital and off-premise demand.** Off-premise dining has become a significant and durable part of our business. Guests increasingly expect to order, pay, reserve tables, and join waitlists through digital channels. Off-premise orders, particularly those fulfilled through third-party delivery marketplaces, carry different margin profiles than dine-in sales because of commissions, packaging, and other costs. We encourage guests to use our brand-owned channels, which generally provide better economics and direct access to guest data.

### Strategic Priorities

Our long-term strategy, described in Item 1, is designed to generate sustainable, profitable growth and attractive returns on invested capital. During fiscal 2024, management's priorities continued to center on the elements of that strategy:

- **Delivering consistently excellent guest experiences** through execution of the fundamentals in our restaurants: properly prepared food, attentive service, clean dining rooms, and accurate off-premise orders.
- **Strengthening our brands through differentiation**, ensuring that each concept remains relevant to its target guests and that our brands do not compete with one another for the same occasion.
- **Balancing value and pricing discipline**, favoring everyday value over deep discounting.
- **Leveraging our scale and shared platform**, including consolidated purchasing, common technology systems, and unified data and analytics capabilities.
- **Expanding our digital and off-premise capabilities**, including brand-owned ordering platforms, loyalty programs, and operational enhancements that support carryout and curbside volumes.
- **Growing our restaurant base prudently**, applying rigorous site selection and return thresholds to new restaurants, remodels, and relocations, and pursuing franchising and licensing where partnering with experienced operators is the most effective path.
- **Investing in our people**, through competitive pay and benefits, training, and clear paths for advancement.
- **Allocating capital with discipline**, balancing investment in growth, reinvestment in existing restaurants, maintenance of a sound balance sheet, and the return of capital to shareholders.

## How We Evaluate Our Performance

Management uses a variety of financial and operating measures to evaluate the performance of our business, allocate resources among our brands, and make decisions about restaurant development and capital allocation. Some of these measures are presented in our consolidated financial statements. Others are operating metrics or non-GAAP financial measures. The measures most important to management are described below.

### Revenues

Revenues are the primary indicator of the demand for our brands and the scale of our operations. We monitor revenues at the consolidated, segment, brand, and individual restaurant levels. Changes in revenues are driven principally by the following:

- changes in sales at existing restaurants;
- the opening of new restaurants and the closure of existing restaurants;
- changes in the number and performance of franchised and licensed restaurants, which affect royalty and fee revenues;
- gift card breakage and other ancillary revenues.

### Comparable Restaurant Sales

Comparable restaurant sales measure the change in sales at restaurants that have been open for a defined period, generally a period long enough for a restaurant to move beyond its initial opening period. This measure excludes the effects of new restaurant openings and closures. Management considers it a key indicator of the health of our existing restaurant base and of the effectiveness of our menu, pricing, marketing, and operational initiatives. Comparable restaurant sales are affected by changes in guest traffic and in average check.

### Guest Traffic and Average Check

We analyze changes in comparable restaurant sales by separating them into two components:

- **Guest traffic** is the change in the number of guests or transactions.
- **Average check** is the change in the average amount spent per guest or transaction.

Average check is influenced by menu pricing, menu mix, beverage attachment, and the mix of dine-in and off-premise sales. Management places significant weight on guest traffic as an indicator of guest loyalty and of guest perceptions of value. Sustained traffic growth is generally a stronger foundation for long-term performance than check growth driven primarily by pricing.

### Restaurant-Level Operating Costs

We monitor restaurant-level costs as a percentage of sales. These costs include the following:

- food and beverage costs;
- restaurant labor and benefits, including managing partner compensation;
- occupancy costs;
- utilities and repairs and maintenance;
- third-party delivery commissions and other restaurant operating expenses.

These measures help management evaluate the effectiveness of our purchasing, pricing, labor scheduling, and cost control programs, and they are an important component of the performance evaluations and incentive compensation of restaurant and field leaders.

### Operating Income and Operating Margin

Operating income and operating margin, which is operating income as a percentage of revenues, reflect the combined effect of our revenues, restaurant-level costs, general and administrative costs, depreciation and amortization, and other operating items. They are the primary measures of our consolidated profitability before income taxes and non-operating items.

### EBITDA

EBITDA is a non-GAAP measure that we define as net income before interest expense, income tax expense, and depreciation and amortization. Management uses EBITDA to evaluate operating performance on a basis that excludes the effects of our capital structure, tax position, and the depreciation of our restaurant asset base. EBITDA is defined and reconciled to net income under "Non-GAAP Financial Measures" below.

### Cash from Operations and Free Cash Flow

Cash from operations measures the cash generated by our business. Free cash flow is a non-GAAP measure that we define as cash from operations less capital expenditures. It measures the cash available after reinvestment in our restaurant base for the following uses:

- returning capital to shareholders;
- making other investments;
- reducing indebtedness;
- building liquidity.

Free cash flow is defined and reconciled under "Non-GAAP Financial Measures" below.

### Restaurant Development and Returns

We monitor the number of new restaurant openings, remodels, relocations, and closures, and we evaluate the actual performance of new and remodeled restaurants against the sales and return assumptions approved by our real estate committee. These reviews inform our future site selection, prototype design, and capital allocation decisions. We also evaluate the performance of our franchise and licensing arrangements, including royalty revenues and the development activity of our franchise partners.

### Revenues

Revenues for fiscal 2024 were $11,063,613,016. Our revenues consist primarily of food and beverage sales at company-operated restaurants across our six brands. They also include, to a lesser extent, franchise royalties and fees, gift card breakage, licensing royalties from branded consumer products, and other ancillary revenues.

Sales at company-operated restaurants are recognized when guests pay for their meals, whether dine-in or off-premise. Revenues from gift cards are deferred when the cards are sold and recognized when the cards are redeemed. In addition, we recognize breakage on the portion of gift cards that we expect will not be redeemed, in proportion to the pattern of redemptions by guests, to the extent that we are not required to remit unredeemed balances to governmental authorities. Franchise royalties are recognized as the underlying franchisee sales occur. Initial franchise fees and development fees are generally recognized over the term of the related franchise agreements. Our revenue recognition policies are described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

Several factors influenced our revenues during fiscal 2024 and will continue to influence them in future periods:

- **Guest traffic and average check.** Restaurant sales are driven by the number of guests we serve and the amount each guest spends. Our pricing decisions consider cost inflation, competitive pricing, guest price sensitivity, and the long-term protection of traffic. Average check is also affected by menu mix, appetizer and dessert attachment, and beverage sales, including alcoholic beverages at our full-service brands.
- **Channel mix.** Off-premise channels have become a significant and durable part of our business. These channels include carryout, curbside pickup, delivery, and catering. Greenhollow Bowls has a high proportion of digital and off-premise sales, and Ventana Trattoria offers a robust catering and large-format carryout program. Off-premise orders contribute to revenues but carry different cost structures than dine-in sales.
- **Restaurant development.** New restaurants contribute revenues in their opening periods but typically take time to reach the sales volumes of our mature restaurants. Restaurant closures reduce revenues but can improve overall profitability and returns.
- **Franchising and licensing.** Royalty and fee revenues depend on the sales of our franchisees and licensees, on the number of franchised restaurants, and on development activity under area development and master franchise agreements.
- **Gift cards and loyalty.** Gift card sales are concentrated in the holiday season, and their redemption typically benefits restaurant sales in subsequent months. Our loyalty programs and cross-brand recognition program are designed to encourage repeat visits and exploration of other brands within our portfolio.
- **Seasonality and weather.** Sales tend to be higher during certain holiday periods and during periods of favorable weather, and lower when severe weather reduces traffic or forces temporary closures. Markets with significant tourist activity fluctuate with seasonal travel patterns. Fine dining sales are influenced by the timing of corporate entertainment activity, including the holiday party season.

Information about revenues by reportable segment, and about the disaggregation of revenues by source, is presented in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

### Operating Expenses

Other operating expenses for fiscal 2024 were $9,817,468,547, or approximately 88.7% of revenues. This caption encompasses all of our operating costs, including the following:

- **Food and beverage costs.** The cost of the food, beverages, and related supplies used in preparing and serving meals, net of supplier rebates and allowances. These costs are affected by commodity prices, menu mix, portion sizes, waste, and the effectiveness of our purchasing programs.
- **Restaurant labor.** Wages, payroll taxes, team member benefits, incentive compensation, and managing partner compensation for restaurant team members and managers. These costs are affected by minimum wage and tip credit rules, competitive wage pressure, staffing levels, team member turnover, overtime, and health care costs, including the costs of our self-insured health plans.
- **Restaurant operating expenses.** Occupancy costs, including rent, property taxes, and common area maintenance; utilities; repairs and maintenance; insurance, including self-insured workers' compensation and general liability; credit card processing fees; third-party delivery commissions; packaging; restaurant supplies; and local marketing.
- **Marketing.** Brand-level and portfolio-wide advertising and promotional costs, including digital and social media advertising, streaming and connected television, and loyalty program costs.
- **General and administrative.** The costs of our Restaurant Support Center and field leadership organization, including corporate salaries and benefits, equity-based compensation, professional fees, technology costs, and other corporate expenses.
- **Depreciation and amortization.** The depreciation of buildings, leasehold improvements, equipment, and technology assets, and the amortization of definite-lived intangible assets.
- **Other operating items.** Pre-opening costs for new restaurants, which include the costs of opening teams and training; asset impairment and restaurant closure costs; and other gains and losses.

Depreciation and amortization reflects the capital we have invested in new restaurants, remodels, relocations, kitchen upgrades, and technology over time. We expect it to continue to reflect the level of our capital investment program.

The relationship between our operating expenses and revenues is influenced by the operating leverage inherent in our business. A substantial portion of our restaurant-level costs are fixed or semi-fixed in the short term, including:

- occupancy;
- management salaries;
- depreciation;
- a portion of hourly labor.

As a result, changes in sales tend to have a proportionately larger effect on our operating margins. When sales increase, fixed costs are spread over a larger base and margins generally expand. When sales decline, margins generally contract more than proportionately. Management therefore focuses on traffic-driven sales growth, disciplined labor scheduling, and the control of semi-variable costs as levers for protecting margins.

Our cost structure is also affected by the mix of our business. Food and beverage cost profiles differ among our brands. Beef and seafood are significant inputs at Amberwood Grill, Ironbark Chophouse, and Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen, while Greenhollow Bowls relies more heavily on produce, grains, and responsibly sourced proteins. Labor models also differ. Our full-service brands employ tipped positions such as servers and bartenders, and Ironbark Chophouse requires a more specialized workforce. Our fast-casual brand operates with cross-trained crew members in a counter-service format. Off-premise sales generally carry incremental packaging costs and, when fulfilled through third-party delivery marketplaces, commission costs. In-restaurant labor requirements for off-premise orders also differ from those for dine-in service.

### Operating Income

Management believes our operating margin reflects several aspects of our business model:

- the contribution of franchise and licensing royalties, which carry limited associated operating costs;
- the disciplines embodied in "the Amberwood Standard," including disciplined cost management.

These benefits are partially offset by inflationary pressures on labor, insurance, and other costs. They are also partially offset by our continuing investment in technology, training, and guest experience initiatives.

### Income Taxes

### Segment Discussion

We evaluate the performance of our reportable segments using segment revenues and segment profitability measures that our chief operating decision maker uses to assess performance and allocate resources. Segment financial information, including revenues and segment profitability, is presented in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8. The discussion below describes the principal factors that influence the results of each segment.

#### Casual Dining

Our Casual Dining segment includes Amberwood Grill, Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen, and Ventana Trattoria. These brands serve a broad guest base, including families, couples, small business groups, and guests celebrating occasions. They offer full bar service and meaningful off-premise businesses.

The principal factors that influence the segment's results include the following:

- **Traffic and value perceptions.** Casual dining competes for guests with a wide range of alternatives, from fast-casual restaurants to independent full-service restaurants to prepared meals from grocery and convenience retailers. Our casual dining brands emphasize everyday value, generous portions, and seasonal features to support traffic, rather than relying on deep discounting.
- **Commodity costs.** Amberwood Grill's menu of hand-cut steaks, rotisserie chicken, and fresh fish, together with its use of hardwood for cooking, exposes it to beef, poultry, seafood, and hardwood costs. Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen is significantly exposed to seafood prices and availability. Those factors are affected by fishery management decisions, harvest restrictions, environmental events such as red tide, water temperature changes, and import tariffs. Ventana Trattoria's menu relies on flour, dairy, produce, and proteins.
- **Labor.** The segment's full-service model employs a large number of tipped team members. That makes it sensitive to changes in minimum wage and tip credit rules, as well as to competition for servers, bartenders, and kitchen staff.
- **Off-premise and catering.** Amberwood Grill's dedicated carryout areas and curbside pickup, and Ventana Trattoria's catering and large-format carryout program, support sales beyond the dining room. Off-premise mix affects packaging costs, labor deployment, and, for delivery orders fulfilled through marketplaces, commission costs.
- **Geography and weather.** Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen reflects our Florida heritage, and our casual dining brands have a significant presence in the southeastern United States. The segment is therefore exposed to hurricanes, tropical storms, and seasonal tourism patterns in that region.
- **Alcoholic beverages.** Craft cocktails, regional beers, and wine contribute to average check and margins. Changes in alcohol consumption patterns could affect the segment's results, partially offset by our expanding selection of non-alcoholic and low-alcohol beverages.

#### Fine Dining

Our Fine Dining segment consists of Ironbark Chophouse. Ironbark offers prime and premium steaks, chops, seafood, and an extensive wine program in an upscale setting, and its private dining business is an important component of its sales.

The principal factors that influence the segment's results include the following:

- **Corporate entertainment and higher-income spending.** Ironbark's results are more sensitive than those of our other brands to corporate entertainment budgets and to the discretionary spending of higher-income households. Private dining demand depends heavily on corporate events, client entertainment, and holiday party activity. Spending by higher-income households can be affected by changes in equity markets and other asset values, even when broader economic indicators are stable.
- **High average check.** Because Ironbark's check average is substantially higher than that of our other brands, changes in its traffic can have an outsized effect on segment profitability.
- **Premium beef and wine.** Ironbark purchases substantial quantities of prime and premium beef, whose prices can be highly volatile. It also carries significant wine inventories, which contribute to our consolidated inventory balance and require careful management of purchasing, storage, and turnover.
- **Specialized workforce.** Ironbark depends on experienced culinary professionals, sommeliers, and wine stewards, whose skills are in limited supply. Its operating model makes it more difficult to reduce costs quickly when sales decline.
- **Seasonality.** Fine dining sales are influenced by the timing of corporate entertainment activity, including the holiday party season.

#### Fast Casual and Emerging Brands

Our Fast Casual and Emerging Brands segment includes Greenhollow Bowls and Daybreak Porch. These brands are at earlier stages of growth than our established casual dining brands.

The principal factors that influence the segment's results include the following:

- **Digital and off-premise sales at Greenhollow Bowls.** Greenhollow Bowls has a high proportion of digital and off-premise sales. Its newer restaurants include dedicated digital pickup shelves and, in certain formats, drive-up pickup lanes. Its results are therefore sensitive to the performance of our digital platforms, to the economics of third-party delivery relationships, and to weekday lunch demand.
- **Real estate flexibility.** Greenhollow Bowls' smaller footprint and lower build-out costs relative to our full-service concepts allow it to enter a variety of real estate settings, including urban storefronts, suburban end-caps, mixed-use developments, and non-traditional venues such as office campuses and universities. We evaluate new locations against return thresholds that reflect this lower investment profile.
- **Development-phase economics at Daybreak Porch.** Daybreak Porch is in a development phase in which we are refining its operating model and prototype design. Its daytime-only hours concentrate sales into a narrower window. That limits its ability to absorb fixed occupancy costs, but we believe it also supports team member recruitment and retention. We are expanding Daybreak Porch selectively, and its results may reflect pre-opening costs and the initial operating inefficiencies associated with new restaurants.
- **Investment in growth.** As we invest in these brands, the segment's results may reflect a higher proportion of new restaurants, which typically incur higher operating costs during their opening periods and take time to reach mature sales volumes and margins.

## Liquidity and Capital Resources

Our principal sources of liquidity are cash generated from operations, cash and cash equivalents on hand, and access to our credit arrangements. Our principal uses of cash are the following:

- operating expenses, including food and beverage purchases, labor, and occupancy costs;
- capital expenditures for new restaurants, remodels, relocations, kitchen upgrades, and technology;
- other investing activities;
- dividends and share repurchases;

Like many restaurant companies, we operate with low levels of accounts receivable and inventory relative to our sales. Management believes that our cash on hand, our expected cash flows from operations, and our access to credit arrangements will be sufficient to fund our operating needs, planned capital expenditures, lease and debt obligations, and anticipated dividends for at least the next twelve months and for the foreseeable future thereafter. However, as discussed in Item 1A, our liquidity could be affected by the following:

- declines in consumer spending;
- significant cost increases;
- severe weather events affecting our southeastern markets;
- disruptions in the credit markets;
- other adverse developments.

### Capital Allocation Framework

Our capital allocation decisions are guided by expected returns, strategic fit, and the preservation of financial flexibility to withstand periods of economic uncertainty. Our Board of Directors periodically reviews our capital allocation priorities with management. Our framework generally prioritizes the uses of capital as follows:

1. **Reinvestment in our existing restaurants and infrastructure.** Maintaining the appeal, safety, and operational effectiveness of our restaurants is essential to protecting our brands and sales. This includes remodels, refreshes, kitchen upgrades, technology, and weather-hardening investments in coastal markets.
2. **Disciplined new restaurant development.** We invest in new restaurants that meet return thresholds reflecting the investment required and the risk profile of the site and market. We also pursue franchising and licensing where partnering with experienced operators is the most effective path to growth with limited capital investment by the Company.
3. **Maintenance of a sound balance sheet.** We seek to maintain liquidity and a capital structure that preserves our access to financing and our ability to withstand adverse developments, including severe weather events and economic downturns.
4. **Return of capital to shareholders.** We return capital through dividends and share repurchases, with the level of returns calibrated to our cash generation, investment needs, and balance sheet objectives.

During fiscal 2024, capital returned to shareholders through dividends and share repurchases totaled $990,067,970. That represented approximately 88.5% of net income and approximately 99.5% of free cash flow. Management believes this level of capital return was consistent with our framework, given the cash-generating characteristics of our restaurant base. Our capacity to return capital in future periods will depend on the following:

## Seasonality, Inflation, and Other Factors

### Seasonality and Quarterly Fluctuations

Our sales and operating results fluctuate from quarter to quarter due to seasonal patterns, holidays, weather, and the timing of restaurant openings and closures. The principal seasonal influences on our business include the following:

- **Holidays.** Sales tend to be higher during certain holiday periods, and calendar shifts in the timing of holidays can move sales between quarters.
- **Weather.** Sales are generally higher during periods of favorable weather and lower when severe weather affects guest traffic or causes temporary restaurant closures. Hurricanes and tropical storms are more common during the Atlantic hurricane season in the late summer and fall, and they disproportionately affect our Florida and southeastern markets. Winter storms can affect restaurants in other regions.
- **Tourism.** Sales in markets with significant tourist activity fluctuate with seasonal travel patterns.
- **Gift cards.** Gift card sales are concentrated in the holiday season, and the redemption of those cards typically benefits sales in subsequent months.
- **Corporate entertainment.** Fine dining sales at Ironbark Chophouse are influenced by the timing of corporate entertainment activity, including the holiday party season.

Other factors that can cause our quarterly results to fluctuate include the following:

- changes in commodity and labor costs;
- pre-opening costs and initial operating inefficiencies of new restaurants;
- impairment charges and restaurant closure costs;
- changes in self-insurance reserves;
- the timing of marketing and promotional activities;
- legal settlements and other non-recurring items.

As a result, results for any interim period are not necessarily indicative of results for a full fiscal year.

### Effects of Inflation

Inflation affects many components of our cost structure, including the following:

- food and beverage costs;
- wages and benefits;
- occupancy costs;
- utilities;
- insurance;
- packaging;
- construction and equipment;
- technology.

We seek to mitigate the effects of inflation through purchasing programs, menu engineering, labor productivity initiatives, and, where appropriate, menu price increases. Our ability to offset inflation through pricing is limited by competitive conditions and by guest sensitivity to price changes. We consider the importance of protecting traffic over the long term when making pricing decisions, and we generally favor everyday value over deep discounting.

Several inflationary factors deserve particular mention:

- **Labor.** Scheduled minimum wage increases in several states and localities where we operate directly affect our labor costs. They also tend to create upward pressure on wages for team members paid above the minimum. Changes to tip credit rules could significantly affect our full-service brands.
- **Insurance.** Property insurance costs, particularly for wind and flood coverage in Florida and other coastal markets, have risen significantly. Increases in property taxes and insurance costs passed through by landlords can also increase our occupancy costs.
- **Commodities.** Beef prices, which are significant for Amberwood Grill and Ironbark Chophouse, can be highly volatile and are affected by cattle herd cycles, drought, and export demand. Seafood prices, which are significant for Saltmarsh Seafood Kitchen, are affected by fishery management, environmental events, and trade conditions.
- **Construction.** Increases in construction, equipment, and labor costs, including as a result of tariffs, can raise the investment required for new restaurants and remodels and affect the returns on those investments.

### Franchising and Licensing Considerations

Our franchise and licensing arrangements contribute royalty and fee revenues with limited associated capital investment by the Company. Royalty revenues depend on the sales and financial health of our franchisees and licensees, which in turn are affected by many of the same factors that affect our company-operated restaurants. International franchise royalties are also affected by foreign economic conditions and currency fluctuations. Franchisee financial difficulties could result in lower revenues and bad debt expense. We monitor the performance of our franchise system and the creditworthiness of our franchisees as part of our ongoing management of these arrangements.

Actual results could differ materially from those estimates.

We consider an accounting estimate to be critical if both of the following are true:

- it requires management to make assumptions about matters that are highly uncertain at the time the estimate is made;
- different estimates that could reasonably have been used, or changes in the estimate that are reasonably likely to occur, could have a material effect on our financial condition or results of operations.

Management has discussed the development, selection, and disclosure of our critical accounting estimates with the Audit Committee of our Board of Directors. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

### Impairment of Long-Lived Assets and Operating Lease Right-of-Use Assets

**Description.** We review our long-lived assets, including property and equipment and operating lease right-of-use assets, for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a restaurant or group of restaurants may not be recoverable. We generally evaluate these assets at the individual restaurant level, which is the lowest level at which identifiable cash flows are largely independent of the cash flows of other assets. Indicators of potential impairment include the following:

- a history of operating losses or negative cash flows at a restaurant;
- a significant decline in a restaurant's sales or profitability;
- adverse changes in the trade area or business climate;
- a decision to close or relocate a restaurant before the end of its previously estimated useful life.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** When indicators are present, we compare the carrying value of the restaurant's assets to the estimated undiscounted future cash flows expected to result from their use and eventual disposition. If the carrying value exceeds the undiscounted cash flows, we measure an impairment loss as the amount by which the carrying value exceeds the estimated fair value. Fair value is generally determined using discounted cash flow models or, for right-of-use assets, estimates of market rents. These analyses require significant judgments about the following:

- future sales and margins;
- the remaining useful lives of the assets;
- the likelihood and timing of lease renewals;
- market rents;
- appropriate discount rates.

**Sensitivity.** Because other assets, which include property and equipment and right-of-use assets, represented approximately 82.7% of our total assets at the end of fiscal 2024, changes in these estimates could result in material impairment charges. New restaurants, restaurants in new markets, and restaurants of our emerging brands may be particularly sensitive to changes in assumptions, because their operating histories are shorter and their sales trajectories are less certain. Impairment charges reduce our reported earnings and stockholders' equity, and, as discussed above, our stockholders' equity represents a relatively small portion of our total assets.

### Goodwill and Indefinite-Lived Intangible Assets

**Description.** Goodwill was $1,293,700,944 at the end of fiscal 2024, and intangible assets, net, were $15,432,858. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level at least annually, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the fair value of a reporting unit may be below its carrying value. We test indefinite-lived intangible assets, including trademarks associated with acquired brands, for impairment at least annually and more frequently when indicators arise.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** We may first assess qualitative factors to determine whether it is more likely than not that the fair value of a reporting unit or intangible asset is less than its carrying value. If we bypass the qualitative assessment, or if it indicates potential impairment, we perform a quantitative test. In that test, we estimate fair value using income-based approaches, such as discounted cash flow models, market-based approaches, or a combination of both. Trademarks are typically valued using a relief-from-royalty method. These valuations require significant judgments about the following:

- future sales growth and margins;
- capital expenditure requirements;
- terminal growth rates;
- royalty rates;
- discount rates that reflect the risks of the reporting unit or asset;
- comparable company multiples.

**Sensitivity.** Goodwill represented approximately 61.7% of our total stockholders' equity at the end of fiscal 2024. To illustrate the potential significance of this estimate, a hypothetical impairment of 10% of our goodwill balance would have been $129,370,094. That would have represented approximately 6.2% of total stockholders' equity and approximately 11.6% of fiscal 2024 net income. This illustration is not an indication that an impairment is likely. Factors that could lead to impairment include the following:

- declines in the performance or outlook of a brand or segment;
- adverse changes in the business climate;
- increases in discount rates resulting from higher interest rates or changes in risk premiums;
- sustained declines in our market capitalization.

Ironbark Chophouse, whose results are sensitive to corporate entertainment spending and to the spending of higher-income households, and our emerging brands, whose unit economics are still being refined, present particular sources of estimation uncertainty.

### Self-Insurance Liabilities

**Description.** We retain a significant portion of expected losses under our workers' compensation, general liability, and team member health benefit programs through self-insurance or high-deductible arrangements. We obtain excess or stop-loss coverage to limit our exposure to large individual claims. We record liabilities for the estimated ultimate cost of claims incurred, including claims incurred but not yet reported.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** Our self-insurance liabilities are based on actuarial estimates that incorporate assumptions about the following:

- claim frequency and severity;
- loss development patterns;
- medical cost trends;
- legal and settlement trends;
- the effect of changes in our insurance program, including deductible and retention levels.

We engage third-party actuaries to assist us in developing these estimates, and we review them periodically.

**Sensitivity.** Actual losses may differ materially from our estimates. The following developments could require us to increase our reserves:

- unexpected increases in the number or severity of claims;
- adverse developments in pending claims;
- rising medical costs;
- "social inflation" in jury verdicts and settlements.

Workplace injury trends in our restaurants, our large hourly workforce, and the claims environment in the states where we operate, including Florida, are important influences on these estimates.

### Gift Card Breakage and Loyalty Program Obligations

**Description.** We sell gift cards for each of our brands, as well as a portfolio-wide gift card redeemable across participating brands. We record a liability when gift cards are sold and recognize revenue when cards are redeemed. We recognize breakage revenue on the portion of gift card balances that we expect will not be redeemed. Breakage is recognized in proportion to the pattern of redemptions by guests, to the extent that we are not required to remit unredeemed balances to governmental authorities under unclaimed property laws. Our loyalty programs give rise to obligations for rewards earned by members, for which we defer a portion of the related revenue until rewards are redeemed or expire.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** Breakage estimates are based on historical redemption patterns, which we analyze by brand and by distribution channel. These estimates require judgment about future redemption behavior and about the application of unclaimed property laws. Loyalty program estimates require judgment about the standalone selling price of rewards and the proportion of rewards that members will ultimately redeem.

**Sensitivity.** Changes in guest redemption behavior, in the mix of gift cards sold through different channels, in the terms of our loyalty programs, or in unclaimed property laws and their interpretation could affect the timing and amount of revenue we recognize. As discussed in Item 1A, state unclaimed property audits can be lengthy and costly and could require us to remit amounts to states.

### Leases

**Description.** We lease the majority of our restaurant properties. At lease commencement, we recognize a right-of-use asset and a lease liability based on the present value of the remaining lease payments over the lease term.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** Lease accounting requires judgment about the following:

- **The lease term.** This includes whether we are reasonably certain to exercise renewal options. Our leases typically include multiple renewal options, and our assessment of whether we will exercise them considers the economic incentives associated with leasehold improvements, the performance of the restaurant, and our strategic plans for the location.
- **The discount rate.** Because the rates implicit in our leases are generally not readily determinable, we use our incremental borrowing rate. That rate reflects the rate we would pay to borrow on a collateralized basis over a similar term.
- **Lease classification.** We must determine whether each lease is an operating lease or a finance lease.
- **Variable lease payments.** We must account for variable lease payments, including contingent rent based on sales.

**Sensitivity.** Different judgments about lease terms or discount rates would change the amounts of right-of-use assets and lease liabilities we recognize, which are significant components of our other assets and other liabilities. They would also change the pattern of lease expense we recognize. Changes in our assessment of lease terms, for example when we decide to close or relocate a restaurant, can result in remeasurement of lease liabilities and, in some cases, impairment of right-of-use assets.

### Income Taxes

**Description.** We are subject to income taxes in the United States and certain foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate for fiscal 2024 was approximately 10.2%, reflecting income tax expense of $126,925,860 on pretax income of $1,246,144,469.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** Determining our income tax expense requires significant judgment, including judgment about the following:

- the amount and availability of tax credits, including credits associated with FICA taxes paid on team member tip income;
- the realizability of deferred tax assets and the need for valuation allowances;
- the recognition and measurement of uncertain tax positions, which we recognize only when it is more likely than not that the position will be sustained upon examination by taxing authorities;
- the interpretation of tax laws in the jurisdictions where we operate.

**Sensitivity.** Changes in tax laws, including changes in corporate income tax rates, the treatment of tip income and related employer tax credits, and the deductibility of business meals, could significantly affect our effective tax rate. The outcome of tax audits could also differ from the amounts we have recorded. Because our effective tax rate is substantially affected by tax credits, changes in the availability or calculation of those credits could have a meaningful effect on our net income.

### Managing Partner and Incentive Compensation

**Description.** Under our managing partner program, eligible general managers participate in a share of the cash flow or profits of the restaurants they lead, in addition to their base compensation. In some cases, their participation is subject to multi-year commitments and other conditions. We also maintain incentive compensation programs for restaurant leaders, field leaders, and corporate team members, and we grant equity-based compensation to eligible leaders.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** Accounting for these arrangements requires judgment about the following:

- the measurement of restaurant-level cash flow or profits;
- the probability that participants will satisfy service and other conditions;
- the timing of expense recognition for arrangements with multi-year terms;
- for equity-based awards, the estimated fair value of awards and the probability that performance conditions will be achieved.

**Sensitivity.** Changes in restaurant performance, participant turnover, or our assessment of the probability of achieving performance conditions could affect the amount and timing of compensation expense we recognize.

### Legal Contingencies

**Description.** We are subject to a variety of lawsuits, claims, and governmental investigations in the ordinary course of business. These include the following:

- wage-and-hour class and collective actions;
- employment-related claims;
- premises liability claims;
- claims involving food or allergic reactions;
- accessibility claims;
- consumer protection claims;
- privacy claims.

We record accruals for legal matters when we determine that a loss is probable and the amount of the loss can be reasonably estimated.

**Judgments and uncertainties.** Assessing the probability and amount of potential losses requires significant judgment about the following:

- the merits of claims;
- the likely outcomes of litigation and settlement negotiations;
- the availability of insurance coverage.

**Sensitivity.** Because many legal matters are inherently uncertain, actual outcomes may differ materially from our accruals. An unfavorable resolution of one or more matters, or a significant settlement, could adversely affect our results of operations in the period in which it occurs.

## Closing Perspective

Fiscal 2024 demonstrated the cash-generating capacity of our multi-brand restaurant portfolio. We generated revenues of $11,063,613,016, operating income of $1,246,144,469, net income of $1,119,218,609, and cash from operations of $1,529,770,861. That cash enabled us to fund capital expenditures of $534,956,682 and other investing activities of $647,059,612. It also enabled us to return $990,067,970 to shareholders through dividends and share repurchases.

We ended the year with cash and cash equivalents of $174,174,725, long-term debt of $1,191,400,742, and total stockholders' equity of $2,098,428,814.

Looking ahead, management will continue to focus on the elements of our strategy:

- delivering consistently excellent guest experiences;
- strengthening the differentiation of our brands;
- balancing value with pricing discipline;
- leveraging our scale and shared platform;
- expanding our digital and off-premise capabilities;
- growing our restaurant base prudently;
- investing in our people;
- allocating capital with discipline.

We remain mindful of the risks described in Item 1A. These include our exposure to consumer discretionary spending, labor and commodity cost inflation, severe weather in our Florida and southeastern markets, and the substantial liabilities on our balance sheet. We intend to manage our business with the financial flexibility necessary to navigate those risks while pursuing attractive long-term returns for our shareholders.
