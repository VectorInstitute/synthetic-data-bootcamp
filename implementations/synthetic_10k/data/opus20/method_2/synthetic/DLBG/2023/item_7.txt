### Overview

#### Our Business

Ironwood is a financial holding company headquartered in Chicago, Illinois. Through our subsidiaries, we provide asset servicing, investment management, and wealth and fiduciary services to:

- institutional investors and financial intermediaries;
- corporations and public entities;
- endowments, foundations, and family offices; and
- high-net-worth individuals and families.

Our principal banking subsidiary is Ironwood Trust Bank (the "Bank"), an Illinois state-chartered bank with trust powers and a member of the Federal Reserve System. Our principal investment advisory subsidiary is Ironwood Asset Management LLC ("IAM"). We also operate Ironwood Securities LLC, a registered broker-dealer, and a network of non-U.S. subsidiaries and branches in Europe, the Middle East, and the Asia-Pacific region.

We manage our business through three reportable segments: Asset Servicing, Investment Management, and Wealth and Fiduciary Services. Activities not allocated to these segments are reported in Corporate and Other. These include certain corporate treasury functions, the results of investment securities not attributed to client deposits, and certain corporate expenses.

#### How We Generate Revenue

Our revenues come from two broad sources: client-related fees and the deployment of our balance sheet.

**Fee revenue.** Most of our fees are earned for custody, fund accounting and administration, transfer agency, middle office outsourcing, data and analytics, investment management, and wealth and fiduciary services. Many of these fees are calculated by reference to:

- the market value of assets under custody, administration, or management;
- transaction volumes;
- the number of accounts or funds serviced; or
- negotiated fixed fees.

As a result, fee revenue is sensitive to the level and volatility of global equity and fixed income markets, to currency exchange rates, and to client activity.

**Balance sheet and trading revenue.** Institutional clients hold operating cash with the Bank in connection with their custody and settlement activity. We invest these deposits primarily in high-quality, liquid assets, including central bank deposits, U.S. Treasury and agency securities, agency mortgage-backed securities, and highly rated non-U.S. sovereign and supranational securities. We also earn revenue from foreign exchange trading and securities lending.

Interest paid on client deposits and other funding is reported as interest expense. Its level depends on the interest rate environment, competitive deposit pricing, and the volume and mix of our interest-bearing liabilities.

#### Operating Environment in Fiscal 2023

Several conditions shaped our fiscal 2023 results.

**Interest rates.** Interest rates in the United States and in most major currencies stayed elevated throughout the year. The Federal Reserve continued to raise its policy rate during the first half of the year and then held it at a restrictive level. Higher short-term rates raised the yields available on floating-rate and newly purchased assets. They also raised the rates we paid on interest-bearing client deposits. In addition, clients' expectations about deposit pricing became more rate-sensitive across the industry, and many institutional investors moved cash balances into money market funds and other higher-yielding alternatives.

**Bank failures and the industry response.** The failures of several regional banks in early 2023 sharpened attention across the industry on uninsured deposits, unrealized losses on investment securities, liquidity risk management, and capital adequacy. We maintained our established practices throughout the period: a conservative investment portfolio, a substantial buffer of highly liquid assets, and close engagement with clients on their operating liquidity. These events also produced a series of regulatory proposals, described in Part I, Item 1, "Business—Supervision and Regulation." They include:

- the July 2023 proposal to implement the final components of the Basel III framework;
- the August 2023 proposals on long-term debt and insured depository institution resolution planning; and
- the November 2023 FDIC final rule imposing a special deposit insurance assessment.

**Markets.** Global equity markets generally ended the year higher, although with considerable volatility during the year. Fixed income markets were volatile as investors reassessed the path of monetary policy. Because much of our fee revenue depends on the value of client assets, both the level of markets during the year and the mix of asset classes our clients held influenced our fee revenue.

**Client activity and industry structure.** Several longer-term trends continued in our institutional client base:

- private markets continued to grow;
- ETFs continued to be adopted;
- asset managers continued to outsource operations; and
- clients continued to demand integrated data.

At the same time, fee pressure persisted in the custody of public market assets. Asset owners and asset managers continued to consolidate, and clients periodically ran competitive processes that led to fee renegotiation.

#### Management Priorities

In fiscal 2023, we continued to pursue the strategic priorities described in Part I, Item 1, "Business—Our Mission and Strategy":

- deepening relationships with core institutional clients;
- investing in technology and data platforms;
- growing our investment management franchise;
- expanding our wealth and fiduciary business;
- maintaining a strong balance sheet and disciplined risk management; and
- operating efficiently and responsibly.

We continued our multi-year program to modernize our core custody and accounting infrastructure. We also prepared for significant changes in market structure, including the shortening of the U.S. securities settlement cycle and new central clearing requirements for certain U.S. Treasury transactions. In managing the balance sheet, we prioritized liquidity and capital strength over short-term returns, consistent with our view that clients' confidence in our financial strength is fundamental to our franchise.

#### Total Revenues

Total revenues were $8,038.6 million in fiscal 2023, equal to 5.0% of year-end total assets. Our revenues come from the following principal activities.

**Asset servicing fees.** These are fees for custody and safekeeping, fund accounting and administration, transfer agency and shareholder services, ETF servicing, middle office outsourcing, and data, analytics, and reporting. They are the largest component of our fee revenue and are earned primarily in the Asset Servicing segment. The principal drivers in fiscal 2023 were:

- the market value of assets under custody and administration, which reflects the levels of equity and fixed income markets during the year;
- the onboarding of new client mandates, including fund administration mandates for private markets vehicles, which require extended implementation periods before revenue is fully realized;
- transaction volumes and the number of funds and accounts serviced; and
- the effect of contract renegotiations and pricing pressure on public market custody relationships.

The effect of currency exchange rates on non-U.S. dollar assets and revenues also influenced reported asset servicing fees.

**Investment management fees.** These are fees earned by IAM for managing assets across liquidity, fixed income, equity, index and factor, multi-asset, alternatives, and customized separately managed account strategies. They are calculated principally as a percentage of assets under management and, for certain mandates, include performance-based fees. The principal drivers were:

- market levels;
- net client flows; and
- the mix of assets managed among strategies with different fee rates.

During fiscal 2023, demand for our liquidity management strategies benefited from the elevated rate environment, as institutional and individual clients sought higher yields on cash balances. Industry-wide trends toward lower-cost index strategies and fee compression in active strategies continued to affect the fee rate on assets managed. Performance-based fees are recognized when contractual measurement periods conclude and can vary from period to period.

**Wealth and fiduciary fees.** These are fees for investment management and advice, wealth planning, trust and estate administration, family office services, and foundation and endowment services, earned in the Wealth and Fiduciary Services segment. Many of these fees are based on the market value of assets under management or administration. Others are fixed or activity-based, such as fees for estate settlement and trust administration. Results reflected market levels, client acquisition and retention, and the continuing contribution of our Midwest franchise, along with our efforts to expand in other markets.

**Foreign exchange trading revenue.** This is revenue from spot and forward foreign exchange transactions with clients executed on a principal basis, standing instruction programs, and currency overlay and hedging programs. It depends on currency volatility, client cross-border investment activity, and pricing. Industry-wide competitive and regulatory scrutiny of standing instruction pricing has intensified, and clients continue to migrate toward electronic and direct execution channels.

**Securities lending revenue.** This is our share of the fees earned in our agent lending program. It depends on borrower demand for specific securities, prevailing lending spreads, the volume of lendable assets, and the return on reinvested cash collateral. Demand for particular securities, and therefore securities lending revenue, can rise around dividend dates and during periods of heightened market activity.

**Balance sheet revenue and other income.** We earn revenue by investing client deposits and other funding in interest-earning assets, principally deposits with central banks and other banks, investment securities, and loans to private banking clients. Other income includes revenue from transition management, agency trading, and other services, as well as income from certain investments.

The elevated level of short-term interest rates in fiscal 2023 raised yields on our floating-rate assets and on securities purchased during the year. These benefits were offset in part by the effect of fixed-rate securities purchased in lower-rate periods, which continued to earn below-market yields until they matured or were reinvested.

#### Interest Expense

Interest expense was $4,823.2 million in fiscal 2023, equal to 60.0% of total revenues and 3.3% of year-end total liabilities of $146,655.0 million. It consists principally of interest paid on client deposits, together with interest on short-term borrowings and other funding.

Three factors drove the level of interest expense in fiscal 2023:

- **Rates paid on client deposits.** As policy rates rose and stayed elevated, we raised the rates paid on interest-bearing client deposits, particularly non-U.S. dollar and U.S. dollar deposits held by clients with significant cash balances. The share of rate increases passed through to deposit pricing, sometimes called the deposit "beta," rose across the industry during the year as institutional clients became more sensitive to rate differentials and compared deposit rates with money market fund yields and other alternatives.
- **Volume and composition of funding.** Some clients moved balances out of noninterest-bearing deposits into interest-bearing deposits or into off-balance sheet alternatives, including IAM liquidity strategies. This shifted our funding mix toward higher-cost liabilities. The net reduction in client deposits and other funding during the year, reflected in other financing activities in our consolidated statement of cash flows, also affected the average volume of interest-bearing liabilities.
- **Non-U.S. rate environments.** We hold deposits in several currencies, and interest expense also reflects the level of policy rates set by central banks outside the United States.

Interest expense is managed together with the yields on our interest-earning assets through our asset and liability management process, described under "Risk Management—Interest Rate Risk" and in Part II, Item 7A.

#### Cost Base and Expense Management

Several features of our cost structure shape how we manage expenses, even though this MD&A does not present a separate breakdown of operating expense line items.

**Fixed costs.** A large part of our cost base is relatively fixed in the near term. It includes the costs of our people, technology infrastructure, operations centers, and regulatory and compliance programs. Many of these costs do not fall in proportion to revenues when markets decline, which can magnify the effect of market movements on our margins.

**Non-cash charges.** Depreciation and amortization, a non-cash charge reflected in our results and added back in our consolidated statement of cash flows, was $1,087.0 million in fiscal 2023, equal to 13.5% of total revenues. It includes depreciation of premises and equipment, amortization of capitalized software developed or obtained for internal use, and other amortization, including amortization of premiums and discounts on investment securities.

**Expense priorities.** Our expense management focuses on:

- continuing to invest in our technology modernization program and in data, automation, and cybersecurity capabilities;
- improving productivity through process simplification, location strategy, and automation;
- managing third-party and occupancy costs; and
- maintaining the control environment and regulatory programs our clients and regulators expect.

**Regulatory cost pressures.** In November 2023, the FDIC adopted a final rule imposing a special assessment to recover losses to the Deposit Insurance Fund arising from the protection of uninsured depositors in connection with certain bank failures earlier in 2023. Because the assessment is based on each institution's uninsured deposits, and a substantial portion of the Bank's deposits are held by institutional clients and exceed insurance limits, the rule affects us more than institutions with predominantly insured deposits. We have reflected our estimate of the special assessment in our results in accordance with GAAP. The FDIC has indicated that the amount of the special assessment may be revised as estimated losses to the Deposit Insurance Fund are updated.

### Business Segment Discussion

#### Basis of Presentation

We report results for three business segments, Asset Servicing, Investment Management, and Wealth and Fiduciary Services, together with Corporate and Other. Segment results reflect management's methodologies for allocating revenues, expenses, and capital, and for transferring funds and their associated interest income and expense between the business segments and the corporate treasury function.

Under our funds transfer pricing methodology, segments that gather client deposits, principally Asset Servicing and Wealth and Fiduciary Services, receive credit for the value of those deposits. That value is based on their expected behavior and duration. Corporate and Other retains the results of managing the investment securities portfolio and the consolidated interest rate position.

These methodologies are periodically refined to reflect changes in our organization, products, and internal management reporting. Segment results are not necessarily comparable with similar information presented by other financial institutions. Revenues, income before income taxes, and other financial information for each segment, together with a reconciliation to consolidated amounts, are presented in the segment information Note to our Consolidated Financial Statements in Part II, Item 8.

#### Asset Servicing

Asset Servicing is our largest segment and the foundation of our institutional franchise. It provides custody, fund accounting and administration, transfer agency, ETF servicing, middle office outsourcing, data and analytics, securities lending, foreign exchange, and cash management services to asset owners and asset managers.

**Revenue drivers in fiscal 2023**

- **Market values of assets under custody and administration.** Custody and administration fees are frequently calculated by reference to asset values. Market levels during the year, the mix of equity and fixed income assets, and currency translation of non-U.S. dollar assets all affected segment fee revenue.
- **New business and implementations.** We continued to onboard new mandates, including fund administration and middle office mandates that involve multi-year implementation efforts. Revenue from these mandates builds over time as transitions are completed. Implementation costs are incurred in advance of the associated revenue, which affects segment margins in the periods in which they occur.
- **Private markets and alternatives.** Demand continued for administration of private equity, private credit, real estate, and hedge funds, including capital call and distribution processing, waterfall calculations, investor allocations, and loan administration. We view private markets servicing as an important growth opportunity and continued to invest in specialized technology and personnel.
- **ETF servicing.** ETF sponsors continued to launch new products and expand into new asset classes, supporting demand for our integrated custody, accounting, and basket processing capabilities.
- **Pricing.** Persistent fee pressure in the custody of public market assets, together with periodic client requests for proposals, affected the fee rate on existing relationships. We seek to offset pricing pressure by deepening relationships across multiple services and by providing differentiated data, technology, and private markets capabilities.
- **Client deposits.** The value of client operating deposits under our funds transfer pricing methodology is an important part of segment revenue. It reflects the level of interest rates, the volume and mix of deposits, and deposit pricing. During fiscal 2023, institutional clients across the industry managed cash balances actively in response to higher rates, and some balances moved into money market funds and other alternatives.
- **Foreign exchange and securities lending.** Transaction-based revenues reflected currency volatility, client cross-border activity, borrower demand for specific securities, and lending spreads.

**Operating priorities**

During fiscal 2023, the segment devoted significant resources to:

- modernizing our core custody and accounting platforms;
- preparing for the shortening of the U.S. standard settlement cycle, including changes to trade processing, foreign exchange, securities lending recall, and funding practices; and
- preparing clients for central clearing of certain U.S. Treasury transactions.

These investments are designed to support scalability, reduce operational risk, and improve the client experience. During implementation, they add to the segment's cost base.

#### Investment Management

Investment Management, conducted principally through IAM and its affiliates, provides investment management services across a broad range of strategies and vehicles to institutional, intermediary, wealth, and international clients.

**Revenue drivers in fiscal 2023**

- **Assets under management.** Segment revenues are earned primarily as fees calculated as a percentage of assets under management. Market levels and net client flows determine the asset base on which fees are earned.
- **Liquidity management.** Elevated short-term rates supported client demand for money market funds and separately managed cash accounts. These strategies also serve as vehicles for Asset Servicing clients' operating cash and for cash collateral from securities lending. Liquidity strategies generally carry lower fee rates than longer-duration or active strategies, so growth in this area affects the average fee rate on assets managed.
- **Fixed income, equity, and multi-asset strategies.** Flows into and out of active and index strategies, outsourced chief investment officer mandates, and liability-driven investment solutions reflected investment performance, client asset allocation decisions, and the continued industry shift toward lower-cost index and factor products.
- **Alternatives and private markets.** We continued to develop selected alternatives capabilities, including private credit, real assets, and multi-manager private markets programs, in response to institutional and wealth client demand.
- **Performance-based fees.** Performance-based fees are recognized when contractual measurement periods conclude and vary with investment results.

**Regulatory developments**

In 2023, the SEC adopted amendments to money market fund rules that include mandatory liquidity fees for certain funds and increased liquidity requirements. We are implementing the operational and product changes these amendments require and are monitoring their potential effect on investor demand for particular money market fund types.

**Operating priorities**

IAM's priorities include:

- maintaining competitive investment performance;
- expanding distribution through intermediary, wealth, and international channels;
- broadening our customized separately managed account and direct indexing capabilities; and
- managing the business efficiently amid continuing fee pressure.

#### Wealth and Fiduciary Services

Wealth and Fiduciary Services provides investment management and advice, wealth planning, trust and estate administration, private banking, family office services, and foundation and endowment services to high-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth individuals and families, business owners, family offices, and charitable organizations.

**Revenue drivers in fiscal 2023**

- **Market-based fees.** Fees calculated on assets under management or administration reflected market levels during the year.
- **Client acquisition and retention.** We continued to emphasize multi-generational relationships, advisor recruitment and development, and targeted expansion beyond our Midwest base.
- **Fiduciary and planning fees.** Trust administration, estate settlement, and planning fees reflected client activity and the complexity of the relationships we serve, including trusts holding closely held business interests, real estate, farmland, mineral interests, and other specialty assets.
- **Private banking.** The segment's results include the value of client deposits and net interest income from private banking loans, principally residential mortgages, securities-based lines of credit, and customized credit facilities. In fiscal 2023, higher rates raised loan yields. Private clients, like institutional clients, also grew more sensitive to deposit rates and moved some balances into money market funds and other alternatives.

**Credit quality**

Our private banking underwriting standards emphasize the overall financial strength and liquidity of borrowers and the quality of collateral. Credit quality in the private banking portfolio is discussed under "Risk Management—Credit Risk."

**Operating priorities**

The segment's priorities include:

- continuing to recruit experienced relationship professionals;
- enhancing our digital client experience and planning tools;
- expanding family office and trust capabilities; and
- broadening our presence in attractive markets outside the Midwest while preserving the fiduciary-centered service model on which our franchise is built.

#### Corporate and Other

Corporate and Other includes the results of our corporate treasury function, which manages:

- the consolidated balance sheet;
- liquidity and capital;
- funding; and
- interest rate risk.

It also includes the results of the Bank's investment securities portfolio not attributed to client deposits under our funds transfer pricing methodology, certain corporate expenses not allocated to the business segments, costs associated with certain corporate initiatives, and the results of certain investments and other activities. Corporate and Other results can vary significantly from period to period. Key factors include the yield on the investment securities portfolio relative to funds transfer pricing charges, gains or losses on securities, and the timing of corporate items.

#### Investment Securities Portfolio Considerations

Interest rates rose across the industry in recent years, which lowered the market value of fixed-rate securities purchased in lower-rate periods.

- **Available-for-sale securities.** Unrealized losses, net of tax, are recognized in AOCI and reduce stockholders' equity. They are a principal component of the $1,364.2 million AOCI loss at December 31, 2023.
- **Held-to-maturity securities.** These are carried at amortized cost. Unrealized gains and losses on them are not recognized in the balance sheet. They would be realized only if the securities were sold.

We have the intent and ability to hold securities in an unrealized loss position until recovery of their amortized cost basis, which may be at maturity. We manage the portfolio so that we can meet liquidity needs through cash, central bank deposits, unencumbered securities eligible for pledging, and repurchase agreement and central bank facilities, without needing to sell securities at a loss.

As securities mature or prepay, we reinvest the proceeds at prevailing market yields or use them to fund other balance sheet needs. Over time, this pulls carrying values toward par and reduces the associated unrealized losses. During fiscal 2023, net cash from the investment portfolio and other interest-earning assets helped fund the net reduction in client deposits and other funding.

We evaluate available-for-sale securities in an unrealized loss position, and held-to-maturity securities, for expected credit losses under the current expected credit loss ("CECL") framework. Most of our portfolio consists of securities issued or guaranteed by the U.S. government, U.S. government agencies, or highly rated sovereign and supranational issuers. For these securities, we expect no credit loss given the issuers' credit quality and history.

### Capital

#### Capital Management Objectives

Capital management is central to our ability to serve clients, meet regulatory requirements, and create long-term value for shareholders. Our objectives are to:

- maintain capital that comfortably exceeds regulatory minimums and buffers, and supports our credit ratings and client confidence across a range of economic and market conditions, including severe stress;
- support the growth of our businesses and our investments in technology and client capabilities;
- absorb the effects of potentially rapid increases in client deposits, which can expand our balance sheet and our leverage exposure; and
- return capital to shareholders when it exceeds the amounts needed for these purposes, subject to regulatory requirements and the approval of our Board of Directors.

Our Board of Directors approves our capital policy, capital plan, and capital actions. Our Asset and Liability Committee and the Risk Committee of the Board oversee capital adequacy. We assess capital adequacy through our internal capital adequacy assessment process. This process includes:

- forward-looking capital projections;
- enterprise-wide stress testing under baseline and adverse scenarios; and
- evaluation of risks not fully captured in regulatory capital measures, such as operational, fiduciary, and strategic risks.

#### Regulatory Capital Framework

We and the Bank are subject to risk-based and leverage capital requirements adopted by the Federal Reserve based on the Basel III framework. These requirements include minimum ratios of:

- common equity tier 1 capital to risk-weighted assets;
- tier 1 capital to risk-weighted assets;
- total capital to risk-weighted assets;
- tier 1 capital to average total consolidated assets (the tier 1 leverage ratio); and
- tier 1 capital to total leverage exposure, including certain off-balance sheet exposures (the supplementary leverage ratio).

We are also subject to a stress capital buffer requirement, which the Federal Reserve determines based in part on supervisory stress test results. We must maintain this buffer above the minimum risk-based capital requirements to avoid restrictions on capital distributions and certain discretionary bonus payments.

At December 31, 2023:

- our regulatory capital ratios and those of the Bank exceeded all applicable minimum requirements and buffers; and
- the Bank met the requirements to be categorized as "well capitalized" under the prompt corrective action framework.

Our regulatory capital ratios, the related minimum requirements and buffers, and the components of regulatory capital, risk-weighted assets, and leverage exposure are presented in the regulatory capital Note to our Consolidated Financial Statements in Part II, Item 8.

Because our balance sheet consists predominantly of low-risk-weighted assets, leverage-based requirements can be a meaningful constraint on our balance sheet capacity. Examples of such assets include central bank deposits and government securities. Increases in client deposits, such as those that can occur when clients seek safety during market stress, raise our leverage exposure even though the resulting assets carry little credit risk. We monitor leverage capacity closely. Our capital planning provides for the potential effect of deposit inflows under stress.

As a simple balance sheet measure, which differs from regulatory leverage ratios:

- stockholders' equity equaled 8.8% of total assets at December 31, 2023; and
- tangible stockholders' equity, a non-GAAP measure, equaled 8.4% of tangible assets.

#### Regulatory Developments

Several pending regulatory proposals could significantly affect our capital requirements.

**Basel III proposal.** In July 2023, the U.S. federal banking agencies proposed rules to implement the final components of the Basel III framework. As proposed, the rules would:

- revise the calculation of risk-weighted assets for credit risk, market risk, and credit valuation adjustment risk;
- introduce a new standardized approach to operational risk calibrated in significant part to fee-based revenues, which is of particular relevance to custody and asset management businesses such as ours; and
- require recognition of unrealized gains and losses on certain available-for-sale securities in regulatory capital, which would make our capital ratios more sensitive to interest rate movements.

At December 31, 2023, AOCI was a loss of $1,364.2 million, of which net unrealized losses on available-for-sale securities were the principal component.

**Long-term debt proposal.** In August 2023, the federal banking agencies proposed requiring certain large banking organizations to maintain minimum amounts of eligible long-term debt that could absorb losses in resolution. If adopted, this requirement could increase our funding costs and change the composition of our liabilities.

We are evaluating the potential effects of these proposals and engaging with the industry on their calibration. The final content and timing of any rules remain uncertain. Depending on their final form, we may adjust:

- the composition of our balance sheet;
- the pricing of certain products and services;
- our capital distributions; or
- our issuance of debt or other capital instruments.

See Part I, Item 1A, "Risk Factors—Credit, Liquidity, Capital, and Balance Sheet Risks."

### Liquidity and Funding

#### Liquidity Risk Management Framework

Liquidity risk is the risk that we cannot meet our cash and collateral obligations when they come due, without incurring unacceptable losses, because of our inability to liquidate assets or obtain funding. Liquidity is especially important for us because clients depend on us for their operating liquidity and for the timely settlement of their transactions. Most of our deposits are held by institutional clients and exceed deposit insurance limits.

Our liquidity framework is overseen by the Asset and Liability Committee and the Risk Committee of the Board. It includes:

- a liquidity risk appetite statement and limits approved by the Board;
- daily monitoring of liquidity positions across legal entities, currencies, and time horizons, including intraday liquidity;
- internal liquidity stress tests across a range of idiosyncratic and market-wide scenarios and time horizons, including severe, rapid deposit outflows informed by the industry events of early 2023;
- a buffer of highly liquid assets sized to meet projected net stressed cash outflows;
- a contingency funding plan that identifies early warning indicators, escalation procedures, and actions available in a stress event; and
- regular testing of our access to contingent funding sources, including the Federal Reserve's discount window and repurchase agreement markets.

We are also subject to the liquidity coverage ratio and the net stable funding ratio, each as tailored to our size and risk profile. During fiscal 2023, we met all applicable regulatory liquidity requirements.

Our principal sources of liquidity are:

- **Cash and cash equivalents**, which were $5,787.8 million at December 31, 2023.

### Risk Management

#### Overview

Risk is inherent in our businesses. Our enterprise risk management framework is overseen by our Board of Directors and its Risk Committee. It defines our risk appetite and sets the policies, limits, and processes we use to identify, measure, monitor, control, and report risk. The framework follows a "three lines" model:

- our business units own and manage the risks of their activities;
- our independent risk management and compliance functions set frameworks, provide independent challenge, and aggregate and report risks across the enterprise; and
- our internal audit function provides independent assurance to the Audit Committee of the Board.

Management committees oversee specific risk categories. These include the Enterprise Risk Committee, the Asset and Liability Committee, the Operational Risk Committee, the Credit Committee, and the Fiduciary Risk Committee. Our principal risk categories are described in Part I, Item 1, "Business—Risk Management," and the risks that could materially affect us are described in Part I, Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

#### Credit Risk

Credit risk is the risk of loss from a borrower's or counterparty's failure to meet its obligations. Our credit risk arises from:

- investment securities and placements with central banks, subcustodians, and other financial institutions;
- intraday and overnight credit extended to clients to support settlement of securities, foreign exchange, and payments;
- counterparty exposure from foreign exchange and other derivatives;
- securities lending indemnification;
- private banking loans and commitments; and
- receivables from clients.

Our credit risk profile reflects the predominance of high-quality, low-risk assets on our balance sheet. Most of our investment securities are issued or guaranteed by the U.S. government, U.S. government agencies, or highly rated sovereign and supranational issuers. Our private banking portfolio is underwritten with an emphasis on the borrower's overall financial strength and liquidity and on the quality of collateral. A significant portion of it is secured by marketable securities or residential real estate.

We manage credit risk through:

- credit approval authorities;
- counterparty and concentration limits by obligor, industry, country, and product;
- collateral requirements and daily mark-to-market practices;
- netting agreements; and
- ongoing monitoring.

Exposures to financial institutions, which arise naturally from our role in global custody, settlement, and foreign exchange, are subject to specific limits and review.

We maintain an allowance for credit losses under the CECL framework for loans, held-to-maturity securities, and certain other exposures, including off-balance sheet commitments. Credit quality metrics for our loan portfolio and changes in the allowance for credit losses are presented in the Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements. See also "Critical Accounting Estimates—Allowance for Credit Losses."

#### Market Risk

Market risk is the risk of loss from changes in market factors such as interest rates, foreign exchange rates, credit spreads, and equity prices. Our trading-related market risk arises principally from foreign exchange activities with clients. We manage it through limits on positions, sensitivities, and value-at-risk, and through stress testing.

Our non-trading market risk arises principally from interest rate risk in our balance sheet, discussed below, and from foreign currency translation of our non-U.S. operations. We also face significant indirect market risk because fee revenues depend on the market value of client assets. Quantitative disclosures about market risk appear in Part II, Item 7A.

#### Interest Rate Risk

Interest rate risk is the risk that changes in interest rates will hurt our earnings or the economic value of our equity. It arises from differences in the timing of repricing and maturity of our interest-earning assets and interest-bearing liabilities, from changes in the shape of the yield curve, from prepayments on mortgage-backed securities, and from the behavior of client deposits.

Our Asset and Liability Committee manages interest rate risk within limits approved by the Board. We measure it using two principal approaches:

- **Earnings sensitivity analysis**, which estimates how hypothetical changes in interest rates would change our net interest income over a forward-looking horizon.
- **Economic value of equity analysis**, which estimates how such changes would affect the present value of our assets, liabilities, and off-balance sheet positions.

These analyses depend significantly on assumptions about client deposit behavior. Those assumptions include:

- deposit betas, which measure how much of a rate change we pass through to deposit pricing;
- the stability of operational deposits; and
- migration between noninterest-bearing and interest-bearing balances and off-balance sheet alternatives.

In fiscal 2023, we refined these assumptions to reflect the deposit behavior seen during the rising rate environment, including higher deposit betas and greater movement of balances into money market funds. Interest expense of $4,823.2 million, equal to 60.0% of total revenues, illustrates how sensitive our results are to deposit pricing in a high-rate environment.

Our balance sheet is generally positioned so that falling short-term rates would reduce asset yields faster than we could reduce deposit rates. We use the duration and composition of the investment securities portfolio, and interest rate derivatives where appropriate, to manage this sensitivity.

Changes in interest rates also affect the fair value of our available-for-sale securities. Those changes are reflected in AOCI, which was a loss of $1,364.2 million at December 31, 2023. Results of our earnings sensitivity and economic value of equity analyses appear in Part II, Item 7A.

#### Operational Risk

Operational risk is the risk of loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems, or from external events. It includes technology, cyber, third-party, model, fiduciary, and legal risk. Operational risk is among the most significant risks we face, because we process very high volumes of transactions involving large dollar amounts relative to the fees we earn.

Our operational risk management program includes:

- risk and control self-assessments;
- key risk indicators;
- incident and loss event reporting and analysis;
- scenario analysis;
- independent review and challenge by the second line; and
- testing by internal audit.

During fiscal 2023, our operational risk priorities included:

- managing the transition risks of our technology modernization program;
- preparing for shortened settlement cycles and Treasury central clearing;
- strengthening cybersecurity defenses;
- enhancing third-party risk management, including oversight of subcustodians and technology providers; and
- advancing our operational resilience program.

Our cybersecurity risk management, strategy, and governance are described in Part I, Item 1C, "Cybersecurity."

#### Allowance for Credit Losses

We maintain an allowance for credit losses under the CECL framework for loans, held-to-maturity securities, and certain off-balance sheet credit exposures. We also evaluate available-for-sale securities in an unrealized loss position for credit-related impairment. The allowance is management's estimate of expected credit losses over the contractual life of these exposures.

Estimating the allowance requires significant judgment about:

- macroeconomic forecasts, including unemployment, interest rates, equity market levels, and real estate values, over a reasonable and supportable forecast period, followed by reversion to historical loss experience;
- the probability of default and loss given default for each portfolio segment;
- the value and liquidity of collateral, including marketable securities, residential real estate, and less liquid assets such as closely held business interests and specialty assets;
- prepayment and utilization assumptions; and
- qualitative adjustments for risks not fully captured in modeled estimates.

Most of our investment securities are issued or guaranteed by the U.S. government, U.S. government agencies, or highly rated sovereign and supranational issuers, and much of our private banking portfolio is secured by marketable securities or residential real estate. As a result, our expected credit losses are low relative to our total assets. Even so, a significant deterioration in economic conditions, a sharp decline in securities or real estate values, or credit deterioration at specific borrowers or issuers could require a meaningful increase in the allowance.

#### Fair Value Measurements

We measure certain financial instruments at fair value, including:

- available-for-sale investment securities;
- derivatives, principally foreign exchange contracts; and
- certain other assets and liabilities.

We disclose the fair value of other financial instruments, including held-to-maturity securities and loans. Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date.

We use a three-level fair value hierarchy based on the observability of valuation inputs. Most of our instruments measured at fair value are Level 1 or Level 2. Their values come from quoted prices in active markets or from models that use observable inputs, such as interest rate curves, credit spreads, and foreign exchange rates, typically obtained from independent pricing services.

We validate pricing service values through price challenges, comparisons with alternative sources, and review of pricing methodologies. A limited portion of our instruments is classified as Level 3 because significant inputs are unobservable. Valuing these instruments requires greater judgment.

Fair values of available-for-sale securities directly affect AOCI, which was a loss of $1,364.2 million at December 31, 2023. They would also affect regulatory capital if the July 2023 Basel III proposal is adopted as proposed.

#### Goodwill

Goodwill was $707.2 million at December 31, 2023. It is allocated to the reporting units that benefited from the acquisitions that generated it. We test goodwill for impairment annually and when events or circumstances indicate that a reporting unit's fair value may be below its carrying amount. Such events or circumstances include:

- a significant decline in market conditions or in the reporting unit's projected performance;
- the loss of significant clients; and
- a sustained decline in our market capitalization.

We may first perform a qualitative assessment. When a quantitative test is required, we estimate the fair value of each reporting unit using income approaches, such as discounted cash flow analysis, and market approaches, such as comparisons with valuation multiples of comparable businesses, as appropriate. Key assumptions include:

- projected revenues, which depend on market levels, client flows, pricing, and new business;
- projected expenses and margins;
- long-term growth rates; and
- discount rates that reflect the reporting unit's risk profile and current market conditions.

Based on our most recent annual impairment assessment, we concluded that goodwill was not impaired. Goodwill represents a relatively small share of our capital, 5.0% of total stockholders' equity. However, adverse changes in market conditions, investment performance, or client retention in the relevant reporting units could reduce their estimated fair values and lead to impairment charges in future periods.

#### Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States at the federal, state, and local levels, including in Illinois, and in the non-U.S. jurisdictions where we operate. Income tax expense was $915.9 million in fiscal 2023, an effective tax rate of 28.5%.

Determining our tax provision requires judgment, including:

- interpreting complex tax laws and regulations in multiple jurisdictions;
- allocating income among jurisdictions;
- assessing whether deferred tax assets will be realized and whether a valuation allowance is needed; and
- evaluating uncertain tax positions.

We recognize the benefit of an uncertain tax position only if it is more likely than not to be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. We measure the benefit as the largest amount more than 50% likely to be realized on settlement. Our tax positions are subject to examination by taxing authorities. Examination outcomes, or changes in tax laws or their interpretation, could cause actual tax expense to differ from our estimates.

#### Contingencies

In the ordinary course of business, we are subject to claims, lawsuits, regulatory investigations, and other proceedings. Examples include claims alleging operational errors, breach of contract, or breach of fiduciary duty; claims relating to foreign exchange pricing, securities lending, or fees; and claims arising from trust and estate administration.

We establish reserves when a loss is probable and the amount can be reasonably estimated. Assessing whether a loss is probable and estimating its amount requires significant judgment about:

- the merits of the claims;
- the procedural posture of each matter;
- the outcome of similar matters; and
- potential settlement values.

Our estimates may change as matters develop, and actual outcomes could differ materially from the amounts reserved. Information about significant legal proceedings appears in Part I, Item 3, "Legal Proceedings," and in the Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements.

#### Revenue Recognition

We recognize fee revenue when, or as, we satisfy our performance obligations to clients, in an amount reflecting the consideration we expect to receive. Most of our fees relate to services delivered over time and are recognized as the services are performed, generally based on asset values, transaction volumes, or fixed fee schedules.

Performance-based investment management fees are a form of variable consideration. We recognize them only when it is probable that a significant reversal of the cumulative revenue recognized will not occur, which is generally when the contractual measurement period ends. Implementation and onboarding arrangements for new mandates may require judgment about:

- whether the services are distinct performance obligations; and
- whether costs incurred to fulfill contracts should be capitalized and amortized.

### Recent Accounting Developments

#### Accounting Standards Adopted in Fiscal 2023

**Troubled debt restructurings and vintage disclosures.** On January 1, 2023, we adopted accounting guidance that:

- eliminated the recognition and measurement guidance for troubled debt restructurings by creditors;

#### Accounting Standards Issued but Not Yet Adopted

**Segment reporting.** In November 2023, the Financial Accounting Standards Board ("FASB") issued guidance to improve reportable segment disclosures. It requires public entities to disclose, among other items, significant segment expenses regularly provided to the chief operating decision maker. The guidance is effective for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2023, and interim periods within fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2024, and is applied retrospectively. We are evaluating its effect on our segment disclosures. We do not expect it to affect our consolidated financial position or results of operations.

**Income tax disclosures.** In December 2023, the FASB issued guidance requiring enhanced income tax disclosures, including:

- disaggregated information in the effective tax rate reconciliation; and
- income taxes paid by jurisdiction.

The guidance is effective for annual periods beginning after December 15, 2024. We are evaluating its effect on our income tax disclosures. We do not expect it to affect our consolidated financial position or results of operations.

**Investments in tax credit structures.** In March 2023, the FASB issued guidance permitting entities to elect the proportional amortization method for qualifying tax equity investments beyond low-income housing tax credit structures. The guidance is effective for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2023. We do not expect adoption to have a material effect on our consolidated financial statements.

### Outlook

Our results in future periods will depend on many factors, most of which are outside our control:

- **Markets.** The levels of global equity and fixed income markets, which affect the value of assets on which we earn fees.
- **Interest rates and deposits.** The path of interest rates in the United States and other major currencies, the shape of the yield curve, and client deposit behavior, which together determine the relationship between yields on our interest-earning assets and our interest expense.
- **Client activity.** Client activity and transaction volumes, including foreign exchange and securities lending.
- **New business.** Our success in winning and implementing new mandates, retaining existing relationships, and managing fee pressure.
- **Investment performance.** IAM's investment performance and client flows.
- **Strategic execution.** Our execution of the technology modernization program and productivity initiatives.
- **Regulation.** The final form and timing of pending regulatory changes, including the Basel III proposal and long-term debt requirements, and the final amount of the FDIC special assessment.

We will continue to manage our balance sheet conservatively, with a focus on liquidity and capital strength. We will invest in our technology, data, and client capabilities, and we will pursue disciplined expense management.

We will determine capital distributions, including share repurchases and any dividends, in light of our capital position, regulatory requirements, and the outlook for regulatory change. Our objective is to deliver durable, long-term value for clients and shareholders across a range of market environments.
