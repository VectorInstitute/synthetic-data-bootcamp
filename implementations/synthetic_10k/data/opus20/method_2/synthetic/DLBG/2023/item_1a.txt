## ITEM 1A. RISK FACTORS

An investment in our securities involves risk. Our businesses are subject to many risks. Some arise from the nature of the services we provide. Others arise from our status as a regulated banking organization and from the economic, market, and geopolitical environment in which we operate.

The risks described below are those we currently believe could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, liquidity, capital position, or reputation, or the trading price of our securities. You should consider them carefully, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including:

- Part I, Item 1, "Business";
- Part II, Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations";
- Part II, Item 7A, "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk"; and
- our Consolidated Financial Statements and related Notes in Part II, Item 8.

The risks below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks that are not presently known to us, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also impair our business. Many of these risks are interrelated and may occur together or compound one another, so the effect of any risk could be greater than its description in isolation suggests. We have grouped the risk factors into categories for convenience. A risk discussed in one category may also be relevant to others, and the order of presentation does not indicate relative likelihood or significance.

This Item 1A contains forward-looking statements. Please refer to "Forward-Looking Statements" in Part I, Item 1, and in Part II, Item 7, of this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

### Summary of Risk Factors

The following is a summary of the principal risks that could adversely affect our business. It is qualified in its entirety by the more complete discussion that follows.

**Market, Economic, and Geopolitical Risks**

- Most of our revenues are fees linked to the market value of client assets. Declines or sustained volatility in global equity and fixed income markets could significantly reduce our revenues.
- Weak or uncertain economic conditions, persistent inflation, or recession could reduce client activity, increase credit losses, and impair the value of our assets.
- Geopolitical tensions, armed conflicts, sanctions, trade disputes, pandemics, and other global events could disrupt markets and our operations.
- Changes in interest rates, the shape of the yield curve, and central bank policy could reduce our net interest income and change client deposit behavior.
- Changes in the level or composition of client deposits could reduce our net interest income and affect our liquidity.
- Fluctuations in currency exchange rates, transaction volumes, and market volatility could cause our results to vary materially between periods.

**Credit, Liquidity, Capital, and Balance Sheet Risks**

- Our funding depends heavily on deposits from institutional clients, most of which exceed deposit insurance limits and can be withdrawn quickly.
- Market value declines, credit deterioration, or forced sales in our investment securities portfolio could reduce our capital and earnings.
- We are exposed to credit risk from clients, counterparties, subcustodians, borrowers, and other financial institutions, sometimes in large and concentrated intraday amounts.
- Capital and leverage requirements, which are expected to become more stringent, could constrain our balance sheet, our activities, and our ability to return capital to shareholders.
- As a holding company, we depend on dividends from our subsidiaries, which are subject to regulatory limits.
- A downgrade in our credit ratings could harm our competitive position, funding costs, and client relationships.

**Operational, Technology, and Cybersecurity Risks**

- Our businesses process extremely high volumes of transactions and hold large amounts of client assets. Operational errors could cause significant losses and client harm.
- A cyberattack or other information security incident could compromise client assets or data, disrupt our operations, and damage our reputation.
- Our multi-year technology modernization program may not be completed on schedule or within budget, or may not deliver its expected benefits.
- We depend on third parties, including subcustodians, market infrastructures, and cloud and technology vendors, whose failures could disrupt our services.
- Models, analytics, and artificial intelligence tools that we use may be flawed or misused.
- Our risk management framework may not identify or mitigate every risk.

**Fiduciary, Investment Management, and Business-Specific Risks**

- We act as fiduciary for many clients and could incur significant liability if we fail to meet our fiduciary obligations.
- Poor investment performance, product outflows, or shifts in client preferences could reduce Investment Management revenues.
- Our money market fund, securities lending, and private markets servicing activities carry distinct risks, including potential pressure to provide financial support.
- Fee pressure, contract renegotiations, and the loss of large client relationships could reduce our revenues.
- Large new mandates require significant up-front investment and carry implementation risk.

**Competitive, Strategic, and Reputational Risks**

- We face intense competition from large global institutions, specialized providers, and new technology-driven entrants.
- We may not execute our strategy successfully or achieve our productivity objectives.
- Acquisitions, partnerships, divestitures, and new products and services entail significant risks.
- Our success depends on attracting and retaining skilled employees.
- Damage to our reputation could cause clients to move assets or deposits and could impair our business broadly.

**Regulatory, Legal, and Compliance Risks**

- We are subject to extensive and evolving regulation, and changes in laws, regulations, and supervisory expectations could materially affect our business.
- Failure to satisfy regulatory requirements could result in enforcement actions, restrictions on our activities, and loss of financial holding company status.
- Anti-money laundering, sanctions, and anti-corruption obligations are complex, and violations could result in substantial penalties.
- We are subject to litigation, regulatory investigations, and other proceedings that could result in significant liability.
- Changes in tax laws or accounting standards could adversely affect our results.

**Risks Related to Our Common Stock**

- The market price of our common stock may be volatile.
- Our ability to pay dividends and repurchase shares is subject to regulatory constraints and the discretion of our Board of Directors.
- Banking laws and provisions of Illinois law and our organizational documents could delay or prevent a change in control.

### Market, Economic, and Geopolitical Risks

**Our fee revenues are closely tied to the market value of the assets we custody, administer, and manage. Declines or sustained volatility in financial markets could significantly reduce our revenues and earnings.**

The substantial majority of our revenues comes from fees for asset servicing, investment management, and wealth and fiduciary services. Many of these fees are calculated as a percentage of the market value of assets under custody, administration, or management. In fiscal 2023, our total revenues were approximately $8.04 billion, and a large portion of that amount depended, directly or indirectly, on the level of global equity and fixed income markets.

When securities prices decline, the value of the assets on which we earn fees declines, and our fee revenues generally decline as well. The effect varies by business and by asset class. Equity market declines tend to reduce fees more than fixed income declines, because equity-oriented assets often carry higher fee rates. Declines in non-U.S. markets also affect us, both directly and through the translation of non-U.S. dollar assets into U.S. dollars.

Market declines can also reduce our revenues indirectly:

- They may prompt clients to reduce allocations to higher-fee strategies, move assets to lower-fee products, or delay new mandates and fund launches.
- They may reduce investor demand for funds and vehicles that our Asset Servicing clients sponsor.
- They may lead clients to seek fee concessions.
- Prolonged periods of low valuations can make it harder for the private funds we administer to raise capital and realize investments, reducing activity-based fees.

Many of our expenses do not decline in proportion to revenues when markets fall. These include compensation, technology investment, occupancy, and regulatory and compliance costs. As a result, a market decline can have a disproportionately negative effect on our earnings and operating margins.

Volatility creates further uncertainty. Volatility can sometimes raise transaction-based revenues, such as foreign exchange trading. However, it also increases operational strain, can heighten counterparty and credit risk, and can drive large and unpredictable movements in client deposits. We cannot predict the direction or magnitude of market movements, and a significant or sustained downturn could materially and adversely affect our results of operations.

**Adverse economic conditions in the United States and globally could reduce demand for our services, impair client and counterparty creditworthiness, and harm our financial results.**

Our business is affected by economic conditions in the United States and in the other regions where we and our clients operate, including Europe, the Middle East, and the Asia-Pacific region. Several conditions could adversely affect us:

- an economic slowdown or recession;
- persistent inflation and the policy actions taken to combat it;
- rising unemployment;
- declines in corporate earnings;
- stress in the commercial real estate sector;
- fiscal pressures on governments; and
- disruptions in credit markets.

These conditions can reduce asset values and transaction volumes. They can also lead pension plans, endowments, foundations, and other asset owners to reduce allocations, draw down portfolios to meet obligations, or delay hiring service providers. They may increase the risk that borrowers in our private banking business, or counterparties in our foreign exchange, securities lending, and settlement activities, will be unable to meet their obligations. Economic stress can also reduce the value of collateral, including the marketable securities, residential real estate, farmland, and other assets that secure loans or are held in the trusts we administer.

Inflation can increase our operating costs, including wages, technology services, and occupancy, at a pace that our fee revenues may not match. Many of our client contracts set fees as fixed rates or basis-point schedules that cannot be readily adjusted for inflation. Fiscal and political uncertainty in the United States could also disrupt markets, affect the value and liquidity of U.S. Treasury and agency securities, and harm client confidence. This includes disputes over the federal debt limit and the potential for government shutdowns.

Our wealth business has a significant concentration in the Midwest, where our franchise has its deepest roots. As a result, regional economic conditions there, including conditions in the agricultural sector and in the Chicago metropolitan area, may affect that business disproportionately.

**Geopolitical events, armed conflicts, sanctions, trade disputes, terrorism, pandemics, and other global developments could disrupt markets, our operations, and our clients.**

We conduct business globally and serve clients that invest across many jurisdictions. Geopolitical tensions and conflicts, including the conflict involving Russia and Ukraine and the conflict in the Middle East, have contributed to market volatility, commodity price shocks, supply chain disruptions, and a significant expansion of sanctions programs. Escalation of these conflicts, or the emergence of new ones, including tensions affecting the Asia-Pacific region where we have operations, could cause:

- sharp declines in asset values;
- disruptions in payment and settlement systems;
- restrictions on the transfer or repatriation of assets; and
- the freezing or immobilization of securities held in custody.

When sanctions or countermeasures restrict particular markets, issuers, or depositories, we may be unable to settle trades, collect income, or return assets to clients. We may face claims from clients or counterparties in those circumstances. Sanctions may also require us to block assets, reject transactions, and exit relationships. These steps can be operationally complex and may expose us to conflicting legal obligations across jurisdictions.

Trade disputes, tariffs, export controls, and restrictions on cross-border investment or data flows could reduce global investment activity and affect our clients' businesses. They could also complicate our operating model, which relies on operations centers and service delivery across multiple countries.

Terrorist attacks, civil unrest, and political instability could disrupt our facilities, personnel, and third-party providers. The emergence of a pandemic or other widespread public health crisis could again cause severe market disruption, strain our operations, require extended remote working arrangements that increase operational and cybersecurity risk, and reduce economic activity. The effects of any of these events are difficult to predict and could be material.

**Changes in interest rates, the yield curve, and monetary policy could reduce our net interest income and affect the value of our assets.**

Net interest income is a meaningful component of our revenues. It is earned primarily by investing client deposits in high-quality, liquid assets, including central bank deposits, U.S. Treasury and agency securities, agency mortgage-backed securities, and highly rated non-U.S. sovereign and supranational securities. Our net interest income depends on several factors, many of which are outside our control:

- the level of interest rates in the currencies in which we hold deposits;
- the shape of the yield curve;
- the rates we pay on client deposits relative to the yields on our assets; and
- the volume and composition of our deposits and interest-earning assets.

Rising rates generally increase the yields on our floating-rate and newly purchased assets. However, they may also require us to pay higher rates on deposits and may cause clients to move balances out of deposits and into money market funds or other higher-yielding alternatives. Rising rates also reduce the market value of fixed-rate securities in our portfolio, which can reduce our capital, as discussed below.

Declining rates would generally reduce yields on our assets more quickly than we could reduce deposit rates, which would compress our net interest margin. A return to very low or negative rates in any of the currencies in which we operate would significantly reduce our net interest income. It could also reduce fee revenues associated with money market funds and other liquidity products, because we may waive fees to maintain positive yields for investors.

Changes in the shape of the yield curve, including prolonged inversion, can also reduce our net interest income and complicate our asset and liability management. Our interest rate risk management relies on assumptions about client deposit behavior, prepayment speeds on mortgage-backed securities, and other factors. These assumptions may prove inaccurate, particularly during periods of rapid rate changes that differ from historical experience.

**Changes in the level and composition of client deposits could reduce our net interest income and affect our liquidity and balance sheet management.**

Most of our deposits are held by institutional clients in connection with custody, settlement, and other operational activities. Their level fluctuates, sometimes significantly, based on client activity, market conditions, interest rates, and the availability of alternative investment options. In periods of market stress, clients may hold elevated cash balances with us, which can expand our balance sheet rapidly. This can increase the leverage capital we must hold, even though the expansion may produce only modest additional income. Conversely, rising rates, competitive pricing, changes in client investment strategies, or concerns about our financial condition could cause deposits to decline quickly.

The composition of our deposits also matters. Deposits that regulators and our internal models consider operational, because they arise from clients' custody and settlement activities, generally receive more favorable treatment under liquidity requirements than non-operational deposits. Changes in client behavior, client consolidation, or regulatory definitions could shift our deposit mix toward less stable or less favorably treated categories. That shift would require us to hold additional high-quality liquid assets and could reduce our net interest income.

Our net interest income is also sensitive to the rates we pay. If competition for deposits intensifies, or if clients become more sensitive to rate differentials, we may need to raise deposit rates. That would reduce the spread between what we earn on our assets and what we pay on our liabilities.

**Fluctuations in currency exchange rates, client transaction volumes, and market volatility could cause our revenues to vary materially between periods.**

We earn revenues and incur expenses in multiple currencies, and a significant portion of the assets we custody, administer, and manage is denominated in currencies other than the U.S. dollar. A strengthening U.S. dollar reduces the dollar value of non-U.S. dollar assets on which we earn fees, as well as the dollar value of revenues earned by our non-U.S. operations. Exchange rate movements also affect the value of our non-U.S. dollar assets and liabilities. They may also affect our capital ratios, to the extent our capital and risk-weighted assets are denominated in different currencies.

Revenues from foreign exchange trading, securities lending, transition management, and other transaction-based services depend on client activity and market conditions and can vary considerably:

- Foreign exchange trading revenue depends on currency volatility, client cross-border investment flows, and the pricing of standing instruction programs. Competitive and regulatory scrutiny of standing instruction pricing has intensified across the industry.
- Securities lending revenue depends on borrower demand for particular securities, prevailing spreads, the volume of lendable assets, and the reinvestment return on cash collateral. Each of these can decline during periods of reduced market activity or regulatory change.

Because of these factors, our revenues may fluctuate meaningfully from quarter to quarter and may not follow trends in our asset-based fees.

**The soundness of other financial institutions and market participants could adversely affect us.**

Our businesses are deeply interconnected with other financial institutions. We routinely transact with:

- banks, broker-dealers, and investment funds;
- central counterparties, central securities depositories, and payment systems; and
- other market participants, including in connection with settlement, foreign exchange, securities lending, cash management, and collateral activities.

As a result, defaults, liquidity problems, or rumors of problems at one or more financial institutions could lead to market-wide disruptions that impair our ability to transact, reduce the value of our assets, or trigger losses. The bank failures in early 2023 showed how quickly concerns about one institution can spread to others, causing rapid deposit outflows, sharp movements in the market value of financial company securities, and heightened regulatory scrutiny across the industry.

Even if we have no direct exposure to a distressed institution, contagion effects could affect our deposit levels, our access to funding, and the market price of our securities. Our credit risk may be increased when collateral we hold cannot be realized, or can be realized only at prices insufficient to recover the full amount of our exposure. Losses arising from the failure of a market participant could be significant, particularly if they coincide with broader market stress.

### Credit, Liquidity, Capital, and Balance Sheet Risks

**Our liquidity depends substantially on institutional client deposits, most of which exceed deposit insurance limits and could be withdrawn rapidly. If we were unable to meet our liquidity needs, our business could be materially harmed.**

As of December 31, 2023, we had total liabilities of approximately $146.7 billion, representing approximately 91.2% of our total assets of approximately $160.8 billion. Client deposits are the largest source of funding for the Bank, and a substantial portion of those deposits exceeds FDIC insurance limits. This deposit profile differs from that of many retail-oriented banks. It reflects the operational nature of our relationships with pension plans, asset managers, sovereign entities, and other institutional clients.

These deposits have historically been relatively stable because they support clients' custody and settlement activities. However, uninsured institutional deposits can be withdrawn quickly and in large amounts. Clients may reduce balances because of:

- concerns about our financial condition or the condition of the banking industry;
- adverse market or reputational developments;
- changes in their own investment strategies or liquidity needs; or
- the availability of more attractive alternatives.

Developments in digital banking and payment technology may make large-scale deposit outflows faster than historical experience would suggest.

We maintain a buffer of highly liquid assets, conduct internal liquidity stress tests, and maintain a contingency funding plan. Our liquidity also depends on our ability to access other funding sources, including:

- repurchase agreement markets;
- Federal Reserve facilities, including the discount window;
- wholesale funding markets; and
- the sale of investment securities.

In a period of severe stress, these sources may be unavailable, insufficient, or available only on unfavorable terms. Selling securities to meet withdrawals could require us to realize losses, as discussed below. Our non-U.S. entities are also subject to local liquidity requirements, and regulators in some jurisdictions may restrict the transfer of liquidity among our legal entities. Liquidity stress, or market perceptions of it, could materially and adversely affect our financial condition and results of operations, and in an extreme scenario could threaten our viability.

**Declines in the market value of our investment securities, credit deterioration, or the need to sell securities before maturity could reduce our capital, liquidity, and earnings.**

The Bank's investment securities portfolio is a significant portion of our total assets. It consists primarily of U.S. Treasury and agency securities, agency mortgage-backed securities, highly rated non-U.S. sovereign and supranational securities, and other investment-grade instruments. Although these securities carry high credit quality, their market values change with interest rates, credit spreads, and market liquidity.

The increase in interest rates in recent years has reduced the market value of fixed-rate securities held across the banking industry, including ours. The accounting treatment of these losses depends on how the securities are classified:

- Unrealized losses on available-for-sale securities are recognized in accumulated other comprehensive income and reduce our stockholders' equity.
- Unrealized losses on held-to-maturity securities are not reflected in our balance sheet, but they would be realized if we were required to sell those securities.

If we needed to sell securities with unrealized losses to meet liquidity needs, rebalance our portfolio, or respond to regulatory requirements, we would realize those losses in earnings. That could materially reduce our regulatory capital.

The July 2023 U.S. capital proposal discussed below would require large banking organizations, including us, to reflect unrealized gains and losses on certain available-for-sale securities in regulatory capital. If adopted, it would make our capital ratios more sensitive to interest rate movements. Market and investor scrutiny of unrealized losses on bank securities portfolios has also increased and may affect perceptions of our financial strength, regardless of our intent and ability to hold securities to maturity.

We also hold certain securities that could experience credit deterioration. These include non-U.S. sovereign obligations, which could be affected by fiscal stress, political developments, or changes in credit ratings. Such deterioration could require us to recognize credit losses.

**We are exposed to credit risk from clients, counterparties, subcustodians, and borrowers, including large intraday exposures that arise in the ordinary course of our business.**

Credit risk arises throughout our business, often in forms different from traditional bank lending. For example:

- **Settlement exposures.** We extend intraday and overnight credit to clients to facilitate the settlement of securities transactions, foreign exchange, and payments. These exposures can be very large relative to a client's ordinary balances and can arise with little notice.
- **Foreign exchange and derivatives.** Our trading activities create counterparty exposure that depends on market movements and the counterparty's continued performance.
- **Placements with other institutions.** We place deposits with central banks, subcustodians, and other financial institutions in connection with our global custody and cash management activities.
- **Securities lending indemnification.** We provide indemnification against borrower default, as described below.
- **Collateral reinvestment.** The reinvestment of cash collateral creates credit and liquidity exposure for clients, and potentially for us.

A client or counterparty default, particularly one involving a large intraday exposure or occurring during market disruption, could result in losses. Collateral may be unavailable, may decline in value, or may be difficult to realize because of legal, operational, or market constraints, particularly in non-U.S. jurisdictions. Our exposures may also be concentrated in particular industries, such as financial services, or in particular counterparties or jurisdictions. Concentration increases the potential for a single adverse event to cause significant loss.

Our credit risk management processes depend on accurate and timely information about clients and counterparties. That information may be incomplete or inaccurate, or may change rapidly in a crisis.

**Our private banking loan portfolio is subject to credit risk, including risks associated with collateral values and geographic concentration.**

Through the Bank, we offer private clients residential mortgages, securities-based lines of credit, and customized credit facilities secured by marketable securities, real estate, or other assets. We emphasize the overall financial strength and liquidity of borrowers and the quality of collateral. Nevertheless, these loans expose us to credit risk:

- Declines in securities prices could reduce the value of collateral supporting securities-based lending, and rapid market moves could prevent timely liquidation.
- Declines in residential or commercial real estate values could impair real estate-secured loans.
- Some customized facilities are secured by less liquid assets, such as closely held business interests or specialty assets, whose values are uncertain and may be difficult to realize.

Because our wealth business has a significant concentration in the Midwest, our loan portfolio may also be geographically concentrated. That concentration exposes us to regional economic and real estate conditions. In addition, many of our private banking clients have other relationships with us. A client's financial difficulty could therefore affect several of our businesses at the same time, and enforcing remedies against a client could damage broader relationships or our reputation.

**Capital and leverage requirements, which are likely to become more stringent, could constrain the size and composition of our balance sheet, limit our business activities, and restrict capital distributions.**

As of December 31, 2023, our total stockholders' equity was approximately $14.1 billion. That amount equals approximately 8.8% of our total assets of approximately $160.8 billion, and our total liabilities were roughly 10.4 times our stockholders' equity. This simple ratio is not the same as the regulatory capital and leverage ratios that apply to us, which are calculated under detailed regulatory definitions and are described in Part II, Item 7.

We and the Bank must maintain minimum risk-based and leverage capital ratios. We must also maintain a stress capital buffer determined by the Federal Reserve based in part on supervisory stress tests, and a supplementary leverage ratio that includes certain off-balance sheet exposures. If we fail to keep our capital above these requirements, plus applicable buffers, we would be subject to restrictions on capital distributions and certain discretionary bonus payments. More severe shortfalls could lead to supervisory or enforcement action.

Leverage-based requirements are a particularly important constraint for us. Our balance sheet consists predominantly of low-risk-weighted assets, such as central bank deposits and government securities. Leverage requirements apply the same capital charge to these assets as to riskier ones. When clients deposit large amounts of cash with us, including during periods of market stress when they seek a safe haven, our leverage capital requirements rise. In those circumstances, we may need to:

- discourage deposits;
- impose charges on deposits; or
- raise additional capital,

any of which could harm client relationships or reduce returns.

In July 2023, the U.S. federal banking agencies proposed rules to implement the final components of the Basel III framework. If adopted as proposed, the rules would substantially revise the calculation of risk-weighted assets, including through a new standardized approach to operational risk. Because that approach would be calibrated in significant part to fee-based revenues, it is of particular relevance to custody and asset management businesses such as ours. The proposal would also require recognition of unrealized gains and losses on certain available-for-sale securities in regulatory capital. Separately, the federal banking agencies have proposed long-term debt requirements for certain large banking organizations, and further changes to the stress testing framework and stress capital buffer are possible.

The final form and timing of these rules is uncertain. If they are adopted, we may need to:

- hold significantly more capital;
- change the composition of our balance sheet;
- reprice or reduce certain products and services;
- reduce or suspend share repurchases or dividends; or
- issue additional debt or equity.

Any of these could adversely affect our return on equity and competitive position, particularly relative to competitors that are subject to different or less stringent requirements.

**As a holding company, we depend on dividends and other distributions from our subsidiaries, which are subject to regulatory and contractual limits.**

Ironwood Resources Corp. is a legal entity separate from its subsidiaries. Its principal sources of funds to pay dividends on its common stock, repurchase shares, and service its debt are dividends and other distributions from the Bank, IAM, and our other subsidiaries. Several constraints apply to these distributions:

- Federal and Illinois laws limit the dividends the Bank may pay without regulatory approval, based on its earnings and retained profits, and prohibit dividends that would cause it to become undercapitalized.
- The Federal Reserve and the IDFPR may prohibit dividends that would constitute an unsafe or unsound practice.
- Our non-U.S. subsidiaries and branches are subject to local capital, liquidity, and distribution requirements that may restrict their ability to transfer funds.
- Our broker-dealer subsidiary is subject to net capital rules that limit distributions.
- Sections 23A and 23B of the Federal Reserve Act restrict the Bank's ability to extend credit to, or engage in certain other transactions with, Ironwood and its non-bank affiliates.

In addition, under the source of strength doctrine, we may be required to commit resources to support the Bank at times when we might not otherwise choose to do so. If our subsidiaries cannot pay dividends to us in sufficient amounts, we may be unable to pay dividends, repurchase shares, or meet our obligations to creditors. Our right to participate in a distribution of a subsidiary's assets upon its liquidation is also subordinate to the prior claims of that subsidiary's creditors, including the Bank's depositors.

**A downgrade in our credit ratings or those of the Bank could adversely affect our funding, client relationships, and competitive position.**

Credit ratings are important to our business. Many institutional clients, particularly in custody, securities lending, foreign exchange, and cash management, consider the credit ratings of their service providers when selecting and retaining them. Some clients have internal policies or contractual provisions that require them to move assets or deposits, or to limit their exposure to us, if our ratings fall below specified levels. Counterparties in derivatives and other transactions may also require additional collateral, or may terminate transactions, following a downgrade.

Rating agencies regularly evaluate us and may change their ratings or outlooks based on many factors, including:

- our capital adequacy, liquidity, and asset quality;
- our profitability and business mix;
- our risk management practices;
- the regulatory environment and conditions in the banking industry; and
- their assessment of the likelihood of government support for systemically important institutions.

Changes in rating agency methodologies, or a broad reassessment of the banking sector, could result in a downgrade even if our financial condition has not changed. A downgrade could increase our funding costs, reduce our access to capital markets, cause clients to move deposits and assets, and harm our ability to compete for new business.

**Our estimates of credit losses and the fair value of certain assets depend on judgments and assumptions that may prove incorrect.**

We maintain an allowance for credit losses based on current expected credit losses over the life of our loans, held-to-maturity securities, and certain other exposures. Estimating expected losses requires significant judgment, including forecasts of economic conditions, collateral values, borrower behavior, and other factors. These estimates are inherently uncertain and may change significantly from period to period. If actual losses exceed our estimates, or if economic forecasts deteriorate, we may need to increase our allowance. That would reduce our earnings and capital.

We also measure certain financial instruments at fair value, and some lack observable market prices. Valuing them requires models and assumptions that may not reflect the amounts we could realize in a sale, particularly in stressed or illiquid markets. Our regulators and auditors also review our estimates and could require changes.

**Our access to capital markets and other funding sources could be limited, and our funding costs could increase.**

In addition to deposits, we may rely on capital markets to issue long-term debt, preferred stock, and other securities to fund our operations, satisfy regulatory requirements, and support our capital management. The federal banking agencies have proposed requiring certain large banking organizations to maintain minimum amounts of long-term debt that could absorb losses in resolution. If adopted, this requirement could significantly increase the amount of debt we must issue and the associated costs.

Our access to capital markets depends on market conditions, investor appetite for financial institution securities, our credit ratings, and perceptions of our financial condition. Disruptions in those markets, or a deterioration in perceptions of us or the banking industry, could limit our ability to raise funds on acceptable terms. Higher funding costs could reduce our net interest income and earnings.

### Operational, Technology, and Cybersecurity Risks

**Our businesses depend on processing very large volumes of transactions accurately and on time. Operational errors or failures could result in significant financial losses, client harm, regulatory consequences, and reputational damage.**

Operational risk is among the most significant risks we face. Every business day, we:

- settle securities transactions and process payments;
- collect income and process corporate actions;
- calculate net asset values and maintain books and records for funds;
- execute foreign exchange transactions; and
- administer trusts and accounts for clients around the world.

Many of these activities involve very large dollar amounts relative to the fees we earn, so a single error can cause losses far greater than the revenue associated with the relationship. Examples include:

- a failure to process a corporate action correctly;
- an inaccurate net asset value calculation;
- a payment sent to the wrong party;
- a missed deadline for a tax reclaim or class action filing; or
- a failure to follow client instructions.

Operational failures can result from:

- human error;
- deficiencies in processes or controls;
- flaws in systems or data;
- failures of third parties;
- fraud or misconduct; and
- external events.

Several features of our business increase the complexity of our operations and the potential for error. These include the increasing variety of the assets we service, including private markets assets, loans, and other instruments that are not held in conventional securities depositories. They also include the customization of client arrangements, the shortening of settlement cycles, and the volume of regulatory change.

We may be contractually or legally obligated to compensate clients for losses resulting from our errors. We may also decide to compensate clients for relationship or reputational reasons even where we are not obligated to do so. Operational failures can trigger regulatory scrutiny, enforcement actions, and litigation, and can damage our reputation for reliability, which is fundamental to our franchise. Our insurance coverage may not be sufficient to cover all losses, and some losses may not be covered at all.

**A cyberattack or other information security incident could result in the loss or misappropriation of client assets or data, disrupt our operations, and cause significant financial and reputational harm.**

As a custodian, fiduciary, and investment manager, we hold and have access to substantial client assets and large volumes of confidential and personal information. This makes us an attractive target for cyberattacks. Threat actors include nation-states, organized criminal groups, hacktivists, and insiders. Their methods are sophisticated and evolve constantly, and include:

- ransomware and other malware;
- phishing and social engineering;
- credential theft;
- denial-of-service attacks;
- exploitation of software vulnerabilities; and
- compromises of third-party providers and the software supply chain.

The increasing use of artificial intelligence by threat actors may make attacks more effective and harder to detect. Examples include more convincing impersonation and automated discovery of vulnerabilities.

We maintain an information security program that includes layered technical controls, identity and access management, threat intelligence, security monitoring, incident response planning, and employee training. However, no program can eliminate the risk of a successful attack. We, our clients, and our third-party providers have been and will continue to be targets of attempted attacks. We may not detect an intrusion promptly, and some techniques are not recognized until after they have been used against us or others.

A successful incident could result in:

- fraudulent transfers of client or Company funds or securities;
- theft, corruption, or destruction of data;
- disruption of our ability to process transactions or provide services, potentially across multiple markets or clients at once;
- regulatory investigations, fines, and remediation requirements;
- litigation and liability to clients and others; and
- significant damage to our reputation and client relationships.

Our hybrid working model, the migration of workloads to cloud environments, and our reliance on interconnected market infrastructures and third parties increase our attack surface. We are also subject to rapid incident reporting and disclosure obligations, including the SEC's rules requiring current disclosure of material cybersecurity incidents and federal banking agency notification requirements. Compliance with these obligations may require us to make disclosures before we fully understand an incident's scope. The costs of preventing, detecting, and responding to cyber threats are significant and are likely to increase. Additional information is included in Part I, Item 1C, "Cybersecurity."

**Our multi-year technology modernization program may not be completed on schedule or within budget, may disrupt our operations, or may fail to deliver the expected benefits.**

We are executing a multi-year program to modernize our core custody and accounting infrastructure. The program's goals are to support real-time processing, improve scalability, and reduce the complexity associated with legacy systems. We are also migrating appropriate workloads to cloud environments, building a unified data architecture, and expanding automation. These initiatives require substantial investment and management attention, and involve significant execution risk.

Large-scale technology transformations are complex and frequently encounter delays, cost overruns, and unanticipated technical challenges. Migrating client data and processing from legacy systems to new platforms creates the risk of errors, data loss, service interruptions, and control gaps during transition. During the transition, we must operate legacy and new systems simultaneously, which increases cost and operational complexity. The program depends on skilled technology professionals and third-party vendors, whose availability and performance we do not fully control.

If we do not complete the program successfully, or if the new systems do not perform as expected, we may:

- fail to achieve anticipated efficiencies;
- be unable to meet client expectations for data and digital capabilities;
- lose ground to competitors that modernize more effectively; and
- incur impairment charges on capitalized software.

Conversely, continued reliance on legacy systems carries its own risks, including higher maintenance costs, limited flexibility, and increased vulnerability to failure.

**We depend on third parties, including subcustodians, market infrastructures, and technology and data providers. Their failure to perform could disrupt our services and cause losses.**

We rely extensively on third parties to deliver our services, including:

- a global network of subcustodian banks that provides access to markets where we do not maintain a direct presence;
- central securities depositories, payment systems, and clearing organizations; and
- data providers, software vendors, cloud service providers, and outsourcing firms.

We do not control these parties, and their failures, disruptions, insolvency, cyber incidents, or misconduct could impair our ability to serve clients and could result in losses for which we are responsible.

Subcustodian risk is particularly significant. In some jurisdictions, our contractual or regulatory obligations to clients may make us liable for the acts or omissions of subcustodians, or for the loss of assets held through them, even where we exercised appropriate care in selecting and monitoring them. Under European Union depositary rules, for example, depositaries can bear strict liability for the loss of financial instruments held in custody, including through delegates, subject to limited exceptions. In markets with less developed legal or operational infrastructure, the risk of asset loss, settlement failure, or governmental interference may be higher.

Concentration among technology providers, particularly cloud service providers, creates the risk that a single provider's failure could affect us and many other market participants at once. Regulators are increasingly focused on third-party and concentration risk, including through the European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act and updated U.S. interagency guidance. These developments may increase our compliance costs and constrain how we use third parties. Our third-party risk management program may not identify or mitigate all risks associated with these relationships.

**Changes in market structure, including shortened settlement cycles and new central clearing requirements, could increase our operational risk and costs.**

The financial markets in which we operate are undergoing significant structural change. The SEC adopted rules shortening the standard settlement cycle for most U.S. broker-dealer securities transactions, and similar changes are under consideration in other markets. A shorter cycle compresses the time available to:

- confirm trades;
- arrange foreign exchange;
- recall loaned securities;
- fund transactions; and
- resolve exceptions.

This compression is particularly challenging for non-U.S. clients operating in different time zones. It increases the risk of settlement failures, funding shortfalls, and errors, and may require us to extend more intraday credit or to operate with greater staffing and automation.

The SEC also adopted rules requiring central clearing of certain secondary market transactions in U.S. Treasury securities. These rules will affect the repurchase agreement, collateral management, and settlement activities of our clients and of the Bank. Changes in clearing models could require operational changes, affect the economics of our services, and increase our exposure to clearing organizations. We have devoted, and will continue to devote, significant resources to these changes. If we or our clients are not adequately prepared, we could experience operational failures, client dissatisfaction, and losses.

**Natural disasters, climate-related physical events, infrastructure failures, and other business disruptions could impair our operations.**

Our operations depend on the continuous availability of our facilities, data centers, personnel, and communications and power infrastructure. These could be disrupted by:

- severe weather, floods, wildfires, earthquakes, and other natural disasters, which may become more frequent or severe as a result of climate change;
- power outages or telecommunications failures;
- pandemics;
- terrorism or civil unrest; and
- other events.

We maintain business continuity and disaster recovery plans, geographically dispersed data centers, and alternate operating sites, and we conduct periodic testing. However, these measures may not be effective in every circumstance. A widespread event could affect primary and backup sites simultaneously, or could affect the third parties and market infrastructures on which we depend.

Operational resilience requirements in the United States, the United Kingdom, the European Union, and elsewhere require us to identify important business services and remain within defined impact tolerances. Failure to meet these requirements could result in regulatory consequences. Climate-related physical events could also affect the value of real estate, farmland, and other assets held in the trusts we administer or securing our loans, as well as our clients' businesses more broadly.

**Our use of models, analytics, and artificial intelligence exposes us to risks of error, bias, and misuse.**

We use quantitative models across our business, including models for:

- risk measurement, capital planning, and stress testing;
- liquidity and interest rate risk management;
- credit loss estimation;
- investment management and portfolio construction;
- valuation; and
- anti-money laundering and fraud detection.

We also deploy machine learning and other advanced analytical tools in areas such as reconciliation, exception management, document intake, and anomaly detection, and we are evaluating generative artificial intelligence capabilities.

Models are simplifications of reality and depend on assumptions and data that may be incorrect or incomplete, or may not hold in changing conditions. A model may perform well under historical conditions but fail under unprecedented ones, such as the rapid rate increases and deposit movements of recent periods. Errors in model design, implementation, or use could lead to poor business decisions, inaccurate financial reporting, regulatory violations, investment losses for clients, or inadequate capital or liquidity.

Artificial intelligence tools present additional risks:

- they may produce inaccurate or unexplainable outputs;
- they may reflect biases in training data;
- they may expose confidential information; and
- they may infringe intellectual property rights.

The regulatory landscape for artificial intelligence is evolving rapidly. The SEC has proposed rules relating to the use of predictive data analytics by investment advisers and broker-dealers, and other jurisdictions have adopted or proposed new requirements. Our model risk management and governance processes may not identify or prevent all model failures.

**Failure to manage, protect, and use data effectively could harm our business and expose us to regulatory and legal risk.**

Data is increasingly central to the services we provide and to our clients' expectations. We aggregate, process, and deliver large volumes of investment, transaction, and personal data. Errors in data quality, lineage, or delivery could cause clients to make incorrect decisions, impair regulatory reporting, and expose us to liability.

We are subject to extensive and evolving privacy and data protection laws. These include:

- the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act;

- the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act, which includes a private right of action and has generated substantial litigation; and

Data localization requirements in certain jurisdictions could require us to change our operations. Violations could result in significant fines, litigation, and reputational harm.

**Misconduct or fraud by employees, clients, or third parties could cause losses and damage our reputation.**

We are exposed to the risk of misconduct by our employees, including:

- unauthorized transactions;

If our regulators determine that our risk management or controls are deficient, they could require remediation, restrict our activities or growth, or take enforcement action. A failure of our risk management framework could result in significant losses and damage to our reputation.

### Fiduciary, Investment Management, and Business-Specific Risks

**We act as a fiduciary for many clients and could incur significant liability if we fail to meet our fiduciary obligations.**

We act in fiduciary capacities in many of our businesses. These include roles as:

- trustee, co-trustee, executor, personal representative, and agent for personal trusts and estates;
- investment manager and investment adviser to institutional and individual clients;
- discretionary manager in OCIO mandates;
- trustee and investment manager for employee benefit plans subject to ERISA; and
- agent lender in securities lending.

In these roles, we owe duties of loyalty, prudence, and impartiality, and we must manage conflicts of interest and follow governing instruments and applicable law.

Fiduciary claims can be significant. For example, beneficiaries may allege that we:

- made imprudent investment decisions or failed to diversify;
- made improper discretionary distributions or failed to act impartially among beneficiaries;
- mishandled hard-to-value assets such as closely held businesses, farmland, or mineral interests; or
- favored affiliated products, including IAM strategies, over alternatives.

Disputes among family members and beneficiaries can generate prolonged litigation, and courts may impose surcharges or remove us as fiduciary. ERISA fiduciary obligations carry personal liability for losses resulting from breaches and prohibit many transactions absent an exemption. The U.S. Department of Labor's October 2023 proposal to amend the definition of investment advice fiduciary could, if adopted, expand the circumstances in which our activities are subject to ERISA fiduciary standards and prohibited transaction rules. That could require changes to our products, compensation, and practices.

The use of affiliated products and services by our fiduciary businesses creates inherent conflicts of interest. Affiliated products and services include IAM-managed funds, the Bank's deposit and foreign exchange services, and Ironwood Securities LLC's distribution services. Our policies are designed to manage these conflicts, but they may be challenged by clients, plaintiffs, or regulators. Fiduciary failures could result in significant liability, regulatory sanctions, and reputational harm.

**Poor investment performance, client outflows, or shifts in client preferences could reduce Investment Management revenues.**

Investment Management revenues depend primarily on the level of assets under management. That level depends on market values, client flows, and the mix of products we manage. Investment performance is a key driver of flows. If our strategies underperform their benchmarks or competitors, clients may withdraw assets, investment consultants may stop recommending our strategies, and we may have difficulty attracting new assets. Performance can be affected by:

- market conditions;
- investment decisions;
- the departure of key investment professionals; and
- capacity constraints.

Past performance may not be repeated.

The investment management industry has experienced a sustained shift toward lower-cost index products and fee compression in many active strategies. Our revenues may decline if clients move assets from higher-fee active strategies to lower-fee index or factor strategies, even if total assets under management remain stable. Performance-based fees, where applicable, are inherently variable and may be lower in periods of weak performance.

Demand for our strategies may also be affected by:

- changes in investor preferences, including demand for alternatives and private markets, which we offer more selectively than some competitors;
- the growth of direct indexing and model portfolios; and
- shifts in retirement plan design.

A substantial portion of IAM's assets may be concentrated in particular strategies, such as liquidity management. Poor performance or adverse developments affecting those strategies could have an outsized effect. We may also be unable to develop or acquire new capabilities quickly enough to meet changing client demand.

**Our money market funds and other liquidity products are subject to risks of significant redemptions, regulatory change, and pressure to provide financial support.**

IAM manages money market funds and other liquidity products. These products are an important complement to our Asset Servicing business, serving as vehicles for clients' operating cash and for cash collateral from securities lending. During periods of market stress, money market funds may experience large redemptions that require the sale of portfolio holdings at depressed prices. Such sales could cause a fund's net asset value to decline. In past periods of severe market disruption, sponsors of money market funds across the industry have faced pressure to provide financial support to their funds, even without a legal obligation to do so.

If we chose to provide support to a fund we sponsor, whether for reputational or client relationship reasons, we could incur losses. Such support could also have adverse regulatory capital, accounting, and Volcker Rule consequences, and would be subject to Sections 23A and 23B of the Federal Reserve Act to the extent it involves the Bank. If we chose not to provide support, we could suffer reputational harm and loss of client relationships.

In 2023, the SEC adopted amendments to money market fund rules that include, among other things, mandatory liquidity fees for certain funds and increased liquidity requirements. These changes may reduce investor demand for certain money market fund types, require operational changes, and increase our costs. In a low interest rate environment, we may also waive fees on money market funds to avoid negative yields, which would reduce our revenues.

**Our securities lending activities expose us and our clients to credit, market, and operational risks, including under indemnification obligations.**

As agent lender, we lend clients' securities to approved borrowers in exchange for collateral, and we reinvest cash collateral in accordance with client guidelines. This may include investment in collective vehicles managed by IAM. In certain circumstances, we indemnify clients against the failure of a borrower to return loaned securities. That indemnification is generally limited to any shortfall between the market value of the loaned securities and the value of the collateral held.

If a borrower defaults during a period of rapid market movement, the collateral may be insufficient, and we may be required to cover the shortfall. Because borrowers tend to be large financial institutions, defaults may coincide with broader market stress, when collateral values are most volatile and liquidation is most difficult. The aggregate notional amount of indemnified securities on loan can be substantial relative to our capital.

Cash collateral reinvestment also creates risk. Reinvestment vehicles may experience losses or illiquidity, which could harm clients and lead to claims, litigation, or client relationship pressure for us to absorb losses. Regulatory changes affecting securities lending could further reduce the revenue and profitability of our program, including new reporting requirements, capital treatment of indemnification, and changes to borrower demand from capital and clearing rules.

**Servicing private markets and alternative assets involves valuation complexity and operational risks that differ from those associated with traditional assets.**

We view private markets as an important growth opportunity for our Asset Servicing business. Our services in this area include fund administration, capital call and distribution processing, waterfall and carried interest calculations, and loan administration for private credit vehicles. These activities involve assets that often lack observable prices, are documented through bespoke legal agreements, and require significant manual processing and judgment. Errors in calculations, investor allocations, or reporting could cause investor harm and result in claims against us.

Valuation of private assets is inherently subjective. While valuation responsibility generally rests with fund managers, we may face claims if we are perceived to have failed to apply appropriate procedures or controls. Regulatory scrutiny of private funds and their service providers is increasing, including through the SEC's private fund adviser rules, which have been the subject of litigation. Growth in this business requires continued investment in specialized technology and personnel, and we may not achieve the expected returns on those investments.

**Fee pressure, contract renegotiations, and the loss of significant client relationships could reduce our revenues.**

The asset servicing industry has experienced persistent fee pressure, particularly in the custody of public market assets. Clients periodically conduct formal requests for proposals, often with the assistance of consultants, and these processes frequently result in fee reductions even when we retain the relationship. Consolidation among asset managers and asset owners can increase clients' bargaining power and may lead to the transfer of assets to a competitor that serves the acquiring entity.

Although no single client accounted for a material portion of our consolidated revenues in fiscal 2023, certain large institutional relationships generate significant revenues. Our service agreements generally may be terminated by the client on notice, subject to transition provisions. Losing one or more of these relationships, or granting significant fee concessions to retain them, could adversely affect our results.

Clients may also choose to bring functions in-house or move them to lower-cost providers. They may also unbundle services so that the integrated relationships we seek become more competitive and less profitable. Many of our contracts include service level commitments and performance standards, and failure to meet them could result in fee reductions or termination.

**Large new mandates require significant up-front investment and carry implementation risk, and expected revenues may not be realized.**

New Asset Servicing and middle office outsourcing mandates frequently require extended onboarding periods. During these periods, we incur substantial implementation costs before the associated revenue is fully realized. Transitions of custody, accounting, and middle office functions are complex. They involve the conversion of large volumes of data, the establishment of new processes and interfaces, and coordination with clients' prior providers.

Implementation delays, cost overruns, or errors could:

- damage the client relationship;
- reduce the profitability of the mandate; or
- result in contractual penalties or termination.

Clients may also delay or cancel planned transitions, or may reduce the scope of services after contract signing, leaving us with costs that are not recovered. Because we may pursue multiple large implementations concurrently, operational strain and resource constraints may compound these risks. The timing of revenue from new business is uncertain, and announced wins may not translate into revenue on the schedule we anticipate.

**Our wealth and fiduciary business depends on retaining experienced relationship professionals and on regional market conditions.**

Our Wealth and Fiduciary Services segment relies on dedicated teams of relationship advisors, portfolio managers, wealth planners, fiduciary officers, and private bankers who develop close, long-term relationships with clients. Competition for experienced professionals is intense, and competitors may offer compensation packages, equity ownership, or independence that we cannot or choose not to match. When a relationship professional leaves, clients may follow, particularly in the wealth management industry, where individual advisor relationships are often a primary reason clients choose a provider.

The segment also has a significant concentration in the Midwest. Adverse economic, demographic, or competitive developments in that region could disproportionately affect its growth and profitability. Our efforts to expand into other regions require investment in recruitment, offices, and brand development and may not succeed. Generational wealth transfers create both an opportunity and a risk: heirs may not maintain relationships established by prior generations.

We operate in highly competitive markets. Our competitors include:

- large global custody banks and universal banks with securities services operations;
- specialized fund administrators;
- investment management firms;
- broker-dealers, wealth management firms, trust companies, private banks, and independent registered investment advisers;

Some competitors are larger than we are, have greater financial resources or broader geographic reach, or have more extensive technology budgets. Some are subject to less extensive regulation, or to different capital and liquidity requirements, which may allow them to price more aggressively or invest more heavily.

Competition is based on many factors, including:

- service quality and breadth;
- technology and data capabilities;
- operational reliability;
- investment performance;
- financial strength and credit ratings;
- pricing; and
- client relationships.

Our competitors may develop superior technology, offer lower prices, or form alliances that give them advantages. Consolidation among providers may create competitors with greater scale.

New entrants, including financial technology firms, may target profitable segments of our business with specialized services, novel delivery models, or lower prices. They may also disintermediate traditional providers through new technologies such as distributed ledger systems, tokenization, and direct data delivery. If we cannot compete effectively, we may lose clients, experience fee compression, or be required to increase investment, any of which could harm our results.

**We may not execute our strategy successfully or achieve our productivity and expense objectives.**

Our strategy depends on several priorities:

- deepening relationships with core institutional clients;
- investing in technology and data platforms;
- growing our investment management franchise;
- expanding our wealth and fiduciary business;
- maintaining a strong balance sheet; and
- operating efficiently.

Executing this strategy requires significant investment, effective management, and favorable market conditions. We may fail to execute our initiatives as planned, our investments may not generate expected returns, and competitors may respond in ways that diminish our advantages.

We also seek to improve productivity through process simplification, location strategy, automation, and disciplined expense management. These initiatives may not achieve the expected savings. They may cost more than anticipated or disrupt operations, and they could affect employee morale and retention. Location strategy initiatives, including shifting activities to lower-cost locations, carry operational, regulatory, and geopolitical risks. If we cannot balance the need to invest for growth against the need to control expenses, our margins and competitive position could suffer.

**Acquisitions, partnerships, divestitures, and new products and services involve significant risks.**

We continue to evaluate opportunities to expand our capabilities, including in alternatives and private markets, through organic investment, partnerships, and acquisitions. Acquisitions require regulatory approval. Regulators consider factors such as financial stability, competitive effects, community needs, and anti-money laundering effectiveness, and approval may be delayed, conditioned, or denied. Acquisitions also involve risks, including:

- overpaying;
- failing to integrate operations, technology, and personnel effectively;
- losing clients and key employees of the acquired business;
- assuming unknown liabilities; and
- diverting management attention.

To the extent we record goodwill and other intangible assets in connection with past or future acquisitions, changes in business conditions could require us to recognize impairment charges.

Partnerships and joint ventures depend on the performance of parties we do not control. Divestitures may result in losses or ongoing obligations. New products, services, and markets involve uncertain demand and new risks, and require investment that may not be recovered. Our risk management and controls may not be adequate for new activities. Regulatory restrictions, including the Volcker Rule's limits on sponsoring and investing in covered funds, may also constrain our ability to pursue certain opportunities.

**Developments in digital assets, tokenization, and distributed ledger technology could disrupt our business model or expose us to new risks.**

The development of tokenized and digital representations of traditional assets, and the potential use of distributed ledger technology in clearing, settlement, and recordkeeping, could fundamentally change the market infrastructure on which custody and fund administration rest. If these technologies achieve broad adoption, they could reduce the need for certain intermediary services we provide or enable new competitors to offer them more efficiently.

We participate in industry working groups and pilot programs and evaluate potential offerings in light of client demand, regulatory developments, and our risk appetite. Investments in these areas may not produce returns, and the regulatory treatment of digital assets for banking organizations remains uncertain and restrictive. Engaging in digital asset activities could expose us to:

- novel operational, legal, and cybersecurity risks;
- reputational risks associated with the broader digital asset sector; and
- regulatory scrutiny.

Conversely, if we move too slowly, we may lose clients and opportunities to competitors.

**Our success depends on attracting, developing, and retaining skilled employees and senior leaders.**

Our client service, operations, technology, investment performance, and risk management depend on skilled professionals. Competition for talent in technology, data science, cybersecurity, investment management, wealth management, and risk and compliance is intense, both within financial services and from other industries. Regulatory limits on incentive compensation may restrict our ability to compete with less regulated firms. These limits include deferral, performance adjustment, and clawback requirements, as well as rules proposed under Section 956 of the Dodd-Frank Act.

- wage inflation; and
- the attractiveness of our culture.

Our clients entrust us with the safekeeping of their assets and, in many cases, with their operating liquidity and the administration of their wealth. Trust in our integrity, reliability, financial strength, and competence is therefore fundamental to our franchise. Reputational harm could arise from many sources, including:

- operational failures;
- cybersecurity incidents;
- misconduct;
- litigation or regulatory actions;
- poor investment performance;

### Regulatory, Legal, and Compliance Risks

**We are subject to extensive and evolving regulation and supervision, and changes in laws, regulations, or supervisory expectations could materially affect our business.**

We are subject to comprehensive regulation and supervision by U.S. federal and state authorities and by authorities in the other jurisdictions in which we operate. These include:

- the Federal Reserve, the IDFPR, the FDIC, and the SEC;
- the CFTC, FINRA, and the Department of Labor; and
- numerous non-U.S. regulators.

These regulations are designed primarily to protect depositors, clients, the Deposit Insurance Fund, the financial system, and market integrity, and not to protect our shareholders or creditors. They govern nearly every aspect of our business, including:

- capital, liquidity, and permissible activities;
- dividends and acquisitions;
- risk management and compensation;
- consumer and client protection; and
- recordkeeping and reporting.

The regulatory environment for large banking organizations has become more uncertain and potentially more stringent following the 2023 bank failures. Pending and potential changes include:

- the Basel III capital proposal;
- long-term debt requirements;
- changes to resolution planning requirements;
- revisions to liquidity requirements, including the treatment of uninsured deposits and discount window readiness;
- changes to stress testing; and
- heightened supervisory expectations.

In addition, the SEC has adopted or proposed rules affecting investment advisers, investment companies, private funds, custody, market structure, and cybersecurity. The SEC's proposed changes to the rules governing the safeguarding of client assets by investment advisers could, if adopted, significantly affect the role, obligations, and liabilities of qualified custodians such as the Bank.

We are subject to enhanced prudential standards appropriate to our size and risk profile under the Federal Reserve's tailoring framework. Growth in our size or activities, or changes to the framework, could subject us to more stringent requirements. Compliance with new and changing requirements is costly, consumes management attention, and may require us to change our business practices, reduce or exit certain activities, or reprice products. Regulatory changes may also apply differently to us than to our competitors, including non-bank competitors and non-U.S. institutions, which could disadvantage us competitively.

**Failure to comply with regulatory requirements, or adverse supervisory assessments, could result in enforcement actions, restrictions on our activities, and loss of financial holding company status.**

Our regulators conduct regular examinations and have broad discretion to evaluate our financial condition, risk management, controls, and compliance. They assign confidential supervisory ratings that can affect our ability to engage in new activities, make acquisitions, and pay dividends. They may also issue matters requiring attention and other findings that require costly remediation.

If our regulators determine that we have violated laws, engaged in unsafe or unsound practices, or failed to meet supervisory expectations, they could:

- issue cease and desist orders;
- impose civil money penalties;
- require the removal of officers or directors;
- restrict growth or activities; or
- in the case of the Bank, appoint a conservator or receiver.

The supervisory approach to large banking organizations has become more rigorous, and enforcement actions against financial institutions have resulted in significant penalties and lengthy remediation programs.

To maintain financial holding company status, we and the Bank must remain well capitalized and well managed, and the Bank must maintain at least a satisfactory CRA rating. The Bank is evaluated as a wholesale or limited-purpose institution for CRA purposes. The October 2023 revisions to the CRA framework will change how its performance is assessed. If we fail to meet these requirements, the Federal Reserve could restrict our activities and acquisitions and could ultimately require us to divest the Bank or cease activities permissible only for financial holding companies, such as certain securities and investment activities.

**Anti-money laundering, sanctions, and anti-corruption requirements are complex, and failure to comply could result in substantial penalties and reputational harm.**

We are subject to the Bank Secrecy Act, as amended by the USA PATRIOT Act and the Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020, and to related regulations. These require risk-based programs for customer due diligence, beneficial ownership identification, transaction monitoring, and suspicious activity reporting. We are also subject to economic sanctions administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control and by authorities in other jurisdictions, and to anti-corruption laws including the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the United Kingdom's Bribery Act.

Our global custody, payment, foreign exchange, and fund administration activities involve high volumes of cross-border transactions and assets held for a wide range of clients, funds, and investors. That creates significant compliance complexity. The scope and complexity of sanctions programs have increased significantly, including in connection with the conflict involving Russia and Ukraine, and new designations and restrictions can occur with little notice. Identifying sanctioned ownership of securities held in custody, or sanctioned investors in funds we administer, can be difficult, particularly through omnibus and intermediary structures. Sanctions regimes in different jurisdictions may conflict, placing us in positions where compliance with one regime risks violating another.

Regulators have imposed very large penalties on financial institutions for compliance failures in these areas. Failures could also result in restrictions on our activities, monitorships, and denial of applications for acquisitions. The costs of maintaining compliant programs are significant and increasing.

**Resolution planning requirements could require us to change our structure or operations, and the resolution of the Company or the Bank could adversely affect shareholders and creditors.**

We must periodically submit resolution plans to the Federal Reserve and the FDIC, and the Bank must submit resolution plans to the FDIC. If the regulators jointly determine that our plan is not credible and we do not remedy the deficiencies, they could:

- impose more stringent capital, leverage, or liquidity requirements;
- restrict our growth or activities; or
- ultimately require us to divest assets or operations.

The FDIC's August 2023 proposal to revise insured depository institution resolution planning requirements, and related agency guidance, could increase our compliance obligations and may require changes to our legal entity structure, intercompany arrangements, and operations.

If we were to experience material financial distress, our resolution strategy, or the actions of regulators under the FDIA or the orderly liquidation authority of the Dodd-Frank Act, could result in significant losses to our shareholders and unsecured creditors. Shareholders would generally absorb losses before creditors, and the rights of creditors and shareholders in an orderly liquidation proceeding could differ from those available in bankruptcy.

**FDIC deposit insurance assessments, including special assessments, could increase our expenses.**

The Bank pays deposit insurance assessments to the FDIC based on a risk-based system. In November 2023, the FDIC adopted a final rule imposing a special assessment to recover losses to the Deposit Insurance Fund arising from the protection of uninsured depositors in connection with certain bank failures earlier in 2023. The amount of the special assessment is based on each institution's uninsured deposits. Because a substantial portion of the Bank's deposits are held by institutional clients and exceed insurance limits, this assessment methodology affects us more than institutions with predominantly insured deposits.

The FDIC may revise its estimate of losses, which could change the amount of the special assessment. It may also increase regular assessment rates or impose additional special assessments in the future. Policy proposals to change deposit insurance coverage, including targeted increases for business accounts, could also affect our assessment costs and the competitive dynamics for institutional deposits.

**Our non-U.S. operations are subject to regulatory, legal, political, and operational risks that differ from those in the United States.**

We maintain operations in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Channel Islands, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Australia, among other locations. These operations are subject to regulation by local authorities in addition to the Federal Reserve. Non-U.S. regulators may impose requirements that differ from or exceed U.S. requirements. These may cover:

- capital and liquidity;
- governance and local management presence;
- outsourcing;
- data localization;
- client asset protection; and
- recovery and resolution planning.

Some regimes may restrict the ability of our local entities to transfer capital, liquidity, or data to other parts of the Company, which could trap resources and complicate our global operating model.

Our European Union depositary activities are subject to the Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities Directive and the Alternative Investment Fund Managers Directive. These impose significant duties and liabilities, including potential strict liability for the loss of financial instruments held in custody. Revisions to these directives will impose additional obligations over time. The U.K.'s departure from the European Union has resulted, and may continue to result, in regulatory divergence that requires us to maintain separate arrangements.

Political instability, changes in government policy, expropriation, currency controls, and differences in legal systems may also affect our ability to operate and to enforce our rights in certain jurisdictions.

**We are subject to litigation, regulatory investigations, and other proceedings that could result in significant liability and reputational harm.**

In the ordinary course of business, we are subject to claims, lawsuits, regulatory investigations, and other proceedings, including:

### Risks Related to Our Common Stock

**The market price of our common stock may be volatile and could decline significantly.**

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly for many reasons, some of which are unrelated to our operating performance. These include:

- actual or anticipated variations in our results;
- changes in financial estimates or recommendations by securities analysts;
- market and economic conditions;
- developments affecting the financial services industry, such as the 2023 bank failures;
- regulatory changes;
- announcements by us or our competitors;
- changes in our credit ratings;
- changes in dividend or repurchase policies; and
- perceptions of our financial condition.

Financial services stocks can be particularly sensitive to concerns about liquidity, capital, and contagion, and declines may occur rapidly. Sales of substantial amounts of our common stock, or the perception that such sales could occur, could also depress the market price. Our common stock is subordinate to all of our existing and future indebtedness and to any preferred stock we may issue, and it is effectively subordinated to the obligations of our subsidiaries.

**Our ability to pay dividends and repurchase shares depends on regulatory approvals, our capital position, and the discretion of our Board of Directors.**

Holders of our common stock are entitled to receive dividends only when, as, and if declared by our Board of Directors. Our capital distributions are subject to:

- the Federal Reserve's capital planning and stress capital buffer requirements;
- regulatory limits on dividends from our subsidiaries;
- the Federal Reserve's policy that bank holding companies should generally pay dividends only from current earnings; and
- the terms of any preferred stock or debt instruments we may issue.

If we fail to maintain capital above applicable buffer requirements, we would be subject to automatic restrictions on distributions. Pending regulatory changes, including the Basel III proposal and long-term debt requirements, could reduce the capital available for distribution. We cannot assure you that we will continue to pay dividends at current levels or at all, or that we will repurchase shares in any particular amount. A reduction or suspension of dividends or repurchases could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

**Banking laws and provisions of Illinois law and our organizational documents could delay or prevent a change in control.**

The BHC Act and the Change in Bank Control Act require persons seeking to acquire control of a bank holding company to provide notice to, or obtain approval from, the Federal Reserve. Acquisitions of ten percent or more of our voting securities may be presumed to constitute control in certain circumstances. Illinois law imposes additional requirements on changes in control of Illinois-chartered banks. Non-U.S. regulators may impose their own approval requirements for changes in control of our regulated subsidiaries in their jurisdictions.

These requirements, together with provisions of Illinois corporate law and of our articles of incorporation and by-laws, could have the effect of delaying, deterring, or preventing a merger, acquisition, or other change in control. Those organizational provisions include the ability of our Board of Directors to issue preferred stock without shareholder approval and advance notice requirements for shareholder proposals. These effects could arise even if a change in control would be beneficial to our shareholders, and could depress the market price of our common stock.
