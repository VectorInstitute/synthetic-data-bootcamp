#### Revenue Recognition — Principal Versus Agent Determinations for Interchange, Network Assessments, and Other Third-Party Fees

*Description of the matter.* As described in Notes 2 and 3, the Company recognized revenues of $34,182 million for the year ended December 31, 2023. Many of the Company's arrangements involve interchange fees remitted to card issuers, assessments remitted to payment networks, and other fees paid to third parties. For each arrangement, management determines whether the Company controls the specified service before it is transferred to the client. If it does, the Company acts as principal and presents revenue gross of these third-party fees. If it does not, the Company acts as agent and presents revenue net of these fees.

These determinations are made at the contract or product level. They require management to evaluate several indicators, including:

- which party is primarily responsible for fulfilling the promise to the client;
- whether the Company has discretion in establishing pricing; and
- whether the Company bears risk relating to the service.

The volume and diversity of the Company's arrangements across its Merchant Solutions, Issuer and Network Services, and Enterprise and Banking Payments segments, together with the significance of pass-through amounts, required a high degree of auditor judgment and increased audit effort. In particular, we focused on evaluating management's conclusions for arrangements with differing contractual terms, pricing structures, and fulfillment responsibilities.

*How we addressed the matter in our audit.* Our procedures included the following, among others:

- We tested the design and operating effectiveness of controls over the Company's evaluation of principal-versus-agent considerations. These included controls over the review of new and modified contract templates, product structures, and pricing models.
- We obtained an understanding of the Company's revenue streams and the related flows of funds among the Company, its clients, card issuers, payment networks, sponsor banks, and other third parties.
- For a sample of arrangements, we read the underlying contracts and evaluated management's assessment of the control indicators. We independently assessed whether the Company's conclusions were consistent with the contractual terms and the substance of the arrangements.
- We evaluated whether the Company's conclusions were applied consistently to arrangements with similar characteristics.
- We tested the completeness and accuracy of the data used to quantify interchange, network assessments, and other third-party fees presented gross in revenues and operating expenses.
- We evaluated the adequacy of the Company's disclosures regarding principal-versus-agent considerations.

#### Goodwill — Assessment of Reporting Unit Fair Values

*Description of the matter.* As described in Notes 2 and 7, the Company's goodwill balance was $11,100 million at December 31, 2023. Goodwill is tested for impairment at the reporting unit level at least annually, and more frequently if events or circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value.

When performing a quantitative test, management estimates the fair value of each reporting unit using a combination of an income approach based on discounted cash flows and a market approach based on comparable companies. Significant assumptions include:

- projected revenue growth rates;
- projected operating margins;
- terminal growth rates;
- discount rates; and
- the selection of comparable companies and valuation multiples.

Auditing these estimates was complex and highly judgmental. The assumptions are sensitive to economic conditions, competitive and pricing dynamics, the pace of client adoption of the Company's modernized platforms, and regulatory developments affecting the payments industry. Our procedures to evaluate these assumptions required the involvement of professionals with specialized skill and knowledge in valuation.

*How we addressed the matter in our audit.* Our procedures included the following, among others:

- We tested the design and operating effectiveness of controls over the Company's goodwill impairment assessment. These included controls over the development of forecasts and the selection of discount rates and other valuation assumptions.
- We evaluated management's projections by comparing them to historical results, to internal communications to management and the Board of Directors, and to industry and market data.
- We assessed the reasonableness of management's projections in light of the Company's strategic initiatives, including platform modernization and client migration programs.
- With the assistance of our valuation professionals, we evaluated the valuation methodologies used by management. We also evaluated the discount rates and terminal growth rates by developing independent ranges and comparing them to those selected by management.
- We performed sensitivity analyses of significant assumptions to evaluate how changes in those assumptions would affect the fair values of the reporting units.
- We tested the reconciliation of the aggregate fair values of the reporting units to the Company's market capitalization.
- We evaluated the adequacy of the Company's disclosures.

#### Income Taxes — Uncertain Tax Positions and the Effective Tax Rate

*Description of the matter.* As described in Notes 2 and 12, the Company recognized income tax expense of $1,691 million on income before income taxes of $18,035 million for the year ended December 31, 2023, an effective tax rate of approximately 9.4%. The Company operates in numerous U.S. and foreign jurisdictions, and its effective tax rate is affected by the jurisdictional mix of earnings, tax credits and incentives, the tax effects of share-based compensation, and changes in reserves for uncertain tax positions.

Management recognizes the benefit of an uncertain tax position only if it is more likely than not that the position will be sustained upon examination based on its technical merits. Auditing the identification and measurement of uncertain tax positions was challenging. It involved complex interpretations of tax laws in multiple jurisdictions, the evaluation of intercompany arrangements and transfer pricing, and judgment about the outcomes of examinations by taxing authorities. Our procedures required the involvement of professionals with specialized skill and knowledge in income taxes.

*How we addressed the matter in our audit.* Our procedures included the following, among others:

- We tested the design and operating effectiveness of controls over the Company's process for identifying, recognizing, and measuring uncertain tax positions, and over the computation of the income tax provision.
- With the assistance of our income tax professionals, we evaluated the technical merits of significant tax positions. We considered applicable tax laws, regulations, and case law; correspondence with taxing authorities; and the Company's transfer pricing documentation.
- We evaluated management's assessment of the measurement of the benefit expected to be realized upon ultimate settlement.
- We assessed the jurisdictional allocation of income before income taxes and tested the computation of the reconciliation of income tax expense at the U.S. federal statutory rate to reported income tax expense.
- We evaluated the adequacy of the Company's income tax disclosures.

### Definition and Limitations of Internal Control Over Financial Reporting

A company's internal control over financial reporting is a process designed to provide reasonable assurance regarding the reliability of financial reporting and the preparation of financial statements for external purposes in accordance with generally accepted accounting principles. A company's internal control over financial reporting includes those policies and procedures that:

1. pertain to the maintenance of records that, in reasonable detail, accurately and fairly reflect the transactions and dispositions of the assets of the company;
2. provide reasonable assurance that transactions are recorded as necessary to permit preparation of financial statements in accordance with generally accepted accounting principles, and that receipts and expenditures of the company are being made only in accordance with authorizations of management and directors of the company; and
3. provide reasonable assurance regarding prevention or timely detection of unauthorized acquisition, use, or disposition of the company's assets that could have a material effect on the financial statements.

Because of its inherent limitations, internal control over financial reporting may not prevent or detect misstatements. Also, projections of any evaluation of effectiveness to future periods are subject to the risk that controls may become inadequate because of changes in conditions, or that the degree of compliance with the policies or procedures may deteriorate.

# Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements

## Note 1 — Organization, Nature of Business, and Basis of Presentation

### Organization and Nature of Business

Stonegate Networks Group, Inc., together with its consolidated subsidiaries ("Stonegate," the "Company," "we," "us," or "our"), is a global provider of payment processing, transaction infrastructure, and financial technology services. Our principal executive offices are located in New York, New York.

We enable merchants, financial institutions, corporations, governments, and technology platforms to accept, issue, move, and manage money across physical, digital, and embedded channels. Our services include the authorization, clearing, settlement, risk management, data, and value-added services that allow payments to occur securely and reliably.

We organize our operations into three reportable segments:

- **Merchant Solutions** provides payment acceptance and processing, cloud-based point-of-sale software (Stonegate Commerce Point), gateway and e-commerce services (Stonegate Gateway and E-Commerce Services), integrated and embedded payments (Stonegate Connect), enterprise acceptance, hardware, and value-added services to merchants of all sizes.
- **Issuer and Network Services** provides issuer processing (including the Stonegate Card Platform), debit and network services, ATM processing, card production and fulfillment, fraud and risk solutions, customer engagement and digital services, commercial card solutions, and credit and analytics services to banks, credit unions, retailers, fintech companies, and program managers.
- **Enterprise and Banking Payments** provides payment hub and orchestration services (Stonegate Treasury Rail), real-time payments connectivity, bill payment and presentment, disbursements and payouts, cross-border payments, banking technology, and corporate payments and receivables solutions to financial institutions, corporations, billers, and public sector entities.

Enterprise-wide functions and activities that are not allocated to our reportable segments are reported in **Corporate and Other**. See Note 20 for additional information about our segments.

### Basis of Presentation

We prepared the accompanying consolidated financial statements in accordance with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States of America ("GAAP") and the rules and regulations of the Securities and Exchange Commission (the "SEC"). The consolidated financial statements include the accounts of Stonegate Networks Group, Inc. and its wholly owned and majority-owned subsidiaries, as well as any variable interest entities for which we are the primary beneficiary. All intercompany accounts and transactions have been eliminated in consolidation.

Our fiscal year ends on December 31. References to "fiscal 2023" or "2023" refer to the fiscal year ended December 31, 2023.

The consolidated balance sheet is presented on an unclassified basis, consistent with the presentation used by other entities whose balance sheets are significantly affected by settlement activities. Settlement-related assets and obligations, which turn over rapidly, are reflected within accounts receivable, other assets, accrued liabilities, and other liabilities, as described in Note 4. Short-term debt and long-term debt are presented separately. Short-term debt comprises borrowings and the current portion of long-term obligations due within twelve months of the balance sheet date.

Amounts in the primary financial statements are presented in whole U.S. dollars. Amounts in these notes are presented in millions of U.S. dollars unless otherwise indicated, and exact amounts are included in parentheses where useful for reference. Because amounts in the notes are rounded, totals may not equal the sum of their components, and percentages are calculated from unrounded amounts.

### Use of Estimates

Preparing financial statements in conformity with GAAP requires management to make estimates and assumptions. These affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses, as well as related disclosures, including disclosures of contingent assets and liabilities. Significant estimates and assumptions include those related to:

- revenue recognition, including principal-versus-agent determinations, the identification of performance obligations, the estimation of standalone selling prices, and the estimation of variable consideration;
- the amortization periods for capitalized client incentives and contract costs;
- the valuation of goodwill and intangible assets, including the estimation of reporting unit fair values and the useful lives of intangible assets;
- the allocation of purchase price in business combinations;
- the capitalization and useful lives of internal-use software;
- reserves for merchant chargeback losses and allowances for expected credit losses;
- income taxes, including uncertain tax positions and the realizability of deferred tax assets;
- share-based compensation, including the probability of achieving performance conditions;
- the fair value of financial instruments, including derivatives; and
- legal and regulatory contingencies.

We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions, and other assumptions that we believe are reasonable. Actual results could differ materially from these estimates.

### Reclassifications and Presentation

Certain amounts within the notes to the consolidated financial statements have been aggregated or presented in a manner consistent with how management evaluates the business. These presentation choices had no effect on reported net income, total assets, total liabilities, or total stockholders' equity.

## Note 2 — Summary of Significant Accounting Policies

### Principles of Consolidation

We consolidate entities in which we hold a controlling financial interest. We evaluate our interests in other entities to determine whether they are variable interest entities ("VIEs"). If an entity is a VIE, we consolidate it if we are the primary beneficiary. The primary beneficiary is the party that has both (1) the power to direct the activities that most significantly affect the VIE's economic performance and (2) the obligation to absorb losses of, or the right to receive benefits from, the VIE that could potentially be significant to the VIE.

We participate in certain joint ventures and revenue-sharing arrangements, including arrangements with financial institution partners in connection with merchant referral and distribution. For each arrangement, we evaluate whether consolidation or the equity method of accounting is appropriate. Investments in entities over which we have significant influence but not control are accounted for using the equity method. Our share of the earnings or losses of equity method investees is included in operating expenses or other components of operating results, depending on the nature of the investee's operations. Equity investments without readily determinable fair values in which we do not have significant influence are measured at cost, less impairment, adjusted for observable price changes in orderly transactions for identical or similar investments of the same issuer.

### Foreign Currency

The functional currency of each of our foreign subsidiaries is generally its local currency. We translate the assets and liabilities of foreign subsidiaries into U.S. dollars at exchange rates in effect at the balance sheet date. We translate revenues and expenses at average exchange rates during the period. Translation adjustments are recorded as a component of accumulated other comprehensive income (loss) in stockholders' equity.

Gains and losses arising from transactions denominated in currencies other than an entity's functional currency are recognized in earnings as incurred. These include remeasurement of monetary assets and liabilities and gains and losses associated with settlement of client transactions in foreign currencies. Foreign currency gains and losses associated with our dynamic currency conversion, multi-currency pricing, and cross-border payment services are recognized as part of the revenue or cost of those services, as described in Note 3.

### Cash and Cash Equivalents

Cash and cash equivalents consist of cash on deposit with financial institutions and highly liquid investments with original maturities of three months or less at the date of purchase. These investments include money market funds, time deposits, and similar instruments. Cash equivalents are stated at cost, which approximates fair value.

A portion of our cash and cash equivalents is held by subsidiaries that hold money transmitter licenses or similar authorizations. Those subsidiaries are subject to net worth, capital, and permissible investment requirements that may limit our ability to transfer cash among subsidiaries. A portion of our cash and cash equivalents is also held by subsidiaries outside the United States. These amounts are presented within cash and cash equivalents because they are available for the operating needs of the subsidiaries that hold them.

For purposes of the consolidated statement of cash flows, cash and cash equivalents include any amounts restricted as to withdrawal or usage that are presented within the cash and cash equivalents caption on the consolidated balance sheet. Funds held on behalf of clients that are subject to contractual or regulatory restrictions and that are not presented within cash and cash equivalents are classified within other assets, as described in Note 4. Changes in those balances are reflected in the consolidated statement of cash flows according to the nature of the underlying activity.

### Settlement Assets and Settlement Obligations

In the ordinary course of business, we facilitate the movement of funds between merchants and payment networks, issuers and cardholders, billers and payers, corporations and recipients, and financial institutions and payment systems. Settlement assets represent amounts receivable from payment networks, sponsor banks, settlement banks, issuers, and other counterparties for transactions we have processed. Settlement obligations represent amounts payable to merchants, clients, and other counterparties for those transactions.

Settlement assets and obligations generally turn over within a short period, often within one to several business days, and can fluctuate significantly from day to day. The balances at any period end are affected by whether the period end falls on a weekend or holiday, by the volume of transactions processed in the final days of the period, and by seasonal patterns. Settlement-related balances are reflected within accounts receivable, other assets, accrued liabilities, and other liabilities, depending on their nature and counterparty. See Note 4.

### Accounts Receivable and Allowance for Credit Losses

Accounts receivable consist primarily of amounts due from clients for processing, subscription, value-added, hardware, professional services, and card production fees. Receivables may also include certain settlement-related amounts due from clients. Accounts receivable are recorded at the invoiced or contractually due amount, net of an allowance for expected credit losses.

We estimate expected credit losses on receivables and other financial assets measured at amortized cost using a current expected credit loss model. The allowance reflects:

- historical loss experience for pools of receivables with similar risk characteristics;
- current conditions, including the aging of balances and the financial condition of specific clients; and
- reasonable and supportable forecasts of economic conditions that may affect collectability.

Receivables are written off against the allowance when we determine they are uncollectible. Recoveries of amounts previously written off are credited to the allowance. See Note 5.

### Merchant Reserves and Chargeback Losses

In our merchant acquiring business, we may be financially liable for chargebacks and other amounts owed by merchants if a merchant is unable or unwilling to satisfy its obligations. Such circumstances may arise, for example, when a merchant ceases operations after accepting payment for goods or services that it does not deliver.

To mitigate this exposure, we require certain merchants to maintain reserves, which may take the form of cash held by us, delayed funding of settlement amounts, letters of credit, or other collateral. Cash reserves held on behalf of merchants are recorded as liabilities within accrued liabilities or other liabilities, as applicable.

We record a liability for estimated losses arising from merchant chargebacks and similar exposures. The estimate considers historical loss experience, the volume and characteristics of transactions processed, merchant risk profiles, the amount of reserves and collateral held, and current and expected economic conditions. Losses, net of recoveries, are recognized in operating expenses. See Note 19.

### Inventories

Inventories consist primarily of payment terminals, point-of-sale stations, peripherals, card bodies, chips, and personalization materials. Inventories are stated at the lower of cost or net realizable value, with cost determined using a first-in, first-out or weighted-average method. We record provisions for excess, obsolete, or slow-moving inventory based on forecasted demand, product life cycles, network certification requirements, and technological change. Inventories are included in other assets.

### Property, Plant, and Equipment

Property, plant, and equipment are recorded at cost less accumulated depreciation. Depreciation is computed on a straight-line basis over the estimated useful lives of the assets. Useful lives are determined by asset class:

- Computer equipment and data center hardware are depreciated over relatively short lives that reflect the pace of technological change.
- Card production equipment, network infrastructure, and furniture and fixtures are depreciated over intermediate lives.
- Buildings are depreciated over longer lives.
- Leasehold improvements are depreciated over the shorter of their estimated useful lives or the remaining lease term.
- Payment devices that we lease to merchants are depreciated over their expected service lives.

Land is not depreciated. Expenditures for maintenance and repairs are expensed as incurred, while expenditures that extend the useful lives of assets are capitalized. When assets are retired or otherwise disposed of, their cost and related accumulated depreciation are removed from the accounts. Any resulting gain or loss is recognized in operating expenses.

### Capitalized Software

We capitalize certain costs incurred to develop or obtain internal-use software, including software used to deliver our processing and software services to clients. Capitalization begins when the preliminary project stage is complete, management commits to funding the project, and it is probable that the project will be completed and the software will be used as intended. Capitalization ceases when the software is substantially complete and ready for its intended use.

Capitalized costs include:

- external direct costs of materials and services;
- payroll and payroll-related costs of employees directly associated with the development activities; and
- interest costs incurred during development, where applicable.

Costs incurred during the preliminary project stage and the post-implementation and operation stage, including training and maintenance costs, are expensed as incurred. Capitalized software is amortized on a straight-line basis over its estimated useful life, beginning when the software is ready for its intended use.

Implementation costs incurred in cloud computing arrangements that are service contracts are capitalized under the same criteria. They are amortized over the term of the hosting arrangement, including reasonably certain renewals, and presented in other assets. The related amortization is presented in the same line item as the fees for the hosting arrangement.

We evaluate capitalized software for impairment when events or circumstances indicate that its carrying amount may not be recoverable. These circumstances include decisions to consolidate, replace, or retire platforms as part of our modernization program. We also reassess remaining useful lives when our technology plans change. See Note 6.

Costs of software developed for sale or license to clients are capitalized after technological feasibility has been established. Such costs have not been significant because of the short period between the establishment of technological feasibility and general release.

### Business Combinations

We account for business combinations using the acquisition method. The purchase price, including the fair value of any contingent consideration, is allocated to the identifiable assets acquired, liabilities assumed, and noncontrolling interests, if any, based on their estimated fair values at the acquisition date. The excess of the purchase price over the fair value of the net identifiable assets acquired is recorded as goodwill.

Estimating the fair values of acquired intangible assets requires significant judgment. Such assets include client relationships, acquired technology, and trade names. The key assumptions are projected revenues, client attrition rates, technology obsolescence, royalty rates, and discount rates.

During the measurement period, which may be up to one year from the acquisition date, we may record adjustments to the assets acquired and liabilities assumed, with corresponding adjustments to goodwill. Subsequent changes in the fair value of contingent consideration classified as a liability are recognized in earnings. Acquisition-related costs are expensed as incurred and are reported in Corporate and Other for segment purposes.

The results of operations of acquired businesses are included in our consolidated financial statements from their respective acquisition dates. Cash paid for acquisitions, net of cash acquired, is presented within other investing activities in the consolidated statement of cash flows.

### Goodwill

Goodwill represents the excess of the purchase price of acquired businesses over the fair value of the net identifiable assets acquired. Goodwill is not amortized. It is assigned to the reporting units expected to benefit from the related business combination. Our reporting units are our operating segments or components one level below our operating segments for which discrete financial information is available and regularly reviewed by segment management.

We test goodwill for impairment annually as of the first day of our fourth fiscal quarter, and more frequently if events or circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. We may first assess qualitative factors to determine whether it is more likely than not that the fair value of a reporting unit is less than its carrying value. If we bypass the qualitative assessment, or if we conclude from it that a quantitative test is necessary, we compare the estimated fair value of the reporting unit to its carrying value, including goodwill. If the carrying value exceeds fair value, we recognize an impairment charge equal to the excess, limited to the amount of goodwill allocated to the reporting unit.

We estimate reporting unit fair values using a combination of an income approach and a market approach:

- The income approach is based on the present value of projected future cash flows. It requires assumptions about revenue growth rates, operating margins, capital expenditures, terminal growth rates, and discount rates.
- The market approach is based on valuation multiples of comparable publicly traded companies. It requires judgment in selecting comparable companies and appropriate multiples.

See Note 7.

### Intangible Assets

Intangible assets with finite lives consist primarily of client relationships, acquired technology, trade names, and contract-based intangibles. Client relationship intangibles are amortized over their estimated useful lives using a method that reflects the pattern in which their economic benefits are consumed. That method may be an accelerated method or the straight-line method, depending on expected client attrition patterns. Other finite-lived intangible assets are generally amortized on a straight-line basis.

We review finite-lived intangible assets and other long-lived assets for recoverability whenever events or circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. Recoverability is assessed by comparing the carrying amount of the asset group to the undiscounted cash flows expected to result from its use and eventual disposition. If the carrying amount is not recoverable, we recognize an impairment loss equal to the excess of the carrying amount over fair value.

Intangible assets with indefinite lives, if any, are not amortized. They are tested for impairment at least annually and whenever events or circumstances indicate that they may be impaired.

### Leases

We determine whether an arrangement is or contains a lease at inception. We lease office facilities, data centers, operations centers, client support centers, card production facilities, and equipment.

For leases with terms greater than twelve months, we recognize at the commencement date:

- a right-of-use ("ROU") asset, representing our right to use the underlying asset for the lease term; and
- a lease liability, representing our obligation to make lease payments, measured at the present value of the remaining lease payments.

Because the rate implicit in most of our leases is not readily determinable, we use our incremental borrowing rate, determined on a collateralized basis for a similar term and in a similar economic environment, to discount lease payments. Lease terms include options to extend or terminate the lease when it is reasonably certain that we will exercise them. We have elected to account for lease and non-lease components together for our real estate leases. We do not recognize ROU assets or lease liabilities for leases with terms of twelve months or less.

Operating lease cost is recognized on a straight-line basis over the lease term. For finance leases, we recognize amortization of the ROU asset on a straight-line basis and interest expense on the lease liability using the effective interest method. Operating lease ROU assets are presented within other assets, and operating lease liabilities are presented within accrued liabilities and other liabilities.

As a lessor, we lease payment terminals and point-of-sale devices to certain merchants. These arrangements are generally classified as operating leases or, in certain circumstances, as sales-type leases. Lease income from operating leases is recognized over the lease term and included in revenues. See Note 11.

### Capitalized Contract Costs and Client Incentives

**Costs to obtain a contract.** We capitalize the incremental costs of obtaining a contract with a client, primarily sales commissions, if we expect to recover them. Capitalized costs to obtain a contract are amortized on a systematic basis consistent with the transfer of the related services, generally over the expected period of benefit. That period may extend beyond the initial contract term when we expect renewals. As a practical expedient, we expense the incremental costs of obtaining a contract as incurred if the amortization period would be one year or less.

**Costs to fulfill a contract.** We capitalize costs incurred to fulfill a contract that are not within the scope of other guidance if they meet three criteria: they relate directly to a contract or anticipated contract, they generate or enhance resources that will be used to satisfy future performance obligations, and they are expected to be recovered. These costs consist primarily of client conversion and implementation costs. They are amortized over the expected period of benefit.

**Client incentives.** We may make payments to clients, or provide other incentives, in connection with entering into or renewing contracts. Examples include signing bonuses, conversion assistance, and payments that are not in exchange for distinct goods or services. Such incentives are capitalized and amortized as a reduction of revenue over the expected period of benefit.

Capitalized contract costs and client incentives are included in other assets. We evaluate them for impairment when events or circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable, including when a client relationship is terminated or substantially modified.

### Revenue Recognition

We recognize revenue when, or as, control of promised services or goods is transferred to clients, in an amount that reflects the consideration we expect to be entitled to receive in exchange. We apply the following five-step framework:

1. identify the contract with a client;
2. identify the performance obligations in the contract;
3. determine the transaction price;
4. allocate the transaction price to the performance obligations; and
5. recognize revenue when, or as, we satisfy each performance obligation.

Our accounting policies for revenue recognition, including principal-versus-agent considerations, are described in detail in Note 3.

### Operating Expenses

Operating expenses consist of the costs of delivering our services and operating our business. They include:

- personnel costs;
- technology and infrastructure costs;
- network fees and other pass-through costs in arrangements in which we act as principal;
- residuals, commissions, and revenue-sharing payments to distribution partners;
- the cost of hardware and card materials;
- transaction losses, net of recoveries;
- depreciation and amortization;
- selling, marketing, and general and administrative costs; and
- costs associated with acquisitions, integration, restructuring, and similar activities.

Advertising costs are expensed as incurred. Research and development costs that do not qualify for capitalization are expensed as incurred.

### Residuals and Partner Payments

We pay residuals, commissions, and revenue shares to independent sales organizations, agents, financial institution partners, and software partners that refer or distribute our services. These payments are generally calculated as a percentage of the revenue or margin generated by the merchants or clients referred by each partner. Residuals and similar partner payments are recognized as operating expenses in the period in which the related revenue is recognized.

Where a partner is determined to be our client, payments to that partner are evaluated as consideration payable to a client and recognized as a reduction of revenue. This may be the case, for example, when the partner contracts for our services and resells them to its own customers.

### Share-Based Compensation

We grant share-based awards to employees and non-employee directors. These may include restricted stock units, performance-based restricted stock units, stock options, and rights to purchase shares under our employee stock purchase plan. We measure share-based compensation cost at the grant-date fair value of the award.

The fair value of restricted stock units is based on the closing market price of our common stock on the grant date, adjusted where applicable for dividends not received during the vesting period. The fair value of awards with market conditions is estimated using a Monte Carlo simulation. The fair value of stock options and employee stock purchase plan rights is estimated using an option-pricing model.

Compensation cost is recognized on a straight-line basis over the requisite service period, which is generally the vesting period. For performance-based awards, cost is recognized when it is probable that the performance condition will be achieved, and is adjusted to reflect changes in the expected outcome. We account for forfeitures as they occur. See Note 15.

### Income Taxes

We account for income taxes using the asset and liability method. Deferred tax assets and liabilities are recognized for the future tax consequences of temporary differences between the financial statement carrying amounts of assets and liabilities and their respective tax bases, and for operating loss and tax credit carryforwards. Deferred tax assets and liabilities are measured using enacted tax rates expected to apply in the years in which the temporary differences are expected to reverse. The effect of a change in tax rates on deferred taxes is recognized in income in the period that includes the enactment date.

We record a valuation allowance to reduce deferred tax assets to the amount that is more likely than not to be realized. In assessing realizability, we consider all available positive and negative evidence, including projected future taxable income, the reversal of existing taxable temporary differences, tax planning strategies, and recent operating results.

We recognize the tax benefit of an uncertain tax position only if it is more likely than not that the position will be sustained upon examination by the taxing authorities, based on its technical merits. The benefit recognized is measured as the largest amount that has a greater than 50 percent likelihood of being realized upon ultimate settlement. We recognize interest and penalties related to unrecognized tax benefits within income tax expense.

We have elected to account for the tax on global intangible low-taxed income as a period cost when incurred. See Note 12.

### Derivative Financial Instruments

We may use derivative financial instruments, including interest rate swaps, foreign currency forward contracts, and cross-currency swaps, to manage our exposure to changes in interest rates and foreign currency exchange rates. We do not use derivative instruments for speculative or trading purposes.

All derivatives are recognized on the consolidated balance sheet at fair value, within other assets or other liabilities, as applicable. The accounting for changes in fair value depends on whether the derivative has been designated and qualifies as a hedge, and on the type of hedging relationship:

- For **fair value hedges**, changes in the fair value of the derivative and of the hedged item attributable to the hedged risk are recognized in earnings in the same line item.
- For **cash flow hedges**, changes in the fair value of the derivative are recorded in accumulated other comprehensive income (loss). They are reclassified into earnings in the same period, and in the same line item, in which the hedged transaction affects earnings.
- For **net investment hedges**, changes in the fair value of the hedging instrument are recorded in the foreign currency translation component of accumulated other comprehensive income (loss). Amounts excluded from the assessment of effectiveness are recognized in earnings on a systematic basis.
- For derivatives **not designated as hedges**, changes in fair value are recognized in earnings.

See Note 17.

### Fair Value Measurements

Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date. We use a three-level hierarchy that prioritizes the inputs used in measuring fair value:

- **Level 1:** Quoted prices in active markets for identical assets or liabilities.
- **Level 2:** Observable inputs other than Level 1 prices. These include quoted prices for similar assets or liabilities, quoted prices in markets that are not active, and other inputs that are observable or can be corroborated by observable market data.
- **Level 3:** Unobservable inputs that are supported by little or no market activity and that are significant to the fair value of the assets or liabilities.

See Note 18.

### Loss Contingencies

We record a liability for loss contingencies when it is probable that a liability has been incurred and the amount of the loss can be reasonably estimated. If a loss is reasonably possible but not probable, or is probable but not reasonably estimable, we disclose the nature of the contingency and, where feasible, an estimate of the possible loss or range of loss. Legal fees are expensed as incurred. See Note 19.

### Guarantees and Indemnifications

We recognize a liability at the inception of a guarantee for the fair value of the obligation undertaken, if the guarantee is within the scope of the applicable guidance. In the ordinary course of business, we provide indemnifications to clients, partners, sponsor banks, and other counterparties. These cover matters such as intellectual property infringement, breaches of representations and warranties, and losses arising from our services. We evaluate these arrangements and record liabilities when losses are probable and reasonably estimable. See Note 19.

### Treasury Stock and Share Repurchases

Repurchases of our common stock are recorded at cost, including direct costs of acquisition and any applicable excise tax, and are presented within other equity. When treasury shares are reissued, we use a weighted-average cost method, and any difference between the cost of the shares and the proceeds received is recorded in additional paid-in capital or, if additional paid-in capital from treasury stock transactions is exhausted, in retained earnings. Shares repurchased under accelerated share repurchase arrangements are accounted for as a treasury stock transaction and a forward contract indexed to our common stock. The forward contract is classified within equity. See Note 13.

### Comprehensive Income

Comprehensive income consists of net income and other comprehensive income (loss). Other comprehensive income (loss) includes several items:

- foreign currency translation adjustments;
- the effective portion of changes in the fair value of derivatives designated as cash flow hedges and net investment hedges;
- unrealized gains and losses on available-for-sale debt securities, if any; and
- changes in the funded status of defined benefit pension and postretirement plans, as applicable.

See Note 14.

### Segment Reporting

Our chief operating decision maker ("CODM") is our Chief Executive Officer. The CODM evaluates segment performance and allocates resources primarily on the basis of segment revenue and segment operating income. Segment operating income excludes items reported in Corporate and Other. See Note 20.

## Note 3 — Revenue

### Overview

Revenues for fiscal 2023 were $34,182 million ($34,182,102,995). We generate revenue primarily from fees charged for processing transactions and for providing related services. Our revenue streams are:

- **Transaction-based fees**, which are calculated as a percentage of the value of the transactions we process, as a fixed fee per transaction, or as a combination of both;
- **Account-based and subscription fees**, which are charged per active card account, per merchant location, per device, or per software license;
- **Value-added service fees**, which cover fraud and risk management, tokenization, analytics, loyalty programs, chargeback management, currency conversion, and data services;
- **Hardware and equipment revenue**, which arises from the sale or lease of point-of-sale terminals, payment devices, and related peripherals;
- **Professional services fees**, which cover implementation, conversion, consulting, customization, and managed services; and
- **Card production and fulfillment fees**, which cover physical card manufacturing, personalization, and distribution.

### Identification of Contracts

Our contracts with clients range from standard terms accepted by small and micro-merchants through digital self-service channels to negotiated multi-year agreements with large financial institutions, enterprise merchants, and corporations.

We account for a contract when all of the following conditions are met:

- it has approval and commitment from both parties;
- the rights of the parties are identified;
- payment terms are identified;
- the contract has commercial substance; and
- collectability of consideration is probable.

Many of our merchant agreements may be terminated by either party on short notice without substantive penalty. For such agreements, the enforceable contract term is generally limited to the period for which services have been provided and the notice period. Many of our agreements with financial institutions, enterprise merchants, and corporate clients have stated terms of multiple years. They may be terminated for convenience upon payment of a termination fee, upon a change of control, or for failure to meet service levels.

Contract modifications, such as renewals, amendments to pricing, or the addition of services, are evaluated to determine whether they should be accounted for as a separate contract, as the termination of the existing contract and creation of a new contract, or as part of the existing contract.

### Identification of Performance Obligations

We evaluate each contract to identify the promised services and goods and determine which are distinct performance obligations. A promised service or good is distinct if the client can benefit from it on its own or together with other readily available resources, and if it is separately identifiable from other promises in the contract.

**Processing services.** Our promise to stand ready to provide continuous access to our processing platforms and to process transactions as and when requested by the client is generally a single performance obligation. This applies across merchant acquiring, gateway services, issuer processing, debit and network services, payment hub and orchestration, bill payment, and disbursement services. The nature of the promise is to provide an integrated service, which includes authorization, routing, clearing, settlement, and related support. The quantity of transactions is not known at contract inception and is determined by the client's activity.

We have concluded that these processing services constitute a series of distinct service periods, typically daily, that are substantially the same and have the same pattern of transfer to the client. Accordingly, they are accounted for as a single performance obligation satisfied over time.

**Software subscriptions.** Subscriptions to cloud-based software, such as Stonegate Commerce Point and our banking technology platforms, are generally accounted for as stand-ready performance obligations satisfied over time. The client does not have the contractual right to take possession of the software. In certain cases, we license software on premises. A license that provides a right to use our intellectual property as it exists at the point in time the license is granted is recognized at that point in time, and the related post-contract support is recognized over time.

**Value-added services.** Value-added services are evaluated to determine whether they are distinct from the related processing services. Examples include fraud screening, tokenization, chargeback management, analytics, loyalty programs, and currency conversion. Where distinct, they are generally separate stand-ready or usage-based performance obligations satisfied over time as the services are performed.

**Hardware.** Payment terminals, point-of-sale stations, and peripherals that are sold to clients are generally distinct performance obligations satisfied at a point in time, typically upon shipment or delivery. Devices leased to clients are accounted for under the lease accounting guidance, as described in Note 11.

**Professional services.** Implementation, conversion, and set-up activities are evaluated to determine whether they transfer a distinct good or service to the client. Activities that do not transfer a distinct service are not separate performance obligations. Fees for such activities are included in the transaction price and recognized over the period of the related processing or subscription service, which may include expected renewal periods. Distinct professional services are recognized over time as performed, generally using an input method based on labor hours or costs incurred.

**Card production and fulfillment.** The manufacture, personalization, and distribution of physical payment cards are generally distinct performance obligations. They are satisfied at a point in time when the cards are shipped to the client or the cardholder. In certain arrangements in which cards are produced to client specifications and we have an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date, revenue is recognized over time as the cards are produced.

### Determination of the Transaction Price

The transaction price is the amount of consideration to which we expect to be entitled in exchange for transferring promised services or goods to a client. It excludes amounts collected on behalf of third parties, such as sales taxes. Much of the consideration in our contracts is variable. Examples include:

- transaction-based fees that depend on the number or value of transactions;
- account-based fees that depend on the number of active accounts;
- tiered or volume-based pricing;
- volume discounts and rebates;
- client incentives;
- service level credits and penalties; and
- contract termination fees.

For processing services accounted for as a series of distinct service periods, we allocate variable consideration to the distinct service period in which the related activity occurs when two conditions are met: the variable payment relates specifically to our efforts in that period, and allocation to that period is consistent with the allocation objective. As a result, transaction-based and account-based fees for processing services are generally recognized in the period in which the transactions are processed or the accounts are maintained. We do not need to estimate total variable consideration at contract inception.

For variable consideration that does not meet these criteria, we estimate the amount using the expected value or most likely amount method, whichever better predicts the consideration to which we will be entitled. Examples include volume-based rebates measured over periods longer than a single service period and certain service level credits. We include estimates of variable consideration in the transaction price only to the extent that it is probable that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue recognized will not occur when the uncertainty is resolved.

Contract termination fees are recognized when the contract is terminated, the fee is fixed or determinable, and collectability is probable. When a termination fee is received in connection with a contract modification under which we continue to provide services, the fee is included in the transaction price of the modified contract.

Our contracts generally do not include a significant financing component. Payment terms are typically short. For transaction-based fees, consideration is often collected through net settlement or shortly after the transaction is processed. As a practical expedient, we do not adjust the transaction price for the effects of a significant financing component when the period between the transfer of services and payment is one year or less.

### Allocation of the Transaction Price

For contracts with multiple performance obligations, we allocate the transaction price to each performance obligation based on its relative standalone selling price. Where standalone selling prices are not directly observable, we estimate them using all reasonably available information, including market conditions, client-specific factors, internal pricing guidelines, and expected costs. We use the adjusted market assessment approach, the expected cost plus a margin approach, or, in limited circumstances, the residual approach.

Variable consideration is allocated entirely to a specific performance obligation, or to a distinct service period within a series, when the variable terms relate specifically to our efforts to satisfy that performance obligation or transfer that distinct service. Variable consideration is allocated in this way only when such allocation is consistent with the overall allocation objective.

### Principal-Versus-Agent Considerations

Many of our arrangements involve fees paid to third parties, including interchange fees remitted to card issuers, assessments and other fees remitted to payment networks, and fees paid to sponsor banks, payment systems, and other service providers. When another party is involved in providing services to a client, we evaluate whether our promise is to provide the specified service ourselves (principal) or to arrange for the other party to provide it (agent). This evaluation depends on whether we control the specified service before it is transferred to the client.

Indicators that we consider include:

- whether we are primarily responsible for fulfilling the promise to provide the specified service, including responsibility for the acceptability of the service;
- whether we have inventory risk, which is generally not relevant to processing services; and
- whether we have discretion in establishing the price for the specified service.

**Merchant Solutions.** In merchant acquiring arrangements, our performance obligation is to provide merchants with access to payment acceptance and processing services. Interchange fees and network assessments are paid to card issuers and payment networks. We generally do not control the authorization and funding of transactions by issuers or the network services provided by payment networks. In arrangements in which these fees are passed through to the merchant at cost and we lack discretion over their amount, we act as agent with respect to them, and revenue is presented net of interchange and network assessments.

In certain arrangements, particularly bundled or flat-rate pricing arrangements offered to small and mid-sized merchants and certain arrangements in our Stonegate Connect program, we have concluded that we are primarily responsible for providing an integrated payment acceptance service. In those arrangements, we have discretion in establishing the price charged to the merchant and bear the risk of changes in interchange and network costs. There, we act as principal, and revenue is presented gross of interchange and network fees, with the corresponding costs recognized in operating expenses.

**Issuer and Network Services.** In issuer processing arrangements, we generally act as principal with respect to the processing services we provide to issuers. Network fees paid on behalf of issuer clients are evaluated to determine whether we control the related services. Fees that we pass through to issuers at cost, and for which we act as agent, are presented net. Costs of postage, card materials, and similar items incurred in connection with card production and statement services are generally presented gross, because we control the goods and services before they are transferred to the client.

**Enterprise and Banking Payments.** In payment hub, bill payment, disbursement, and cross-border arrangements, we evaluate fees paid to payment systems, correspondent banks, and local payment partners to determine whether we control the related services. Revenue from cross-border payments and foreign exchange services is generally presented as the net spread earned on the conversion, together with any separately charged fees. This is because our performance obligation is to facilitate the transfer and conversion of funds rather than to provide the underlying currency.

Principal-versus-agent conclusions are made at the contract or product level and can have a significant effect on the amounts of revenue and operating expenses presented. These conclusions generally do not affect operating income.

### Timing of Revenue Recognition

The following summarizes the general timing of revenue recognition for our principal revenue streams:

| Revenue Stream | Primary Segments | General Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Transaction-based fees | Merchant Solutions; Issuer and Network Services; Enterprise and Banking Payments | Over time, as transactions are processed in each distinct service period |
| Account-based and subscription fees | Issuer and Network Services; Merchant Solutions; Enterprise and Banking Payments | Over time, ratably or as accounts are maintained in each period |
| Value-added service fees | All segments | Over time, as services are performed |
| Hardware sales | Merchant Solutions | Point in time, upon shipment or delivery |
| Hardware leases | Merchant Solutions | Over the lease term (operating leases) or at commencement (sales-type leases) |
| Professional services | All segments | Over time as performed (distinct services) or over the related service period (non-distinct services) |
| Card production and fulfillment | Issuer and Network Services | Point in time upon shipment, or over time where criteria are met |

The substantial majority of our revenues are recognized over time.

### Disaggregation of Revenue

We disaggregate revenue by reportable segment and by the nature of the revenue stream, as described above. These categories reflect how the nature, amount, timing, and uncertainty of our revenues and cash flows are affected by economic factors. Transaction-based fees are most sensitive to changes in consumer and business spending, transaction mix, and pricing. Account-based and subscription fees tend to be more recurring and less sensitive to short-term changes in transaction values. Hardware, professional services, and card production revenues vary from period to period based on device deployment cycles, implementation timing, and card reissuance cycles.

Total revenues of $34,182 million for fiscal 2023 comprise the revenues of our three reportable segments and the revenues reported in Corporate and Other, net of intersegment eliminations. Revenue by reportable segment is presented in Note 20.

No single client accounted for a material portion of our consolidated revenues for fiscal 2023.

### Contract Balances

The timing of revenue recognition, billings, and cash collections results in accounts receivable, contract assets, and contract liabilities.

**Accounts receivable.** We record a receivable when we have an unconditional right to consideration, meaning that only the passage of time is required before payment is due. Accounts receivable, net, were $4,979 million ($4,978,731,881) at December 31, 2023. See Note 5.

**Contract assets.** Contract assets represent our right to consideration in exchange for services or goods we have transferred to a client when that right is conditioned on something other than the passage of time. They arise, for example, when revenue is recognized for professional services or card production before the contractual billing milestone is reached. Contract assets are included in other assets.

**Contract liabilities.** Contract liabilities, also referred to as deferred revenue, consist of consideration received or due from clients before we transfer the related services or goods. Examples include upfront implementation and conversion fees for non-distinct activities, which are recognized over the related service period, and subscription fees billed in advance. Contract liabilities are included in accrued liabilities and other liabilities, depending on the expected timing of revenue recognition.

Changes in contract asset and contract liability balances during fiscal 2023 resulted from the timing of billings relative to the satisfaction of performance obligations, revenue recognized from beginning-of-period contract liabilities, and additions from new contracts. Contract assets and contract liabilities are presented on a net basis for each contract.

### Remaining Performance Obligations

Remaining performance obligations represent the transaction price allocated to performance obligations that are unsatisfied or partially unsatisfied at the end of the reporting period.

We apply the optional exemptions that permit us to omit disclosure of remaining performance obligations in two circumstances. The first is for contracts with an original expected duration of one year or less. The second is for variable consideration allocated entirely to a wholly unsatisfied distinct service period within a series of distinct service periods. The substantial majority of our revenues is variable consideration within the scope of this second exemption, including transaction-based and account-based processing fees.

Our remaining performance obligations therefore consist principally of the fixed or minimum consideration in our multi-year agreements. These include minimum monthly fees, fixed subscription fees, and contracted professional services. They are expected to be recognized over the remaining terms of the related agreements.

### Capitalized Contract Costs and Client Incentives

As described in Note 2, we capitalize certain incremental costs of obtaining contracts, certain costs to fulfill contracts, and certain upfront incentives paid to clients. These are presented in other assets.

- Capitalized costs to obtain and fulfill contracts are amortized as operating expenses over the expected period of benefit.
- Capitalized client incentives are amortized as a reduction of revenue over the expected period of benefit.

We did not recognize material impairments of capitalized contract costs or client incentives during fiscal 2023.

### Sales Taxes and Similar Amounts

We exclude from the transaction price sales, use, value-added, and other taxes that we collect from clients and remit to governmental authorities.

### Interest Income on Client Funds and Settlement Balances

In certain businesses, we earn interest or similar income on settlement balances and client funds held pending settlement or disbursement, to the extent permitted by applicable law and contractual terms. This income is generally presented within revenues, because earning a return on these balances is part of the ordinary activities of the related businesses and is considered in the pricing of our services.

## Note 4 — Settlement Assets, Settlement Obligations, and Client Funds

### Nature of Settlement Activities

Our role in the settlement of transactions varies by business.

**Merchant Solutions.** In our merchant acquiring business, we receive funds from payment networks for card transactions processed on behalf of merchants. We then fund settlement to merchants, net of interchange, network fees, and our fees, as applicable. In some cases, we fund merchants before receiving funds from the networks. In others, we delay funding to manage risk. Payment network rules generally require that merchant acquiring be conducted by or under the sponsorship of a member financial institution. Where we are not a direct member, settlement funds flow through our sponsor banks.

**Issuer and Network Services.** In our issuer processing and debit network businesses, we facilitate settlement between issuers, networks, and acquirers. In most issuer processing arrangements, the issuer is responsible for funding settlement, and we do not hold settlement funds. In certain debit network and ATM processing arrangements, we act as settlement agent and may hold funds temporarily pending settlement.

**Enterprise and Banking Payments.** In our bill payment, disbursement, and cross-border businesses, we may receive funds from payers, billers, corporations, or other originators and hold those funds pending remittance or disbursement to recipients. Certain of these activities are conducted by subsidiaries that hold money transmitter licenses or similar authorizations. Those subsidiaries are subject to requirements relating to net worth, permissible investments, and safeguarding of client funds.

### Balance Sheet Presentation

Settlement-related balances are reflected in several captions on our consolidated balance sheet:

| Balance Sheet Caption | Settlement-Related Components |

| Accounts receivable, net | Certain amounts receivable from clients in connection with settlement, including amounts advanced to merchants subject to recovery |

### Offsetting

We present settlement assets and settlement obligations on a gross basis unless a legally enforceable right of offset exists, we intend to settle on a net basis, and the other offsetting criteria are met. Certain amounts receivable from and payable to the same payment network or counterparty are presented net when those criteria are satisfied.

### Client Funds and Permissible Investments

Client funds held by our licensed subsidiaries are invested in permissible investments as defined by applicable law. These may include cash, deposits at financial institutions, money market funds, and high-quality debt securities. Client funds are held in accounts that are segregated from our operating funds as required by applicable law or contract. Client funds and the related obligations are presented within other assets and accrued liabilities or other liabilities, respectively.

Investments of client funds in debt securities, if any, are generally classified as available-for-sale and carried at fair value. Unrealized gains and losses are recorded in accumulated other comprehensive income (loss) and are not available for general corporate purposes.

### Credit and Liquidity Risk

Our settlement activities expose us to credit risk if counterparties fail to meet their settlement obligations. They also expose us to liquidity risk, because we may be required to fund settlement before receiving funds from counterparties.

We manage these risks in several ways:

- monitoring counterparty credit quality;
- maintaining settlement lines and arrangements with sponsor and settlement banks;
- requiring merchant reserves and collateral;
- delaying funding where appropriate; and
- maintaining cash and cash equivalents and access to other liquidity resources.

Real-time payments reduce the time available between initiation and settlement and may increase our liquidity requirements as those rails grow.

### Cash Flow Classification

Changes in settlement assets and settlement obligations that arise from our operating activities are generally reflected in cash flows from operating activities, within changes in working capital and other. Purchases, sales, and maturities of investments of client funds are reflected in cash flows from investing activities, within other investing activities. Changes in client fund obligations and certain settlement-related borrowings are reflected in cash flows from financing activities, within other financing activities. The classification of a particular balance depends on the nature of the underlying activity and the contractual terms.

## Note 5 — Accounts Receivable and Allowance for Credit Losses

### Composition

Accounts receivable, net, were $4,979 million ($4,978,731,881) at December 31, 2023. This represented approximately 9.0% of total assets and approximately 53 days of fiscal 2023 revenues, based on the year-end balance.

Accounts receivable consist primarily of:

- amounts due from merchants, financial institutions, corporations, billers, and other clients for processing, subscription, value-added, and professional services fees;
- amounts due from clients for hardware sales and card production and fulfillment services;
- amounts due under device lease arrangements, to the extent not classified elsewhere; and
- certain settlement-related amounts due from clients, including amounts advanced to merchants that are subject to recovery.

Fees in our merchant acquiring business are frequently collected through net settlement, in which our fees are deducted from the funds remitted to merchants. Such fees therefore give rise to limited receivable balances. Fees from financial institutions, enterprise clients, and corporations are generally invoiced monthly and are typically due within short periods.

### Allowance for Credit Losses

We maintain an allowance for expected credit losses on accounts receivable. We estimate the allowance by pooling receivables with similar risk characteristics, such as client type, segment, geography, and aging. To each pool we apply loss rates derived from historical experience, adjusted for current conditions and reasonable and supportable forecasts. We also establish specific allowances for individual receivables when we become aware of a client's inability to meet its financial obligations. Examples include bankruptcy filings, significant deterioration in financial condition, and disputes.

Our receivables are spread across a large and diverse client base, which reduces the concentration of credit risk. We monitor the creditworthiness of significant clients, particularly financial institution and enterprise clients whose financial difficulties could result in nonpayment. Stress in the banking sector or in particular industries could increase expected credit losses.

Provisions for expected credit losses are recognized in operating expenses. The allowance for credit losses, provisions, and write-offs during fiscal 2023 were not material to our consolidated financial statements.

### Other Financial Assets at Amortized Cost

We also evaluate expected credit losses on other financial assets measured at amortized cost. These include contract assets, net investments in sales-type leases, and certain advances to partners. Expected credit losses on these assets were not material at December 31, 2023.

## Note 11 — Leases

### Lessee Arrangements

We lease office facilities, data centers, operations centers, client support centers, card production facilities, and equipment. Our principal executive offices are located in New York, New York. Our leases have remaining terms of varying lengths, some of which include one or more options to extend or terminate the lease. Options are included in the lease term when it is reasonably certain that we will exercise them.

Most of our leases are classified as operating leases. Operating lease ROU assets are presented within other assets. Operating lease liabilities are presented within accrued liabilities (current portion) and other liabilities (noncurrent portion). Finance lease ROU assets are presented within property, plant, and equipment, net, and finance lease liabilities are presented within short-term debt and long-term debt.

Components of lease cost include:

- operating lease cost, recognized on a straight-line basis over the lease term;
- short-term lease cost for leases with terms of twelve months or less;
- variable lease cost, including payments for common area maintenance, property taxes, insurance, and usage-based charges not included in the measurement of lease liabilities; and
- finance lease cost, consisting of amortization of ROU assets and interest on lease liabilities.

Operating lease cost and variable lease cost are included in operating expenses. Amortization of finance lease ROU assets is included in depreciation and amortization, and interest on finance lease liabilities is included in interest expense. Lease costs were not material to our consolidated results of operations in fiscal 2023.

As part of our operational excellence initiatives, we periodically evaluate our real estate footprint, including in light of flexible work arrangements. Decisions to exit or sublease facilities may result in impairment of the related ROU assets and leasehold improvements. Such charges are reported in Corporate and Other for segment purposes.

Cash paid for operating leases is classified within operating activities. Principal payments on finance lease liabilities are classified within financing activities.

### Lessor Arrangements

We lease payment terminals and point-of-sale devices to certain merchants in our Merchant Solutions segment. These leases generally have short terms and may be renewed or cancelled by the merchant. Leases that do not transfer substantially all of the risks and rewards of ownership are classified as operating leases. For these leases, the devices remain in property, plant, and equipment and are depreciated over their expected service lives, and lease income is recognized in revenues over the lease term. Leases that transfer substantially all of the risks and rewards of ownership are classified as sales-type leases. For those leases, revenue and the net investment in the lease are recognized at commencement.

Lease income was not material to our consolidated revenues in fiscal 2023.

Determining the amount of unrecognized deferred tax liability on indefinitely reinvested earnings is not practicable because of the complexity of the hypothetical calculation.

### Uncertain Tax Positions

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and in numerous foreign jurisdictions. We are routinely examined by the U.S. Internal Revenue Service, state and local taxing authorities, and foreign taxing authorities. Significant judgment is required in evaluating our tax positions and determining our provision for income taxes. We establish liabilities for uncertain tax positions when, despite our belief that our tax return positions are supportable, those positions may not be sustained upon examination. We adjust these liabilities in light of changing facts and circumstances, such as the progress of tax audits, new case law, and the expiration of statutes of limitations.

Liabilities for uncertain tax positions are presented within other liabilities, or as a reduction of deferred tax assets where appropriate. Interest and penalties related to unrecognized tax benefits are recognized within income tax expense.

Because tax examinations may take several years to resolve, amounts ultimately paid could differ materially from amounts recorded. It is reasonably possible that the amount of unrecognized tax benefits will change in the next twelve months as a result of the resolution of examinations or the expiration of statutes of limitations. We do not expect any such change to have a material effect on our financial position.

### Global Minimum Tax

Many jurisdictions have enacted or are in the process of enacting legislation to implement a global minimum tax framework, generally effective for fiscal years beginning on or after January 1, 2024. We are monitoring these developments and evaluating their potential effects on our effective tax rate, cash taxes, and compliance obligations. We will account for any top-up taxes under this framework as period costs.

### Share Repurchases

We repurchased common stock at a cost of $11,238 million ($11,237,531,043) in fiscal 2023. Repurchases were made under share repurchase programs authorized by our Board of Directors. These programs permit repurchases from time to time through open market purchases, privately negotiated transactions, accelerated share repurchase arrangements, or other means, in accordance with applicable securities laws.

The timing and amount of repurchases depend on market conditions, our liquidity and capital requirements, the price of our common stock, and other factors. The programs do not obligate us to acquire any particular number of shares and may be modified, suspended, or terminated at any time. Information regarding repurchases during the fourth quarter of fiscal 2023 and remaining authorization is included in Item 5, "Market for Registrant's Common Equity, Related Stockholder Matters and Issuer Purchases of Equity Securities."

Repurchased shares are held in treasury and are recorded at cost within other equity. The cost includes direct costs of acquisition and the excise tax on net share repurchases enacted under the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, which is accrued and recorded as part of the cost of treasury stock.
