# ITEM 1A. RISK FACTORS

Investing in our securities involves risk. Before making an investment decision, you should carefully consider the risks and uncertainties described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. That information includes "Item 1. Business," "Item 7. Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," "Item 7A. Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk," and our consolidated financial statements and accompanying notes in "Item 8. Financial Statements and Supplementary Data."

Any of the following risks could materially and adversely affect our business, results of operations, financial condition, liquidity, capital position, or financial strength and credit ratings, and the trading price of our common stock and other securities. The risks described below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks and uncertainties that we do not currently know of, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also adversely affect us. Many of the risks below are interrelated and may occur together or amplify one another. For example, a severe catastrophe event could coincide with financial market dislocation, a reinsurer failure, or a rating agency action. The order in which these risks are presented does not indicate their relative likelihood or the potential magnitude of their consequences.

This section contains forward-looking statements. Actual outcomes may differ materially from those anticipated in these statements as a result of the factors described below and elsewhere in this report.

## Summary of Risk Factors

The following is a summary of the principal risks that could adversely affect our business, results of operations, and financial condition. It is qualified in its entirety by the more complete discussion that follows.

**Insurance and underwriting risks**

- Catastrophe events, including hurricanes, severe convective storms, winter storms, wildfires, earthquakes, terrorism, pandemics, and widespread cyber events, could cause losses that are large relative to our earnings and capital.
- Climate change may increase the frequency, severity, and unpredictability of weather-related losses and may affect our underwriting, pricing, investments, and regulatory environment.
- Our reserves for unpaid losses and loss adjustment expenses, future policy benefits, and other policyholder liabilities are estimates and may prove inadequate. This risk is heightened for long-tail casualty lines, legacy asbestos and environmental exposures, and long-duration life and disability products.
- Loss cost inflation, including economic inflation and social inflation, may outpace our ability to obtain adequate rates.
- Actual mortality, morbidity, persistency, and policyholder behavior experience may differ materially from the assumptions we use to price products and establish liabilities in our Life, Retirement and Group Benefits segment.
- Reinsurance may not be available on acceptable terms, may not adequately protect us, and exposes us to the credit risk of reinsurers.

**Competitive and distribution risks**

- The insurance industry is highly competitive and cyclical, and we may be unable to maintain our market position or underwriting margins.
- We depend on independent agents, brokers, employee benefits consultants, financial institutions, and managing general agents and program administrators. Consolidation among intermediaries, or the loss or deterioration of these relationships, could reduce our premium volume or profitability.

**Investment and financial market risks**

- Changes in interest rates, credit spreads, equity markets, and real estate values could reduce our investment income, impair our investments, increase our liabilities, and reduce our capital.
- Our closed block of variable annuities with guaranteed living benefits, and our indexed products, expose us to market and hedging risks.
- Some of our investments are relatively illiquid and are difficult to value.

**Capital, liquidity, and ratings risks**

- As a holding company, we depend on dividends and other payments from our subsidiaries. Insurance regulators limit those payments.
- A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could materially harm our competitive position, liquidity, and cost of capital.
- We may need additional capital in the future, and it may not be available on favorable terms or at all.

**Regulatory and legal risks**

- We are subject to extensive and evolving regulation, including rate and form regulation, capital requirements, market conduct standards, and rules governing the use of data and artificial intelligence. Compliance failures or adverse regulatory changes could materially affect us.
- We are subject to litigation, regulatory investigations, and assessments, including extra-contractual claims, class actions, guaranty fund assessments, and residual market assessments.
- Changes in tax law and accounting standards could adversely affect our products, results, and financial condition.

**Operational and technology risks**

- Cybersecurity incidents, technology failures, or failures of third-party service providers could disrupt our operations, compromise sensitive data, and expose us to liability and reputational harm.
- Our technology modernization initiatives, including cloud migration and the use of advanced analytics and generative artificial intelligence, may not succeed as planned and may introduce new risks.
- Our models, including catastrophe, pricing, reserving, capital, and hedging models, may prove inaccurate.
- We may be unable to attract and retain key personnel and specialized talent.

**General economic and common stock risks**

- Adverse economic, political, and geopolitical conditions could reduce demand for our products, increase claims, and depress the value of our investments.
- Insurance holding company laws, New York corporate law, and provisions of our governing documents could delay, deter, or prevent a change of control.
- The market price of our common stock may be volatile, and our payment of dividends and share repurchases is not guaranteed.

## Risks Related to Our Insurance Operations

***Catastrophe events could result in losses that materially reduce our earnings, capital, and liquidity.***

Our property and casualty insurance subsidiaries are exposed to losses from catastrophe events, both natural and man-made. Natural perils include hurricanes and other named storms, tornadoes, hail and other severe convective storms, winter storms and freezing events, wildfires, floods, and earthquakes. Man-made events include terrorist attacks, civil unrest, explosions, industrial accidents, and large-scale cyber events. Our Life, Retirement and Group Benefits segment is exposed to catastrophic mortality and morbidity events, such as pandemics and mass casualty incidents.

The incidence and severity of catastrophes are inherently unpredictable, and the losses from one or more catastrophes in a given period could be material. Several features of our business increase our exposure:

- **Geographic concentration.** Our historical roots and our headquarters are in the northeastern United States, and we write substantial personal and commercial property business in densely populated coastal and metropolitan areas where insured values are high.
- **National footprint.** Our personal lines, commercial, and excess and surplus property businesses operate nationally. They include exposure in regions prone to hurricanes, severe convective storms, wildfire, and earthquake.
- **Excess and surplus property.** Our Specialty and Excess Insurance segment writes property coverage for risks with significant catastrophe exposure, unusual occupancies, and vacant or distressed properties. Losses on these risks can be severe and difficult to estimate.
- **Multiple lines affected by a single event.** One event may produce losses across homeowners, automobile physical damage, commercial property, business interruption, inland marine, workers' compensation, group life and disability, and other lines at the same time.

Catastrophe losses are driven by the amount of insured exposure in the affected area and by the severity of the event. Insured exposure has grown with property values, construction costs, and population migration into catastrophe-prone areas. Following a major event, the cost of labor and materials often increases sharply because of "demand surge," which raises the cost of settling claims beyond pre-event estimates. Disputes over coverage, assignment-of-benefits practices, fraud, and litigation can also increase catastrophe losses and extend the time needed to resolve them. In addition, regulators and legislatures in affected jurisdictions may require us to extend grace periods for premium payments, suspend cancellations and non-renewals, or provide coverage beyond the terms of our policies. Each of these could increase our losses.

Severe convective storm and other secondary perils have become a more significant component of industry catastrophe losses. These events are frequently below the attachment points of traditional property catastrophe reinsurance, so a high frequency of moderate-sized events can materially affect our results even if no single event is large. Our aggregate catastrophe reinsurance coverage, where purchased, may not respond to such accumulations or may be exhausted.

Our catastrophe risk management depends on underwriting guidelines, policy terms and deductibles, geographic diversification, catastrophe modeling, exposure limits, and reinsurance. These measures may not adequately mitigate our catastrophe exposure. Catastrophe losses in excess of our expectations, or in excess of our reinsurance protection, could materially reduce our earnings and statutory and GAAP capital. They could also impair our liquidity, given the need to pay claims promptly after an event, and lead to rating agency actions. Because catastrophe losses are seasonal and irregular, our results of operations may fluctuate significantly from quarter to quarter and year to year.

***Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of weather-related losses and may adversely affect our business in ways that are difficult to predict.***

Changing climate conditions may affect the frequency, severity, duration, and geographic distribution of weather-related events. These include hurricanes and tropical storms, severe convective storms, wildfires, drought, extreme heat, flooding, and rising sea levels. Historical loss experience and catastrophe models calibrated to historical data may not reflect current or future risk levels. As a result, we may underestimate expected losses, misprice our products, under-purchase reinsurance, or hold insufficient capital for the risks we assume.

Climate change may also affect our business in other ways:

- **Underwriting and pricing.** Regulatory constraints on rates, or affordability concerns, may prevent us from charging rates that reflect evolving risk. This may lead us to reduce writings in certain geographies, which could harm our relationships with distribution partners, regulators, and customers and reduce our premium volume.
- **Availability and affordability.** If insurance becomes less available or affordable in catastrophe-exposed regions, policyholders may move to residual market mechanisms such as FAIR plans and windstorm pools. This may increase the assessments we pay to those mechanisms. Policymakers may also respond with legislative or regulatory measures that restrict our ability to manage exposure.
- **Reinsurance.** Reinsurers may raise prices, increase retentions, or reduce capacity for weather-related perils, increasing our cost of risk transfer or leaving us with greater net retained exposure.
- **Life and health.** Extreme heat, air quality deterioration, and changes in the incidence of vector-borne disease could adversely affect mortality and morbidity in our life and group benefits businesses over the longer term.
- **Investments.** Physical climate risks may impair the value of real estate collateral underlying our commercial mortgage loans and mortgage-backed securities, as well as municipal bonds issued by jurisdictions in vulnerable areas. Transition risks, including policy, technological, and market changes associated with a shift to a lower-carbon economy, may reduce the value of investments in certain industries.
- **Regulation and disclosure.** Insurance regulators, including the New York State Department of Financial Services, have issued guidance on the management of climate-related financial risks. Additional state climate disclosure requirements may increase our compliance costs and expose us to litigation or enforcement risk. Divergent and evolving stakeholder expectations on climate matters may also expose us to reputational risk, regardless of the positions we take.

The long-term effects of climate change and of societal and policy responses to it are highly uncertain. They could materially and adversely affect our results of operations and financial condition.

***Our reserves for unpaid losses and loss adjustment expenses may be inadequate, which could materially reduce our earnings and capital.***

We maintain reserves for unpaid losses and loss adjustment expenses for our property and casualty businesses. These reserves represent our best estimate of the ultimate cost of resolving all claims incurred through the balance sheet date, including claims not yet reported to us. Reserves do not represent an exact calculation of liability. They are estimates based on actuarial projections and management judgment, and they reflect assumptions about many factors, including:

- loss emergence and development patterns;
- claim frequency and severity trends;
- economic and medical cost inflation;
- changes in claims handling practices and the timing of claim settlements;
- changes in our mix of business, policy terms, and underwriting standards;
- judicial decisions, legislative changes, and evolving legal theories of liability; and
- the effects of litigation trends, including jury awards, litigation funding, and attorney involvement.

Reserving uncertainty is greatest in long-tail lines, where claims may be reported or resolved many years after the coverage period. For us, these lines include general liability, excess and umbrella casualty, commercial automobile liability, products liability, professional and management liability, construction defect, and workers' compensation. Our excess and surplus casualty and program businesses, which cover classes outside standard market appetite and may have less credible historical data, are particularly subject to reserving uncertainty. Reserves for our large deductible and retrospectively rated National Accounts programs depend partly on the projected performance of the underlying insured programs and on the collectibility of amounts due from insureds.

Our reserving process relies on information provided by claims, underwriting, and business unit personnel, and by managing general agents and program administrators for business written under delegated authority. If that information is incomplete, inaccurate, or untimely, our reserve estimates may be adversely affected. Changes in our claims practices, including the adoption of new technology platforms and automation, may also change historical development patterns in ways that make our actuarial methods less reliable.

We review and update our reserve estimates quarterly. If our estimates prove too low, we must increase reserves and recognize the increase as a charge to earnings in the period in which we determine it is needed. Reserve increases, often referred to as adverse prior-year development, could be material. They could also lead rating agencies or regulators to question the adequacy of our capital or the quality of our reserving process. Conversely, favorable development in prior periods does not mean it will continue. Many of our long-tail exposures will not be fully resolved for many years, and the ultimate cost of resolving them could differ materially from our current reserves.

***Our legacy asbestos and environmental exposures are subject to significant uncertainty and could result in material adverse development.***

Our Corporate and Other run-off operations include reserves for asbestos and environmental claims arising from general liability policies written many years ago. Estimating these liabilities is especially difficult. Traditional actuarial reserving techniques are less reliable for these claims for several reasons:

- long and variable latency periods between exposure and manifestation of injury or damage;
- uncertainty about the number, identity, and financial condition of claimants and defendants;
- disputes about whether coverage exists, how many occurrences there are, which policy years are triggered, and how losses should be allocated among insurers and policy periods;
- evolving legal doctrines, including theories of liability involving newly identified substances; and
- the bankruptcy of defendants and the use of bankruptcy and related procedures to resolve mass tort liabilities.

Legislative, regulatory, and judicial developments could expand the liabilities of our policyholders or our coverage obligations. Examples include the designation of additional substances as hazardous, changes to environmental remediation standards, and rulings on the treatment of policy limits or the definition of an occurrence. Emerging exposures, such as claims related to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances and other chemicals, could give rise to new legacy-type liabilities under older or more recent policies. We periodically conduct ground-up reviews of these exposures, and those reviews may result in reserve increases. Commutations and other strategies to reduce these exposures may not be available on favorable terms. Adverse development on these exposures could materially affect our results of operations in any period.

***Loss cost inflation, including social inflation, may cause our pricing to be inadequate and our reserves to develop adversely.***

Our pricing and reserving reflect assumptions about future loss costs, and loss costs have been rising faster than general inflation in several lines. In personal and commercial automobile, repair costs have increased because vehicles now incorporate advanced sensors and electronics, parts and labor costs are higher, and repairs take longer. Medical costs affect our bodily injury, workers' compensation, and disability businesses. Construction material and labor costs affect our homeowners and commercial property businesses.

We also face "social inflation," a term commonly used to describe increases in insured losses beyond general economic inflation. Its drivers include:

- higher jury verdicts and settlements, including so-called "nuclear verdicts";
- broader theories of liability and expanded interpretations of policy coverage;
- increased attorney involvement in claims and aggressive advertising by plaintiffs' attorneys;
- third-party litigation funding;
- legislative changes that extend statutes of limitations or create new causes of action, including reviver statutes for historical abuse claims; and
- erosion of tort reform measures.

These trends particularly affect our general liability, commercial automobile, umbrella and excess casualty, professional and management liability, and excess and surplus casualty lines. Some of these trends tend to emerge gradually and may not be fully reflected in historical data until years after the policies are written.

Rate regulation, competitive pressures, and policyholder affordability constraints may prevent us from raising rates enough or quickly enough to keep pace with loss cost trends, particularly in personal lines. Sustained or unanticipated loss cost inflation could cause our current-year loss ratios to deteriorate, require us to strengthen reserves for prior accident years, and materially reduce our profitability. In response, we may take underwriting actions, such as reducing new business, non-renewing policies, or exiting markets. Those actions could reduce our premium volume and harm our distribution relationships.

***Our pricing and underwriting decisions may prove inadequate.***

Our profitability depends substantially on our ability to underwrite risks and set rates accurately. Pricing requires estimates of future losses, loss adjustment expenses, operating expenses, investment returns, and the cost of capital, often for coverage periods that extend well into the future. Our underwriting and pricing may prove inadequate for several reasons:

- the data underlying our rating plans or predictive models may be incomplete, inaccurate, or not representative of future experience;
- we may fail to identify emerging loss trends or new exposures promptly;
- underwriters may fail to follow our guidelines or exceed their authority;
- competitive conditions may cause us to accept lower rates or broader terms than our indications support;
- regulatory restrictions may delay or prevent us from using rates we consider adequate, or may limit the rating variables or data sources we may use; and
- policy language may be interpreted by courts or regulators to provide broader coverage than we intended.

Our growth strategies carry additional risk. These include expansion of our Specialty and Excess Insurance segment, entry into new specialty classes and programs, expansion into new jurisdictions, and growth through digital and embedded distribution. New products and markets may lack the historical data needed to price reliably, may attract adversely selected risks, and may perform worse than our existing business. If our pricing or underwriting proves inadequate, our results of operations and financial condition could be materially and adversely affected.

***Actual experience in our Life, Retirement and Group Benefits segment may differ materially from our assumptions.***

The profitability of our life insurance, annuity, and group benefits products depends substantially on the extent to which actual experience matches the assumptions we use to price products and establish liabilities. These assumptions concern:

- mortality and longevity;
- morbidity, including disability incidence and recovery rates;
- policyholder persistency, lapse, surrender, withdrawal, and premium payment behavior;
- utilization of policy options, such as conversion privileges and annuitization;
- investment returns and crediting rate spreads; and
- expenses.

We review these assumptions at least annually and update them as appropriate. Assumption changes and differences between actual and expected experience can cause significant volatility in our results. Under current GAAP accounting for long-duration contracts, changes in cash flow assumptions for future policy benefit liabilities are reflected in earnings, and changes in the fair value of market risk benefits, excluding changes attributable to our own credit risk, are generally reflected in net income.

Several areas present particular risk:

- **Mortality.** Higher-than-expected mortality, whether from pandemics, opioid and other substance-related deaths, changing health trends, or other causes, would adversely affect our individual and group life businesses. Our accelerated underwriting programs rely on electronic data sources rather than traditional medical examinations, and they may not perform as expected, which could result in adverse mortality experience.
- **Longevity.** Longer-than-expected lifespans would increase the cost of our single premium immediate annuities and certain other retirement products.
- **Disability.** Disability incidence and recovery rates are affected by economic conditions, unemployment, medical practice, mental health trends, the claims-paying environment, and the effectiveness of our return-to-work programs. A recession could increase disability claims and lengthen claim durations.
- **Policyholder behavior.** In a rising or high interest rate environment, annuity and universal life policyholders may surrender contracts to pursue higher yields elsewhere. This could force us to sell assets at a loss to fund withdrawals. Conversely, in a declining interest rate environment, persistency may increase on contracts with guaranteed minimum crediting rates, compressing our spreads. Policyholders may also make decisions that are less economically rational, or more rational, than our assumptions anticipate, including with respect to guaranteed benefits in our legacy variable annuity block.
- **Group rate guarantees.** Group benefits pricing is generally guaranteed for a rate period of one to three years, so we may be unable to adjust rates promptly in response to adverse experience.

If actual experience is materially less favorable than our assumptions, we may be required to increase liabilities and could experience lower profitability, reduced statutory capital, and potential rating agency actions.

***A pandemic or other widespread public health event could adversely affect our business across multiple segments.***

A pandemic or other widespread public health event could increase mortality claims in our individual and group life businesses and increase disability, critical illness, and hospital indemnity claims. It could also disrupt claims handling, underwriting, and other operations. It may affect our property and casualty business through business interruption, event cancellation, workers' compensation, and liability claims, including claims asserting coverage that our policies were not intended to provide. Legislatures and regulators may seek to retroactively mandate such coverage, require premium refunds or payment deferrals, or prohibit cancellations. A pandemic could also cause financial market volatility, depress the value of our investments, reduce demand for our products, increase credit losses, and impair the ability of our distribution partners, vendors, and employees to perform their functions. The combined effect of these factors could be material.

***Our cyber insurance business exposes us to the risk of large, correlated losses.***

Through our Specialty and Excess Insurance segment, we write first-party and third-party cyber coverage, including coverage for data breaches, ransomware events, network security failures, and business interruption. Cyber risk is a relatively new and rapidly evolving line, and historical loss data is limited. The threat landscape changes continuously as threat actors adopt new tactics, including tactics enabled by artificial intelligence.

A single cyber event could affect a large number of our insureds at the same time. Examples include a widespread malware campaign, the compromise of a widely used software product or managed service provider, or an outage at a major cloud services provider. Such an event could produce aggregated losses far exceeding our expectations. Cyber accumulation modeling is less mature than natural catastrophe modeling, and our estimates of potential accumulation losses may prove inaccurate. Policy wordings, including exclusions for war, infrastructure failures, and systemic events, may be challenged or interpreted differently than we intend. Our commercial property, general liability, management liability, and other policies may also be exposed to "silent" or non-affirmative cyber losses despite our efforts to clarify coverage. Reinsurance capacity for cyber risk may be limited or costly. Any of these factors could materially affect our results of operations.

***Reinsurance may not be available, affordable, or adequate to protect us.***

We rely on reinsurance to manage our exposure to catastrophe events and large individual risks, to support our underwriting capacity, and to manage our capital and earnings volatility. Our reinsurance program includes:

- property catastrophe excess of loss reinsurance;
- per-risk and casualty excess of loss reinsurance;
- quota share and excess of loss treaties supporting specialty lines;
- facultative reinsurance;
- multi-year capacity obtained through catastrophe bonds issued by special purpose reinsurance vehicles;
- yearly renewable term and coinsurance arrangements for life mortality risk; and
- reinsurance of portions of certain closed blocks, including our legacy variable annuity block.

The availability, scope, and cost of reinsurance are subject to prevailing market conditions, which are largely beyond our control. They are influenced by catastrophe experience, reinsurer capital levels, the appetite of alternative capital providers, investment returns, and loss trends in the reinsured lines. In recent years, property catastrophe reinsurance markets have experienced significant price increases, higher attachment points, and narrower terms. Casualty reinsurance markets have tightened in response to adverse development and social inflation.

If we cannot obtain reinsurance on acceptable terms, we may have to retain more risk, reduce our writings in exposed lines or geographies, accept higher reinsurance costs, or hold more capital. Each of these could reduce our profitability or growth. Reinsurance contracts may contain exclusions, limits, reinstatement provisions, and other terms that leave us with greater net losses than anticipated. Disputes with reinsurers over the interpretation of contract terms could delay or reduce our recoveries. Catastrophe bonds may have trigger mechanics, such as industry loss or parametric triggers, that do not correspond precisely to our actual losses, creating basis risk.

***We are exposed to the credit risk of our reinsurers and other counterparties.***

Reinsurance does not relieve us of our primary obligations to policyholders. If a reinsurer fails to pay amounts it owes us, whether because of insolvency, financial distress, a coverage dispute, or other reasons, we remain liable for the underlying claims. The risk of reinsurer non-payment may be greatest following a major catastrophe event or a period of widespread market stress, which is when we would most need our reinsurance recoveries. Reinsurance recoverables on long-tail casualty and life business may remain outstanding for many years, during which the financial condition of the reinsurer may deteriorate.

Collateral arrangements may not be sufficient to cover our exposure, or may be subject to legal challenge. Regulatory changes affecting reinsurance collateral requirements, including reciprocal arrangements with certain foreign jurisdictions, may reduce the collateral available to us. We are also exposed to credit risk on amounts due from:

- insureds under large deductible and retrospectively rated programs;
- captive insurance companies for which we provide fronting arrangements;
- agents and brokers who collect premiums on our behalf; and
- derivative and other financial counterparties.

Losses from counterparty defaults could materially affect our results of operations and financial condition.

***The property and casualty insurance industry is highly competitive and cyclical.***

We compete with a large number of insurers and other risk-bearing entities, many of which have greater financial, marketing, or technological resources than we do or have higher financial strength ratings. Competitors include:

- national and regional stock and mutual insurers;
- reciprocal exchanges;
- specialty underwriters;
- technology-driven insurers and distribution platforms;
- self-insurance programs, captives, and risk retention groups;
- government-sponsored programs; and
- alternative capital providers, including insurance-linked securities funds.

In personal lines, competitors with large direct marketing budgets and strong brand recognition have substantial market presence. In life and retirement, we compete with insurers, banks, asset managers, and other financial services firms.

The property and casualty industry has historically been cyclical. Periods of intense competition and inadequate pricing have alternated with periods of firm pricing and restricted capacity. Commercial lines pricing has generally been firm in recent periods, and the excess and surplus lines market has benefited from a flow of business out of the standard market. These conditions may not continue. New capital entering the market, standard carriers re-entering classes they previously exited, or improved loss experience could lead to softening prices and broadening terms. If we maintain underwriting discipline during a soft market, our premium volume may decline. If we instead compete on price or terms, our profitability may deteriorate. Competitive pressures in workers' compensation have already constrained pricing in that line. Our failure to compete effectively could materially affect our business and results of operations.

***We depend on third-party distribution relationships, and industry consolidation could reduce our access to customers or increase our distribution costs.***

We distribute our products principally through independent agents, retail and wholesale brokers, employee benefits brokers and consultants, banks, broker-dealers, independent marketing organizations, program administrators, managing general agents, and affinity and digital partners. These intermediaries generally represent multiple carriers, are not obligated to place business with us, and may favor competitors that offer higher commissions, broader products, more efficient technology, or greater underwriting capacity.

Consolidation in the insurance brokerage industry, including acquisitions of independent agencies by large brokers and private equity-backed aggregators, has concentrated premium among a smaller number of large intermediaries. This consolidation may increase the bargaining power of these intermediaries. They may demand higher compensation, preferential terms, or data access, or shift business among carriers in large blocks. Although no single distribution partner accounts for a level of our consolidated premiums that we consider material to our business as a whole, the loss of one or more large brokerage relationships could adversely affect certain of our businesses. Our Specialty and Excess Insurance segment relies heavily on wholesale brokers, and our annuity business relies on a limited number of bank and broker-dealer distribution relationships.

Our distribution relationships could also be harmed by:

- regulatory changes affecting producer compensation, including contingent and supplemental compensation arrangements;
- changes in best interest and suitability standards for annuity and life insurance sales;
- litigation or regulatory investigations involving distribution practices;
- disruption in our technology platforms that support agents and brokers; or
- reputational harm affecting us or our distribution partners.

Our direct-to-consumer and digital channels depend on effective marketing, search engine and app store placement, and digital partnerships. These channels may face rising customer acquisition costs and may compete with our independent agent channel, creating channel conflict.

***Business written through managing general agents and program administrators under delegated authority exposes us to additional risks.***

We write certain specialty and commercial business through managing general agents and program administrators to whom we delegate underwriting, and in some cases claims, authority. Although we maintain an oversight framework that includes due diligence, underwriting guidelines, authority limits, audits, and performance monitoring, we have less direct control over the underwriting and claims decisions made by these third parties than over our own employees' decisions. Their interests may not fully align with ours, particularly where compensation is based on premium volume.

We may be exposed to losses if a managing general agent or program administrator:

- binds risks outside our guidelines or authority;
- misprices business;
- reports data inaccurately or late;
- mishandles premiums or claims funds;
- engages in fraud or other misconduct;
- fails to comply with applicable regulations; or
- becomes financially distressed or ceases operations.

Inaccurate or delayed data from these partners may impair our ability to monitor aggregations, estimate reserves, and file accurate regulatory reports. Rating agencies and regulators have focused increasingly on delegated authority arrangements, and heightened scrutiny may increase our oversight costs or limit our ability to use these arrangements. Problems with delegated authority business could cause unexpected losses, regulatory sanctions, and reputational harm.

***Terrorist attacks and acts of war could cause losses that exceed our expectations and are not fully covered by reinsurance or government programs.***

Our commercial property, workers' compensation, commercial automobile, general liability, and group life and disability businesses are exposed to losses from terrorist attacks. Our concentration of commercial and group exposures in major metropolitan areas, including New York City, increases this exposure. Workers' compensation coverage generally cannot exclude terrorism losses, and state law may limit our ability to exclude terrorism from certain property coverages.

The federal terrorism risk insurance program provides a backstop for insured losses from certified acts of terrorism. It is subject to an industry loss trigger, insurer deductibles based on prior-year premiums, coinsurance, and an overall cap. The program does not cover all terrorism events, including events that are not certified. It is scheduled to expire unless reauthorized by Congress, and any reauthorization may contain less favorable terms. Losses from nuclear, biological, chemical, or radiological attacks could be particularly severe, and private reinsurance for these perils is limited. Acts of war, civil unrest, and state-sponsored cyber operations may also give rise to claims and coverage disputes. Terrorism or war-related losses could materially affect our results and financial condition.

***Our participation in residual market mechanisms and government programs exposes us to assessments and other costs.***

We are required by law to participate in various state residual market mechanisms, including:

- automobile assigned risk plans;
- FAIR plans;
- windstorm and beach plans; and
- workers' compensation residual markets.

These mechanisms provide coverage to individuals and businesses unable to obtain insurance in the voluntary market. They often charge rates that do not reflect the risks they assume. As private market availability declines in catastrophe-exposed regions, residual market mechanisms have grown, increasing their exposure to large catastrophe losses. When a residual market mechanism incurs a deficit, participating insurers may be assessed, generally in proportion to their market share in the relevant jurisdiction and lines. These assessments may be large, may be imposed with little notice following a catastrophe, and may not be fully recoverable through policyholder surcharges. We also participate in the National Flood Insurance Program as a write-your-own carrier. Changes to that program, including lapses in authorization or changes in carrier compensation, could affect our operations and costs.

***Changes in technology and consumer behavior could reduce demand for our products or change loss patterns in ways we fail to anticipate.***

Advances in vehicle safety technology, driver assistance systems, and autonomous driving may reduce the frequency of automobile accidents over time, which could reduce demand for personal automobile insurance and premium levels. They could also shift liability from drivers to vehicle manufacturers and technology providers, changing who buys insurance and how it is distributed. Conversely, the cost of repairing vehicles equipped with advanced technology has increased claim severity. Connected home devices, telematics, and other technologies may change loss patterns, the data available to insurers, and customer expectations about pricing and service. The sharing economy, embedded insurance offerings, and new distribution models may reduce the importance of traditional channels in which we have strong positions. If we fail to anticipate or adapt to these developments, our competitive position and results could be adversely affected.

## Risks Related to Our Investments and Financial Markets

***Changes in interest rates could adversely affect our investment income, the value of our investments, our liabilities, and the profitability of our products.***

Our investment portfolio consists primarily of investment-grade fixed maturity securities, and investment income is a significant component of our revenues and earnings. Interest rate changes affect us in several ways.

**Declining or low interest rates** could:

- reduce the yields we earn on new investments and on reinvestment of maturing securities and prepayments;
- increase prepayments on mortgage-backed securities and calls on callable bonds, accelerating reinvestment at lower yields;
- compress spreads on fixed annuities and universal life products, particularly where minimum guaranteed crediting rates prevent us from lowering credited rates;
- increase the cost of guaranteed benefits in our legacy variable annuity block and the liabilities for long-duration products;
- require us to strengthen statutory reserves as a result of asset adequacy testing and principle-based reserving; and
- reduce the profitability of our long-tail property and casualty business, for which investment income on reserves is an important component of total returns.

**Rising or high interest rates** could:

- reduce the fair value of our fixed maturity securities, increasing unrealized losses and reducing GAAP stockholders' equity through accumulated other comprehensive income;
- increase surrenders and withdrawals of annuity and universal life contracts as policyholders seek higher yields, potentially forcing us to sell securities at a loss to fund outflows;
- increase competition from bank deposits, money market funds, and other products offering higher yields; and
- increase our borrowing costs.

Rapid or unexpected interest rate changes, and changes in the shape of the yield curve, may affect us more than gradual changes. Our asset-liability management program seeks to match the duration and cash flows of our assets with those of our liabilities, particularly for our life and retirement business. However, perfect matching is not achievable, and changes in policyholder behavior or liability cash flows may cause mismatches. Our interest rate hedging strategies may not fully offset the effects of rate changes. Interest rate movements could materially affect our results of operations, financial condition, and capital position.

***We are exposed to credit risk in our investment portfolio, and defaults, downgrades, or widening credit spreads could reduce our earnings and capital.***

Our investment portfolio includes:

- corporate bonds;
- municipal bonds;
- residential and commercial mortgage-backed securities;
- other asset-backed securities;
- commercial mortgage loans;
- private placement debt; and
- limited partnerships and other alternative investments.

Issuers, borrowers, and counterparties may default on their obligations or experience credit deterioration, particularly during economic downturns. Widening credit spreads would reduce the fair value of our fixed maturity securities. Rating downgrades of securities we hold may increase the capital we must hold under statutory risk-based capital requirements and rating agency capital models, even absent a default.

Certain portions of our portfolio may present heightened credit risk. Commercial real estate, including office properties affected by changes in work patterns and properties exposed to physical climate risks, may experience declining values, lower occupancy, and refinancing difficulties. These conditions could adversely affect our commercial mortgage loans and commercial mortgage-backed securities. Our municipal bond holdings may be affected by fiscal stress in state and local governments. Private placement debt and private credit investments may lack the transparency and liquidity of public securities, and their credit quality may be harder to assess and monitor. Concentrations in particular issuers, industries, or geographies could magnify losses.

We are required to estimate expected credit losses on many of our investments, and our estimates involve significant judgment. Credit losses and increases in our allowance for credit losses could materially affect our results of operations, capital, and ratings.

***Our legacy variable annuity block and our indexed products expose us to equity market and hedging risks.***

Although we no longer issue new variable annuities with guaranteed living benefits, we continue to administer a closed block of these contracts. The guarantees in these contracts may require us to make payments to policyholders that exceed their account values if equity markets decline, interest rates fall, or market volatility increases. Our exposure under these guarantees is reflected in market risk benefit liabilities, which are measured at fair value. Changes in their value, other than changes attributable to our own credit risk, are generally recognized in net income and may cause significant volatility in our reported results. Our fixed indexed annuities and indexed universal life products credit interest based partly on the performance of external market indices, and we hedge the associated index exposure with derivatives.

We manage these risks through a hedging program and reinsurance, but neither will fully protect us. Hedging risks include:

- basis risk, where the performance of hedging instruments does not correspond to the underlying liabilities;
- the cost of rebalancing in volatile markets;
- the availability and cost of derivatives;
- model risk in estimating liability sensitivities;
- policyholder behavior that differs from our assumptions; and
- counterparty and collateral risk.

Our hedging program is designed with reference to economic, rather than accounting or statutory, measures of exposure, so hedging gains and losses may not align with changes in reported liabilities or statutory reserves in the same period. Equity market declines may also reduce fee income on separate account assets and increase statutory reserve and capital requirements. Severe equity market declines or sustained low interest rates could require us to contribute additional capital to our life subsidiaries.

***Some of our investments are relatively illiquid and may be difficult to sell at favorable prices or at all.***

A portion of our investment portfolio consists of investments that are relatively illiquid, including commercial mortgage loans, private placement debt, limited partnerships, and other alternative investments. Even certain publicly traded securities may become illiquid during periods of market stress. If we need to raise cash quickly, for example to pay catastrophe claims, fund elevated annuity surrenders, post derivative collateral, or meet other obligations, we may be unable to sell these investments promptly or may be forced to sell them at prices significantly below their carrying values. Limited partnership investments may also require us to fund additional capital commitments at times when we would prefer to preserve liquidity. Investment income from limited partnerships and alternative investments is variable, is often reported on a lag, and may decline significantly in adverse market conditions.

***The determination of the fair value of our investments and the recognition of credit losses involve significant judgment and could change materially.***

Many of our investments, and certain of our liabilities and derivatives, are carried at fair value. When market prices are unavailable or markets are inactive, as is often the case for private placements, structured securities, and alternative investments, we estimate fair value using pricing models and unobservable inputs that require significant judgment. During periods of market disruption, it may be difficult to value certain securities, and valuations may change significantly and rapidly. Our valuations may differ materially from the prices we could realize in an actual sale. Our assessments of whether investments have experienced credit-related declines in value, and of the appropriate allowance for credit losses, are inherently uncertain and could change based on new information or changed circumstances. Changes in valuations, credit loss estimates, or the methodologies we use could materially affect our reported results and stockholders' equity.

***Our use of derivatives exposes us to counterparty, collateral, and liquidity risks.***

We use derivatives primarily to hedge interest rate, equity market, and foreign currency risks, including the risks associated with our indexed products and legacy variable annuity guarantees. Our derivative counterparties may fail to perform their obligations, particularly in periods of financial market stress. Our derivatives are generally subject to collateral posting requirements. Significant market movements could require us to post substantial collateral on short notice, straining our liquidity. Regulatory changes affecting derivatives, including clearing, margin, and reporting requirements, may increase our hedging costs or limit the availability of certain instruments. A failure of our hedging program to perform as intended could materially affect our results of operations and capital.

## Risks Related to Our Capital, Liquidity, and Financial Strength Ratings

***As a holding company, we depend on our subsidiaries to meet our obligations and pay dividends, and insurance regulators limit the ability of our subsidiaries to make payments to us.***

Stonegate Systems Holdings, Inc. is a holding company with no direct insurance operations. Its principal assets are the capital stock of its subsidiaries. Its ability to pay dividends to shareholders, repurchase shares, service its indebtedness, and pay corporate expenses depends largely on dividends, interest, and other permitted payments from its subsidiaries.

The insurance laws of our insurance subsidiaries' domiciliary states, including New York, restrict the amount of dividends those subsidiaries may pay without prior regulatory approval. Ordinary dividend capacity is generally determined by formulas based on statutory surplus and statutory net income. Dividends exceeding these limits require prior approval. Regulators may restrict even ordinary dividends if they determine that an insurer's surplus is not reasonable relative to its liabilities or adequate to its financial needs. Transactions between our insurance subsidiaries and affiliates, including service and cost-sharing agreements, intercompany loans, and reinsurance, are also subject to regulatory review and approval requirements.

Several factors could reduce the dividend capacity of our insurance subsidiaries, including:

- catastrophe losses;
- reserve strengthening;
- investment losses;
- changes in statutory accounting or reserving requirements;
- increases in required capital; and
- regulatory actions.

Rating agency capital expectations and our internal capital targets may also cause us to retain capital in our subsidiaries beyond regulatory minimums. Our non-insurance subsidiaries, including Stonegate Benefits Administration, Stonegate Asset Management, and Stonegate Brokerage Services, generate cash flows that are smaller and may be subject to their own regulatory capital requirements. If our subsidiaries cannot pay sufficient dividends, we may be unable to meet our obligations, pay dividends to shareholders, or repurchase shares, and we may need to raise capital on unfavorable terms.

In addition, the claims of creditors and policyholders of our subsidiaries, including in any insolvency or rehabilitation proceeding, generally rank ahead of the claims of the holding company and its creditors and shareholders.

***A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could materially harm our business.***

Financial strength ratings are important to our competitive position, particularly in commercial, specialty, and life and retirement markets, where many distribution partners and customers require insurers to maintain ratings at or above specified levels. Rating agencies evaluate our insurance subsidiaries and the holding company based on:

- capital adequacy, often measured through proprietary capital models;
- operating performance;
- business profile;
- enterprise risk management;
- reserve adequacy;
- financial leverage; and
- other factors.

Rating agencies may change their methodologies or criteria at any time, and those changes may require us to hold more capital or change our business to maintain current ratings.

A downgrade, or the announcement of a negative outlook or ratings watch, could have several adverse effects:

- reduced new business and lower retention, particularly among commercial and specialty customers and distribution partners with minimum rating requirements;
- increased annuity surrenders and difficulty selling annuities and life products through financial institutions;
- triggers in reinsurance, derivative, letter of credit, and other agreements that require us to post additional collateral or permit counterparties to terminate;
- higher borrowing costs and reduced access to capital markets; and
- harm to our reputation with policyholders, distribution partners, regulators, and investors.

Rating agency actions are outside our control. They may be prompted by events affecting us specifically or by developments affecting the insurance industry generally. A downgrade could materially affect our business, results of operations, financial condition, and liquidity.

***Our balance sheet is substantially composed of policyholder-related liabilities, and adverse developments could rapidly erode our stockholders' equity.***

As of December 31, 2025, we reported total assets of approximately $125.3 billion, total liabilities of approximately $92.3 billion, and total stockholders' equity of approximately $32.9 billion. Total liabilities therefore represented approximately 73.7% of total assets, and the ratio of total liabilities to total stockholders' equity was approximately 2.80 to 1.

Most of our liabilities consist of:

- reserves for unpaid losses and loss adjustment expenses;
- unearned premiums;
- future policy benefits;
- policyholder account balances;
- market risk benefits; and
- separate account liabilities.

The measurement of many of these liabilities depends on actuarial estimates and assumptions that are inherently uncertain. Because our liabilities are large relative to our equity, a given percentage increase in our estimated liabilities, or a given percentage decline in the value of our assets, would produce a proportionally larger percentage reduction in our stockholders' equity. Reserve strengthening, catastrophe losses, investment losses, or adverse assumption changes could therefore significantly reduce our equity and statutory capital. Such reductions could, in turn, trigger rating agency actions, reduce our dividend capacity, require us to raise capital, or limit our ability to grow. In addition, a substantial portion of our stockholders' equity may be represented by accumulated other comprehensive income or loss, which fluctuates with interest rates and credit spreads, and by intangible assets and deferred tax assets, whose recoverability depends on future earnings.

***We may need additional capital in the future, which may not be available on favorable terms or at all.***

We may need to raise additional capital to:

- replenish capital following catastrophe losses, reserve strengthening, or investment losses;
- maintain financial strength ratings or regulatory capital levels;
- refinance maturing indebtedness;
- fund growth or acquisitions; or
- meet liquidity needs during periods of financial stress.

Our access to equity and debt markets, bank credit facilities, letters of credit, and other financing sources depends on market conditions, our financial condition and ratings, investor perceptions of the insurance industry, and other factors largely beyond our control. During periods of financial market disruption, financing may be unavailable or available only on unfavorable terms. Equity financing could dilute our existing shareholders, and debt financing could increase our financial leverage, interest expense, and exposure to restrictive covenants. Our debt instruments and credit facilities contain covenants that may restrict our business activities and require us to maintain specified financial ratios. Breach of these covenants could result in acceleration of indebtedness. If we cannot obtain adequate capital when needed, our business, results of operations, and financial condition could be materially and adversely affected.

***Regulatory capital requirements may increase, and our insurance subsidiaries may become subject to regulatory intervention if their capital declines.***

Our insurance subsidiaries are subject to minimum capital and surplus requirements and risk-based capital standards. If an insurer's total adjusted capital falls below specified levels relative to its risk-based capital requirement, regulators must or may take increasingly intensive action, up to and including placing the insurer under regulatory control. Although each of our insurance subsidiaries maintained total adjusted capital above action levels as of the most recent statutory filing date, adverse developments could reduce their capital below those levels.

Regulators periodically revise risk-based capital formulas, including the treatment of particular asset classes such as structured securities and private credit, and the factors applied to catastrophe and underwriting risks. Our group is also subject to the group capital calculation, and international capital standards may influence U.S. regulatory approaches over time. Principle-based reserving and asset adequacy requirements for our life subsidiaries may require additional reserves under adverse scenarios. Changes in these requirements could require us to hold more capital, change our investment strategy, or modify our products. Any of these could reduce our returns.

***Our liquidity could be strained by large, unexpected cash demands.***

We need liquidity to pay claims and policyholder benefits, fund annuity surrenders and withdrawals, pay operating expenses, post collateral, service debt, and pay dividends. Several events could create large and sudden cash demands, including:

- major catastrophe events;
- elevated annuity surrenders triggered by rising interest rates or a ratings downgrade;
- collateral calls on derivatives, reinsurance, or letter of credit arrangements; and
- adverse judgments in litigation.

If our cash and readily marketable assets are insufficient, we may have to sell investments at a loss, draw on credit facilities, or seek financing on unfavorable terms. Liquidity stress at our life subsidiaries could also be exacerbated by policyholder behavior during periods of market disruption. A liquidity shortfall could materially affect our business, financial condition, and ratings.

## Risks Related to Regulation and Legal Matters

***We are subject to extensive regulation, and changes in regulation or our failure to comply could materially affect our business.***

Our insurance subsidiaries are subject to extensive regulation by the insurance regulators of their domiciliary states and of each other jurisdiction in which they do business. The New York State Department of Financial Services is the domiciliary regulator of several of our principal insurance subsidiaries and is widely regarded as one of the most rigorous insurance regulators in the United States. Insurance regulation is designed primarily to protect policyholders, not shareholders or creditors. Regulators have broad discretion over many aspects of our business, including:

- licensing;
- rates and forms;
- capital adequacy and reserves;
- permitted investments;
- statutory accounting;
- market conduct;
- affiliate transactions;
- dividends; and
- changes of control.

Our non-insurance subsidiaries are subject to additional regulation. Stonegate Brokerage Services is a registered broker-dealer and member of the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority. Stonegate Asset Management is a registered investment adviser. Our registered products and separate accounts are subject to federal securities laws. Stonegate Reinsurance Ltd. is regulated in its jurisdiction of domicile.

Regulators conduct periodic financial and market conduct examinations. Those examinations may result in fines, restitution, required changes to business practices, or other sanctions. Laws and regulations, and their interpretation and enforcement, change frequently. Changes may increase our compliance costs, restrict our business activities, require us to hold more capital or reserves, or otherwise reduce our profitability. If we fail to comply with applicable laws and regulations, we could face fines, penalties, license suspensions or revocations, restrictions on our activities, and reputational harm. These consequences could be material.

***Rate and form regulation may prevent us from charging adequate rates or managing our exposures.***

Most states require property and casualty insurers to file rates, rating rules, and policy forms with state regulators, and some states require prior approval before rates or forms may be used. Rate regulation, particularly in personal lines and workers' compensation, can delay or prevent us from implementing rate increases we consider necessary in response to rising loss costs. Regulators may approve smaller increases than we request, may take extended periods to act on filings, or may impose conditions on approval. Political pressure related to insurance affordability may intensify during periods of significant rate increases.

Some jurisdictions have imposed moratoriums on cancellations, non-renewals, or rate increases following catastrophe events or during declared emergencies. Others restrict our ability to withdraw from markets or reduce exposure. Regulators may also limit the rating variables we may use, such as insurance scores, territory, or certain external data. These restrictions could prevent us from pricing accurately for risk. If we are unable to charge adequate rates or manage our exposure, our profitability could suffer, and we may be forced to curtail business in affected jurisdictions, which may itself be subject to regulatory restrictions. Excess and surplus lines insurers enjoy greater freedom of rate and form, but changes in surplus lines regulation could reduce that freedom.

***Evolving regulation of data, algorithms, and artificial intelligence could restrict our underwriting, pricing, marketing, and claims practices.***

We rely on predictive models, machine learning, external consumer data, and, increasingly, generative artificial intelligence across our underwriting, pricing, marketing, and claims functions. Regulators are increasingly focused on whether insurers' use of these tools results in unfair discrimination, lacks transparency, or harms consumers. Many states have adopted the NAIC's model bulletin on insurers' use of artificial intelligence systems, and certain states have adopted or proposed more prescriptive requirements, including testing for disparate impact. New York has issued guidance requiring insurers to demonstrate that their use of external data and artificial intelligence systems does not result in unfair or unlawful discrimination.

These requirements may:

- limit the data sources and rating variables we may use;
- require costly testing, documentation, and governance;
- require us to modify or discontinue models; and
- expose us to enforcement actions or litigation if regulators or plaintiffs conclude that our practices are discriminatory.

Federal and state laws of general applicability governing artificial intelligence, automated decision-making, and consumer data may impose additional obligations. Regulation in this area is evolving rapidly and inconsistently across jurisdictions, which may increase our compliance costs and reduce the effectiveness of our analytical capabilities relative to our expectations.

***Market conduct, sales practice, and best interest standards could increase our costs and expose us to liability.***

We are subject to laws and regulations governing our marketing, sales, underwriting, policyholder service, and claims practices. These include unfair trade practices and unfair claims settlement practices standards. Several states, including New York, have adopted best interest standards for life insurance and annuity transactions that require producers and insurers to act in consumers' best interest when making recommendations. Federal regulators have also proposed or adopted fiduciary and conduct standards affecting retirement product sales. These standards may increase our compliance costs, change the way our products are distributed, reduce sales of certain products, and increase our exposure to litigation and regulatory actions. Our group benefits business is subject to ERISA claims procedure and fiduciary requirements. Our life insurers are subject to requirements relating to the timely payment of death benefits and searches of death records. Failure to comply with any of these requirements could result in fines, restitution, and reputational harm.

***We are subject to litigation and regulatory proceedings that could result in significant liabilities.***

In the ordinary course of business, we are party to numerous legal proceedings. Most involve claims under our insurance policies, but we also face:

- extra-contractual claims alleging bad faith in claims handling, which may seek damages, including punitive damages, exceeding policy limits;
- class actions and other aggregate litigation relating to our underwriting, rating, sales, claims, and policy administration practices, including claims regarding total loss vehicle valuations, depreciation of labor costs, cost of insurance charges on universal life policies, and disability claim determinations;
- coverage litigation, including disputes over business interruption, cyber, mass tort, and catastrophe-related claims;
- employment and other litigation arising from our operations; and
- regulatory investigations and enforcement actions.

Litigation outcomes are inherently unpredictable. Juries in certain jurisdictions have rendered very large verdicts against insurers. Courts may interpret our policies or applicable law in ways that expand our obligations, including retroactively. Changes in law, such as the expansion of statutes of limitations or the creation of new causes of action, may increase our exposure. Our estimates of liabilities for pending litigation may prove inadequate, and an adverse outcome in one or more proceedings could materially affect our results of operations in any period, regardless of our ultimate financial condition.

***Guaranty fund and other assessments could adversely affect our results.***

All states have insurance guaranty associations that pay certain claims of policyholders of insolvent insurers. These associations are funded by assessments on solvent insurers licensed in the state. The amount and timing of assessments are beyond our control and may increase significantly following the insolvency of one or more large insurers. Catastrophe events, adverse reserve development, or financial market stress affecting other insurers could produce such insolvencies. Assessments may be only partially recoverable through premium tax offsets or policy surcharges. We may also be subject to other assessments, including those levied by state workers' compensation funds and other special funds. Material assessments could adversely affect our results of operations.

***Changes in tax laws could adversely affect our products and our financial results.***

Our life insurance and annuity products benefit from favorable federal income tax treatment, including tax deferral on inside build-up and the exclusion of death benefits from income. Changes in tax law that reduce or eliminate these benefits, or that make competing products more attractive, could reduce demand for our products. Changes in the corporate income tax rate, the corporate alternative minimum tax, the taxation of insurance company reserves, the tax treatment of municipal bond interest, or the taxation of investment income could increase our tax burden or change the attractiveness of our investment strategies. We are also subject to state premium taxes and surplus lines taxes, which may increase. Tax authorities may challenge our tax positions, and the outcome of any challenge could be adverse. The realization of our deferred tax assets depends on generating sufficient future taxable income of the appropriate character, and a valuation allowance could be required if that income is not expected.

***Changes in accounting standards and statutory accounting principles could adversely affect our reported results and capital.***

We prepare our consolidated financial statements in accordance with U.S. generally accepted accounting principles. Our insurance subsidiaries prepare statutory financial statements in accordance with statutory accounting principles prescribed or permitted by their domiciliary regulators. Accounting standard setters and regulators periodically issue new standards or change existing ones. These changes may affect the timing and amount of recognized revenues, expenses, liabilities, and capital.

Accounting standards for long-duration insurance contracts have substantially changed how we measure and present liabilities for future policy benefits and market risk benefits, and they have increased the volatility of our reported results and equity. Future changes in GAAP or statutory accounting may require significant system changes and may adversely affect our reported results, regulatory capital, dividend capacity, and rating agency evaluations. These changes could also make our results less comparable to prior periods or to our competitors.

***Privacy and data protection laws impose significant obligations, and our failure to comply could result in substantial liability.***

We collect, use, store, and share significant amounts of personal information, including financial and health information, about customers, claimants, employees, and others. We are subject to a growing body of federal and state privacy and data security laws, including:

- the privacy and safeguards provisions of federal financial services law;
- state insurance privacy and data security laws;
- the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, where applicable;
- the Fair Credit Reporting Act;
- comprehensive state consumer privacy laws; and

Several of these laws provide for substantial penalties and, in some cases, private rights of action. Laws governing biometric information, consumer health data, and the use of tracking technologies on websites and mobile applications have generated significant litigation. Compliance with these laws is costly and may limit our ability to use data in our business. A failure to comply, or a perceived failure, could result in regulatory penalties, litigation, remediation costs, and reputational harm.

***We are subject to anti-money laundering, sanctions, and anti-corruption laws.***

Certain of our insurance products are subject to federal anti-money laundering requirements, and our operations are subject to economic sanctions administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control and to anti-corruption laws. Violations of these laws, even if inadvertent, could result in significant penalties, restrictions on our activities, and reputational harm.

## Risks Related to Our Operations, Technology, and Cybersecurity

***Cybersecurity incidents could disrupt our operations, compromise sensitive information, and expose us to significant liability and reputational harm.***

Our business depends on the secure operation of our information systems and those of our vendors and distribution partners. We collect and maintain large volumes of sensitive personal, financial, and health information, as well as confidential business information. Our systems and those of third parties on which we rely are subject to a range of threats, including:

- ransomware and other malware;
- phishing and social engineering, including schemes using artificially generated audio, video, or text;
- credential theft and account takeover;
- denial-of-service attacks;
- exploitation of software vulnerabilities, including zero-day vulnerabilities;
- supply chain compromises affecting software or service providers;
- insider threats; and
- attacks by nation-state actors and organized criminal groups.

Insurers are attractive targets because of the data they hold and the funds they disburse. The sophistication of threat actors continues to increase, and techniques change frequently, often without warning. Artificial intelligence may enable threat actors to scale and automate attacks.

We maintain an enterprise cybersecurity program, described in "Item 1C. Cybersecurity." However, no security measures can eliminate the risk of a cybersecurity incident, and we may be unable to anticipate, detect, or respond effectively to all threats. We have experienced, and expect to continue to experience, attempted cyber intrusions. Our hybrid work model, cloud migration, and expanding digital connections with distribution partners, vendors, and customers increase our attack surface.

A successful cybersecurity incident could:

- disrupt our ability to quote, bind, issue, and service policies, pay claims, and perform other critical functions, possibly for an extended period;
- result in the theft, loss, corruption, or unauthorized disclosure of personal information or confidential business information;
- result in fraudulent disbursements;
- require us to incur significant costs for investigation, remediation, notification, credit monitoring, and legal defense;
- expose us to regulatory penalties, including under 23 NYCRR Part 500 and state data breach laws, and to litigation, including class actions; and
- damage our reputation with customers, distribution partners, regulators, and investors.

We may be required to disclose cybersecurity incidents publicly, including under SEC rules, before we fully understand their scope. Our insurance coverage for cybersecurity incidents may be insufficient to cover all losses. Any of these consequences could materially affect our business, results of operations, and financial condition.

***Failures or interruptions of our technology systems, or delays or failures in our modernization initiatives, could adversely affect our operations.***

Our business depends on the effective operation of our technology systems for underwriting, policy administration, billing, claims, distribution, investment management, financial reporting, and regulatory compliance. These systems may fail or be interrupted because of hardware or software defects, capacity constraints, power or telecommunications failures, human error, natural disasters, or other events.

We are executing multi-year initiatives to modernize our core platforms, consolidate legacy systems, and migrate applications and data to cloud-based infrastructure. Some of our life and benefits operations continue to run on legacy systems, which may be more difficult and costly to maintain, less secure, and dependent on scarce technical skills. These modernization initiatives involve significant investment and execution risk, including the risk that projects:

- take longer or cost more than planned;
- fail to deliver anticipated functionality or efficiencies;
- disrupt operations during conversion or migration;
- introduce data integrity issues or control weaknesses; or
- divert management attention from other priorities.

If our modernization initiatives fail, we may not achieve the expense reductions, speed to market, and customer and distribution partner experience improvements on which our strategy depends. In that case, our competitive position could be harmed and we may need to write off capitalized software costs.

***We depend on third-party service providers, and their failures could adversely affect our business.***

We rely on third parties for many critical services, including:

- cloud computing and hosting;
- software and data, including catastrophe models, rating data, and consumer data;
- claims services, including independent adjusters, repair networks, and medical bill review;
- investment management for selected asset classes;

Our third-party risk management program may not identify or mitigate all risks associated with our vendors and their own subcontractors. Regulators, including the New York State Department of Financial Services, hold us responsible for the cybersecurity and compliance of our service providers. Disruptions at widely used providers could affect many of our insureds at the same time, increasing our insurance losses while impairing our own operations.

***Our models may prove inaccurate, and our reliance on them could cause us to make poor decisions.***

We rely extensively on models to make business decisions, including:

- catastrophe models to estimate potential losses, manage aggregations, and purchase reinsurance;
- pricing and predictive models to set rates and select risks;
- actuarial models to estimate reserves and policyholder liabilities;
- economic capital and stress testing models to manage capital and risk appetite;
- asset-liability and hedging models to manage interest rate and equity market risk; and
- valuation models to estimate the fair value of investments and liabilities.

Models are simplified representations of complex realities. They depend on assumptions, input data, and methodologies that may be flawed, incomplete, or outdated. Catastrophe models, in particular, are subject to significant uncertainty about event frequency and severity, building vulnerability, demand surge, and secondary perils, and they may not reflect the effects of climate change. Third-party models may be updated in ways that materially change our estimated exposures and capital requirements. Models may also contain errors in design, implementation, or use. Our model risk management framework may not detect all deficiencies. If our models prove inaccurate, we could misprice products, under-reserve, retain excessive risk, or hold insufficient capital, any of which could materially affect our results.

***Our use of artificial intelligence, including generative artificial intelligence, presents operational, legal, and reputational risks.***

We are evaluating and deploying artificial intelligence tools, including generative artificial intelligence, in controlled use cases such as claims document summarization, underwriting submission intake, coding assistance, and customer service support. These technologies are new and evolving and may produce inaccurate, incomplete, biased, or inappropriate outputs, sometimes in ways that are difficult to detect. Their use may also expose confidential or personal information, infringe the intellectual property rights of others, or create cybersecurity vulnerabilities. If we rely on flawed outputs, we could make erroneous underwriting, claims, or other decisions, violate laws or regulations, or harm customers. Regulatory requirements governing artificial intelligence are developing rapidly and may limit our use of these tools or impose significant compliance obligations. Conversely, if competitors adopt these technologies more effectively than we do, our competitive position may suffer.

Our headquarters and significant operations are located in New York, and we maintain regional offices, claims centers, and operations hubs in multiple states. A natural disaster, terrorist attack, pandemic, power outage, or other event affecting these locations or our personnel could impair our ability to operate. Such disruptions may coincide with periods of high claim volume. A catastrophe event affecting our policyholders could also affect our own employees, facilities, and vendors, reducing our capacity to respond at the same time demand for claims service increases. Our business continuity and disaster recovery plans may not be adequate to address all scenarios. Our success depends on our ability to attract, develop, and retain talented employees, including senior executives, underwriters, actuaries, claims professionals, data scientists, technology specialists, investment professionals, and cybersecurity personnel. Competition for talent in many of these disciplines is intense, both within the insurance industry and with technology and other financial services companies. The loss of key personnel, or our inability to recruit and develop qualified employees, could impair our ability to execute our strategy and manage our operations effectively. We maintain internal controls over financial reporting and operations, but control systems provide only reasonable, not absolute, assurance that their objectives will be met. Our controls may fail to prevent or detect errors, fraud, or misconduct. This risk increases during periods of system conversion, organizational change, or rapid growth. Employees, agents, managing general agents, vendors, or others may engage in misconduct, including:

Our stock price could decline, and we could face regulatory sanctions and litigation.

***Damage to our reputation could harm our business.***

Our reputation with customers, distribution partners, regulators, rating agencies, employees, and investors is critical to our success. Our reputation could be harmed by many events, including:

- perceived unfair claims handling, particularly following catastrophe events;
- large rate increases or non-renewals in affected communities;
- cybersecurity incidents;
- regulatory actions or litigation;
- employee or third-party misconduct;
- the positions we take, or decline to take, on public policy matters; and
- negative publicity, including through social media, whether or not accurate.

Reputational harm can spread rapidly and be difficult to reverse. It could reduce our sales and retention, impair our distribution relationships, attract regulatory scrutiny, and adversely affect our ability to attract and retain employees.

***Acquisitions, dispositions, partnerships, and other strategic transactions involve risks that could adversely affect our business.***

We have grown in part through acquisitions, and we may pursue acquisitions, dispositions, partnerships, reinsurance transactions, and other strategic transactions in the future. These transactions involve numerous risks, including:

- difficulties integrating operations, systems, personnel, and cultures;
- failure to realize anticipated synergies, growth, or returns;
- assumption of unknown or underestimated liabilities, including reserve deficiencies in acquired books;
- diversion of management attention;
- regulatory approval requirements that delay transactions or impose conditions; and
- adverse effects on our ratings, capital, or leverage.

Dispositions, including transactions to reduce run-off or legacy exposures, may result in losses on sale, ongoing indemnification obligations, or continued exposure if the counterparty fails to perform. We may be unable to identify suitable transactions or to complete them on acceptable terms. Strategic transactions may not achieve their intended objectives and could materially affect our results of operations and financial condition.

***Our intercompany arrangements, including our pooling arrangement and internal reinsurance, are subject to regulatory oversight and may not achieve their intended objectives.***

Our admitted property and casualty insurers participate in an intercompany pooling arrangement that allocates premiums, losses, and expenses among participants according to fixed percentages. Stonegate Reinsurance Ltd. participates in certain internal risk transfer arrangements. These arrangements are subject to approval and ongoing oversight by insurance regulators, who may require modifications, restrict their use, or challenge the terms. Changes to these arrangements could affect the capital position, dividend capacity, and ratings of individual subsidiaries. The tax treatment of intercompany reinsurance, including arrangements involving offshore affiliates, may be challenged or affected by changes in tax law. If these arrangements are disallowed or modified, our capital efficiency could be reduced and our results adversely affected.

## Risks Related to General Economic and Business Conditions

***Adverse economic conditions could reduce demand for our products, increase claims, and impair our investments.***

Our business is sensitive to general economic conditions in the United States. An economic slowdown or recession could:

- reduce demand for commercial insurance as business activity, payrolls, sales, and insured values decline, reducing premiums in exposure-rated lines such as workers' compensation and general liability;
- reduce employment, decreasing enrollment in our group benefits and voluntary products;
- increase disability claims and lengthen disability claim durations;
- increase fraudulent claims and policy lapses;
- reduce sales of life insurance and annuities and increase surrenders;
- increase credit losses on our investments and amounts due from insureds, agents, and reinsurers; and
- reduce the value of our investment portfolio.

Inflation, even in a growing economy, increases our loss costs, as described above, and may outpace our rate increases. Changes in monetary and fiscal policy, including interest rate decisions, government spending, and public debt levels, could affect interest rates, credit markets, and economic growth in ways that adversely affect us. Disruptions in the banking system or other parts of the financial system could impair liquidity, increase counterparty risk, and depress asset values.

***Political and geopolitical developments could adversely affect our business.***

Geopolitical conflicts, trade disputes and tariffs, sanctions, and political instability could disrupt financial markets and supply chains, increase inflation in repair and replacement costs, and give rise to insured losses. Domestic political developments, including government shutdowns, changes in federal programs such as the National Flood Insurance Program and the federal terrorism risk insurance program, and shifts in regulatory priorities, could affect our business in ways that are difficult to predict. Tariffs on imported vehicles, parts, and building materials could increase claim severity in our automobile and property businesses. State and federal elections may produce changes in insurance regulation, tort law, tax policy, and other areas that affect our results.

## Risks Related to Our Common Stock and Corporate Structure

***Insurance laws, New York corporate law, and provisions of our governing documents could delay, deter, or prevent a change of control.***

The insurance holding company laws of the states where our insurance subsidiaries are domiciled, including New York, require prior regulatory approval of any change of control of a domestic insurer or its holding company. Under these laws, control is generally presumed to exist if a person directly or indirectly acquires a specified percentage of the voting securities of the insurer or its parent. As a result, any person seeking to acquire a significant interest in our common stock may need prior approval from multiple state insurance regulators. These approval requirements may delay, deter, or prevent a change of control, including transactions that some shareholders consider desirable.

As a New York corporation, we are subject to provisions of the New York Business Corporation Law that restrict business combinations with interested shareholders for a period of time after they become interested shareholders unless certain conditions are met. Our certificate of incorporation and bylaws also contain provisions that may make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us or to effect changes in our Board of Directors or management, including:

- advance notice requirements for shareholder proposals and director nominations;
- the ability of our Board to issue preferred stock with terms it determines without shareholder approval; and
- limitations on shareholders' ability to act by written consent or call special meetings.

These provisions could limit the price that investors might be willing to pay in the future for our common stock.

***The market price of our common stock may be volatile.***

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors, including:

- catastrophe losses and reserve developments;
- variations in our quarterly results;
- changes in financial strength or credit ratings;
- changes in analysts' estimates or recommendations;
- announcements of strategic transactions;
- regulatory and legislative developments;
- developments affecting the insurance industry or financial services sector generally; and
- general market and economic conditions.

Many of these factors are beyond our control. Broad market fluctuations may adversely affect the trading price of our common stock regardless of our actual operating performance.

***Our payment of dividends and share repurchases is not guaranteed.***

The declaration and payment of dividends and the repurchase of shares are at the discretion of our Board of Directors. They depend on our results of operations, financial condition, capital requirements, rating agency considerations, the dividend capacity of our subsidiaries, regulatory restrictions, restrictions in our debt instruments, and other factors our Board considers relevant. Our Board may reduce, suspend, or discontinue dividends or share repurchases at any time. A severe catastrophe, significant reserve strengthening, investment losses, or other adverse developments could cause us to retain capital rather than return it to shareholders. Any reduction or suspension of dividends or share repurchases could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

***Future issuances of securities could dilute our shareholders or adversely affect the market price of our common stock.***

We may issue additional shares of common stock, preferred stock, or securities convertible into or exchangeable for common stock to raise capital, fund acquisitions, or compensate employees. These issuances could dilute the ownership interests of existing shareholders and could adversely affect the market price of our common stock. Any preferred stock we issue may have rights, preferences, and privileges senior to those of our common stock, including with respect to dividends and distributions upon liquidation.

***Our indebtedness and other obligations rank effectively junior to the liabilities of our subsidiaries.***

Our holding company indebtedness is structurally subordinated to all existing and future liabilities of our subsidiaries, including policyholder liabilities. In the event of the insolvency, rehabilitation, or liquidation of any of our insurance subsidiaries, the claims of policyholders and other creditors of that subsidiary would be satisfied from its assets before any assets would be available to us. Insurance regulators would control the administration of an insurance subsidiary in such a proceeding, and holders of our securities would have no direct claim against the assets of our subsidiaries. As a result, holders of our debt and equity securities may recover less than holders of the liabilities of our subsidiaries, or nothing at all, in such circumstances.
