## Item 1A. Risk Factors

An investment in our securities involves risk. You should carefully consider the risks described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. That information includes Item 1, "Business," Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," Item 7A, "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk," and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8.

Any of the following risks could materially and adversely affect our business, results of operations, financial condition, liquidity, capital position, financial strength and credit ratings, or the trading price of our securities. These risks are not the only ones we face. Risks that we do not currently know about, or that we currently consider immaterial, could also materially affect us.

Many of these risks are interrelated, and their effects may be compounded when several occur together or in close succession. For example, a severe catastrophe could coincide with dislocation in financial markets, rising reinsurance costs, and rating agency scrutiny. The order in which the risks are presented does not indicate their relative likelihood or the magnitude of their potential effects.

This section contains forward-looking statements. See "Cautionary Note Regarding Forward-Looking Statements" in Item 1.

### Summary of Risk Factors

The following is a summary of the principal risks that could adversely affect us. It is qualified in its entirety by the more complete discussion that follows.

**Insurance operations**

- Catastrophe events, including severe convective storms, hurricanes, wildfires, winter storms, earthquakes, pandemics, and terrorism, could produce losses that exceed our expectations and our reinsurance protection.
- Climate change may increase the frequency, severity, and unpredictability of weather-related losses and may affect our investments and regulatory environment.
- Our property and casualty loss and loss adjustment expense reserves, including reserves for asbestos, environmental, and other legacy exposures, may prove inadequate.
- Social inflation, adverse litigation trends, and economic inflation may increase claim costs beyond our pricing and reserving assumptions.
- Rate regulation, competitive conditions, and model limitations may prevent us from charging adequate prices.
- Actual mortality, morbidity, longevity, and policyholder behavior may differ from our assumptions, including in our closed block of long-term care insurance.

**Reinsurance and counterparties**

- Reinsurance may become unavailable or more expensive, and reinsurers may fail to pay amounts owed to us.
- We are exposed to the credit risk of policyholders in loss-sensitive programs, surety principals, and other counterparties.
- We are subject to assessments from guaranty funds and residual market mechanisms.

**Investments, liquidity, and capital**

- Changes in interest rates, credit spreads, equity markets, and real estate values could reduce the value of, and income from, our investment portfolio and affect the value of our insurance liabilities.
- Our balance sheet is highly leveraged in the sense customary for insurers, so modest percentage changes in asset values or liability estimates can have a large effect on stockholders' equity.
- As a holding company, we depend on dividends from our subsidiaries, which are subject to regulatory restrictions.
- A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could harm our competitive position and increase our cost of capital.

**Regulation and legal matters**

- We are subject to extensive and evolving regulation, including with respect to artificial intelligence, external consumer data, privacy, climate risk, capital, and sales practices.
- Litigation and regulatory proceedings could result in significant liabilities and constraints on our business.

**Technology and operations**

- Cybersecurity incidents, information technology failures, and difficulties in our multi-year platform modernization program could disrupt operations, expose us to liability, and damage our reputation.
- Our reliance on models, data, artificial intelligence, and third-party providers creates operational and compliance risk.

**Strategy and business environment**

- Intense competition, consolidation among distribution partners, technological disruption, and the cyclical nature of the insurance industry could reduce our market share or profitability.
- Our success depends on attracting and retaining talented employees.
- Acquisitions, dispositions, and strategic initiatives may not achieve their intended benefits.

**Our common stock**

- Insurance holding company laws and provisions of our governing documents may discourage takeover attempts.
- Our ability to pay dividends and repurchase shares is not assured.

### Risks Related to Our Insurance Operations

***Catastrophe events could cause losses that are material to our results of operations, financial condition, and liquidity.***

Our property and casualty businesses expose us to claims arising from catastrophes, both natural and man-made. Natural catastrophes include:

- severe convective storms, including hail, tornadoes, and straight-line winds;
- hurricanes and tropical storms, including associated storm surge and flooding;
- wildfires;
- winter storms and freeze events; and
- earthquakes and related fire following.

Man-made catastrophes include terrorism, riots and civil unrest, industrial accidents, explosions, and large-scale infrastructure failures. Catastrophes can also produce losses in other lines, including workers' compensation, commercial automobile, inland and ocean marine, group life, group disability, and individual life insurance.

The incidence and severity of catastrophes are inherently unpredictable. A single event, or a series of events within a short period, could produce losses that substantially exceed our expectations. In recent years, severe convective storms have become a particularly significant and volatile source of industry losses. These storms occur frequently, affect broad geographic areas, and often fall below the attachment points of catastrophe reinsurance programs. A large number of moderate events in a single year could therefore produce significant aggregate losses retained for our own account.

Our catastrophe losses are also affected by factors that may be beyond our control or difficult to predict:

- growth in the number and value of insured properties in areas exposed to catastrophes, including coastal regions and the wildland-urban interface;
- inflation in construction, labor, and materials costs, which may be accelerated after a major event by demand surge;
- changes in building codes and their enforcement;
- the effects of local, state, and federal legislation or regulatory action that expands coverage, extends claim reporting periods, or restricts the application of policy terms after an event; and
- litigation over coverage, including the application of exclusions, deductibles, and concurrent causation provisions.

We seek to manage catastrophe exposure through underwriting, pricing, deductible structures, aggregation management, and reinsurance. These measures may not be effective. As described in Item 1, during the fiscal 2023 reinsurance renewal cycle we accepted a higher retention in certain layers of our property catastrophe reinsurance program in order to manage cost. A higher retention means that more of our catastrophe losses will be borne by us before reinsurance applies, increasing the potential volatility of our results. In addition, losses from an event may exceed the limits of our reinsurance, a reinsurer may dispute or fail to pay its share, or coverage may be exhausted by earlier events in the same period.

Catastrophe losses could materially reduce our earnings in any period. They could also reduce the statutory capital of our insurance subsidiaries and their ability to pay dividends, require us to sell investments at unfavorable prices to meet claim payments, adversely affect our financial strength and credit ratings, and increase the cost or reduce the availability of reinsurance in subsequent periods.

***We rely on catastrophe models that have significant limitations, and our actual catastrophe losses may differ materially from our modeled estimates.***

We use third-party catastrophe models, adjusted by our own analysis, to estimate potential losses at various probability levels, to price property business, to manage exposure concentrations, and to structure our reinsurance program. These models rely on assumptions about the frequency and severity of events, the vulnerability of insured structures, and the accuracy and completeness of exposure data.

Catastrophe models are periodically revised, sometimes significantly, in response to new scientific information and observed losses. Model revisions may increase our estimated exposure, require additional capital or reinsurance, or render prior pricing inadequate. Models may not adequately capture certain perils, such as severe convective storms, wildfire, and flood, or secondary effects such as demand surge, business interruption, contingent business interruption, and the behavior of courts and regulators after an event. Exposure data may be incomplete or inaccurate, particularly for business written through delegated authority or for commercial accounts with many locations.

Rating agencies and regulators also use catastrophe models in assessing our capital adequacy. Changes in the models they use could adversely affect their assessment of our capital position even if our underlying exposure is unchanged.

***Climate change may adversely affect our business, results of operations, and financial condition.***

Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of weather-related catastrophes and change their geographic distribution. Examples include more intense hurricanes and associated rainfall, higher sea levels and storm surge, longer and more severe wildfire seasons, more frequent heat waves and droughts, and changes in the patterns of severe convective storms and winter weather. Historical loss experience, which is a foundation of our pricing, reserving, and catastrophe modeling, may become a less reliable predictor of future losses.

Climate change may also affect us in other ways:

- **Underwriting and pricing.** If we are unable to obtain adequate rates for property exposures in areas of increasing risk, or if we reduce writings in those areas, our premiums, profitability, or market position could suffer.
- **Regulatory and political environment.** Concerns about the availability and affordability of property insurance in catastrophe-exposed areas have led some regulators and legislatures to restrict non-renewals, rate increases, and the use of certain catastrophe models or rating factors. They may also mandate participation in residual market mechanisms that can generate assessments.
- **Investments.** Physical risks may impair the value of real estate, commercial mortgage loans, municipal bonds, and securities of issuers in affected regions or industries. Transition risks associated with the shift to a lower-carbon economy, including changes in policy, technology, and consumer preferences, may impair the value of investments in carbon-intensive industries.
- **Liability exposures.** Litigation alleging that companies contributed to, failed to disclose, or failed to adapt to climate change could produce claims under liability and management liability policies.
- **Disclosure and supervisory expectations.** Our New York-domiciled insurers are subject to NYDFS expectations for managing climate-related financial risk, and climate-related disclosure requirements have been adopted or proposed by other authorities. Compliance may increase our costs, and any perceived shortcomings may expose us to regulatory or reputational consequences.

The effects of climate change are uncertain and may emerge gradually or abruptly. We may not be able to anticipate or adapt to them in a timely or effective manner.

***Our property and casualty loss and loss adjustment expense reserves may prove inadequate, which could materially reduce our earnings and capital.***

We establish reserves for the estimated ultimate cost of claims that have occurred, whether or not reported, together with the expenses of adjusting and settling those claims. Reserves are estimates and do not represent an exact calculation of liability. They depend on actuarial methods and management judgment about many factors, including:

- claim frequency and severity trends;
- inflation in medical costs, wages, vehicle and property repair costs, and legal costs;
- the timing of claim reporting and settlement;
- changes in our claims handling practices, including changes associated with new technology and systems;
- judicial decisions, legislative changes, and jury attitudes; and
- the emergence of new types of claims or new theories of liability.

Estimation is particularly difficult for long-tail lines, such as workers' compensation, general liability, excess casualty, professional liability, and commercial automobile liability. In these lines, claims may be reported and settled many years after the policy period. Lines in which we have limited historical experience, including cyber insurance, newer specialty products, and business written through new programs or channels, also present heightened uncertainty.

We regularly review our reserves and recognize changes in estimates in the period in which they are identified. Increases in reserves for prior accident years reduce earnings in the period recognized, could be significant, and could adversely affect our financial strength and credit ratings. Reserve increases could also cause rating agencies, regulators, or investors to question the adequacy of our current reserves or pricing. Adverse development in one line or accident year may indicate trends that affect other lines or accident years.

Because insurance liabilities represent a substantial portion of our balance sheet, even small percentage changes in reserve estimates can have a large effect on our equity. As of the end of fiscal 2023, our total liabilities were $480.5 billion, compared with total stockholders' equity of $54.9 billion. An unanticipated increase equal to 1% of total liabilities would amount to approximately $4.8 billion, or approximately 8.8% of total stockholders' equity, before tax effects and any reinsurance recoveries.

***Our reserves for asbestos, environmental, and other legacy exposures in our runoff operations are subject to significant uncertainty.***

Our runoff operations include liabilities for asbestos and environmental claims arising from general liability policies written in prior decades, as well as other legacy casualty exposures. Estimating these liabilities is substantially more difficult than estimating reserves for most other lines, for several reasons:

- Asbestos-related diseases have long latency periods, and the number and identity of future claimants are uncertain.
- Legal issues regarding coverage triggers, allocation of losses across policy periods and insurers, the number of occurrences, and the applicability of aggregate limits remain unresolved in many jurisdictions.
- Bankruptcies of asbestos defendants and the operation of related trusts may shift claims to remaining solvent defendants and their insurers.
- Environmental remediation standards, cleanup technologies, and legal theories continue to evolve, and newly identified contaminants may become the subject of regulatory action and litigation.
- Plaintiffs continue to pursue legacy claims under new theories, including claims alleging abuse or other harms in prior decades, sometimes facilitated by legislation that revives claims previously barred by statutes of limitation.

Actual liabilities for these exposures could differ materially from our estimates. We could be required to increase our reserves in future periods by amounts that are material to our results of operations and financial condition.

***Social inflation and adverse developments in the legal environment could increase our claim costs beyond our expectations.***

Casualty claim costs across the industry have increased at rates exceeding general economic inflation in recent years. The industry broadly attributes this to a set of factors referred to as social inflation, including:

- increased attorney involvement in claims, including in lower-severity automobile and workers' compensation claims;
- larger jury verdicts, including so-called nuclear verdicts, and higher settlement values;
- third-party litigation funding, which may increase the number, duration, and cost of lawsuits;
- aggressive plaintiff advertising and claim-generation tactics;
- expanded theories of liability and erosion of tort defenses; and
- legislative actions that extend or revive statutes of limitation, broaden damages, or limit defenses.

These trends particularly affect commercial automobile, general liability, excess casualty, umbrella, professional liability, and directors and officers liability. They affect both pricing for new business and reserves for prior accident years. Social inflation is difficult to quantify and may vary significantly by jurisdiction. Rate increases and claims management practices we implement may not keep pace with these trends. Legal reforms that we support may not be enacted, may be challenged, or may not have their intended effect.

***Economic inflation could increase our claim costs and expenses faster than we can adjust pricing.***

Inflation affects the cost of the claims we pay, including:

- vehicle repair costs, replacement parts, and used vehicle values;
- construction materials, labor, and contractor costs;
- medical services and pharmaceuticals; and
- wages, which affect workers' compensation indemnity benefits and disability benefit payments.

Our pricing reflects assumptions about future inflation. Our reserves for unpaid claims are sensitive to inflation that occurs between the time a loss occurs and the time the claim is paid. If inflation exceeds our assumptions, our pricing may prove inadequate and our reserves may need to be increased.

Inflation also increases our operating expenses, including compensation, technology, and real estate costs. In personal lines and in other lines subject to prior approval rate regulation, we may be unable to obtain timely rate increases to offset rising costs. Elevated inflation may also lead to higher interest rates, with the effects on our investment portfolio and insurance liabilities discussed below.

***Rate regulation and other restrictions may prevent us from charging adequate prices or adjusting our exposure in a timely manner.***

Our admitted insurers must file rates, rating rules, and policy forms with state regulators, and many states require prior approval, particularly for personal automobile and homeowners insurance. Regulators may delay, reduce, or deny requested rate increases, or may restrict the use of particular rating factors, data sources, or catastrophe models. The resulting lag between changes in loss trends and approved rate changes can be significant, particularly in periods of rapid inflation or catastrophe activity.

Several states have also adopted or considered laws and regulations that:

- restrict insurers' ability to cancel, non-renew, or withdraw from lines of business or geographic areas;
- impose moratoria on non-renewals following catastrophes;
- require insurers to offer specific coverages or discounts; or
- limit the circumstances in which insurers may use credit-based insurance scores or other underwriting variables.

These restrictions could prevent us from achieving adequate returns, reducing exposures in areas of elevated risk, or reallocating capital to more profitable opportunities. As described in Item 1, we have restricted new business writings and adjusted renewal criteria in certain states and coastal areas. Such actions may provoke regulatory or political responses, harm relationships with distribution partners, or damage our reputation.

***Our pricing, underwriting, and risk selection depend on assumptions, data, and models that may prove incorrect.***

We price our products based on estimates of future losses, expenses, the cost of reinsurance, investment income, and the cost of capital. Those estimates rely on historical experience, actuarial and predictive models, third-party data, and underwriter judgment. Our estimates may be wrong because:

- past experience may not predict future results;
- data may be incomplete, inaccurate, or not representative of the risks we write;
- our models may contain errors or may not capture changing conditions;
- competitors may adversely select against us by using more accurate pricing or by targeting risks we misprice; or
- distribution partners with delegated authority may not adhere to our guidelines.

Our strategy emphasizes the use of granular rating plans, predictive analytics, and automated underwriting, particularly in personal lines and small commercial. Automated processes can replicate a pricing or underwriting error across many policies before it is detected. Accelerated underwriting programs in our individual life business rely on electronic data in place of traditional medical examinations. If these programs do not identify risks as effectively as traditional underwriting, our mortality experience could be worse than expected.

If our pricing proves inadequate, our results of operations could suffer, and we may be unable to correct the problem quickly because of policy terms, multi-year rate guarantees, or regulatory constraints.

***Actual mortality, morbidity, and longevity experience may differ from our assumptions.***

Our group benefits, individual life, annuity, and runoff long-term care businesses depend on assumptions about mortality, morbidity, and longevity. These assumptions underlie our pricing, our liabilities for future policy benefits, and our disability claim reserves.

- **Mortality.** Higher-than-expected mortality would increase claims under group life, individual term, universal life, and indexed universal life policies. Mortality may be adversely affected by pandemics, the opioid crisis and other substance abuse trends, deteriorating population health, and catastrophic events.
- **Longevity.** Lower-than-expected mortality would increase the cost of payout annuities, deferred income annuities, and guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefits. Advances in medicine may extend lifespans beyond our assumptions.
- **Morbidity.** Disability results depend on claim incidence and on recovery and termination rates. Supplemental health, stop-loss, and long-term care results depend on the frequency and cost of covered health events and services. Stop-loss results have been affected by the rising cost of high-cost specialty pharmaceuticals and advanced therapies.

Under current accounting standards for long-duration contracts, we review cash flow assumptions at least annually and recognize the effects of assumption changes in our results. Unfavorable changes could materially reduce our earnings and equity in the period of change. Group benefits products are often priced under multi-year rate guarantees, which may prevent us from responding promptly to adverse experience.

***Policyholder behavior that differs from our expectations could adversely affect our results.***

The profitability of our life and retirement products depends significantly on policyholder behavior, including:

- lapse and persistency rates on life insurance policies;
- surrender and withdrawal rates on deferred annuities;
- premium payment patterns on flexible-premium universal life policies; and
- utilization of guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefits and other optional benefits.

Policyholder behavior is difficult to predict and may change in response to interest rates, equity market performance, economic conditions, tax law changes, product innovation by competitors, and the advice of financial professionals.

As described in Item 1, the higher interest rate environment in fiscal 2023 increased the risk that holders of in-force annuities written at lower crediting rates would surrender their contracts for higher-yielding alternatives. Elevated surrenders could require us to sell investments at a loss to fund withdrawals, reduce future fee and spread income, and accelerate the recognition of deferred acquisition costs. Conversely, lower-than-expected lapse rates on certain life insurance policies, particularly those for which pricing assumed lapses, could increase our costs. Increased utilization of guaranteed benefits during periods of market stress could increase our liabilities and hedging costs.

***Our closed block of long-term care insurance exposes us to significant uncertainty.***

Our runoff operations include a closed block of long-term care insurance. The liabilities for this business depend on assumptions extending over very long periods about:

- morbidity, including the incidence, duration, and cost of care;
- mortality and the length of time policyholders remain on claim;
- policy persistency;
- interest rates and investment returns; and
- our ability to obtain premium rate increases.

Industry experience with long-term care insurance has frequently been worse than originally assumed, particularly with respect to persistency, claim duration, and the cost of care. Our ability to mitigate adverse experience depends significantly on regulatory approval of premium rate increases. These approvals are uncertain, may be granted in amounts lower than requested or phased in over time, and vary across states. Rate increases may also prompt policyholder complaints, litigation, and reputational harm.

Developments in the cost and delivery of care, including labor shortages among caregivers and changes in the use of facility-based and home-based care, could increase our costs. Adverse changes in assumptions for this block could require us to increase reserves materially and could adversely affect the statutory capital of the subsidiaries that hold the business.

***A pandemic or other widespread health crisis could adversely affect multiple aspects of our business simultaneously.***

A pandemic could increase claims under group life, individual life, group disability, supplemental health, stop-loss, and long-term care coverages. It could also produce claims under property and casualty coverages, including business interruption, event cancellation, workers' compensation, and directors and officers liability, together with litigation regarding the scope of coverage and exclusions. Legislatures or regulators could attempt to mandate retroactive coverage or extend grace periods, deadlines, or other policyholder protections.

A pandemic could also disrupt financial markets and reduce the value of our investments, reduce economic activity and the payroll and sales exposures on which many of our premiums are based, increase credit losses, interrupt our operations and those of our vendors and distribution partners, and impair our ability to sell new business.

***Terrorism, war, geopolitical conflict, and civil unrest could cause substantial losses.***

We have exposure to losses from terrorism in our commercial property, workers' compensation, and other lines. Workers' compensation coverage generally cannot exclude terrorism, and state laws often prohibit or restrict terrorism exclusions in other lines. Concentrations of employees in individual buildings or locations, particularly in major metropolitan areas, create the potential for large workers' compensation and group life losses from a single event.

The federal Terrorism Risk Insurance Program shares losses from certified acts of terrorism above specified deductibles and thresholds. Our deductible under the program is substantial, the program does not cover all forms of terrorism, and it is scheduled to expire unless further extended by Congress. If the program expires or is modified to reduce federal participation, our net exposure to terrorism losses could increase significantly, and terrorism reinsurance may be unavailable or available only at a high cost.

War, armed conflict, and geopolitical tensions could produce claims under marine, cargo, political risk, cyber, and other coverages. They could also give rise to disputes over the application of war exclusions, disrupt financial markets, and contribute to inflation and supply chain disruption. Civil unrest could produce significant property losses.

***Our cyber insurance business exposes us to the potential for large, correlated losses.***

We write cyber insurance covering first-party losses and third-party liability arising from data breaches, network security failures, ransomware, and similar events. Cyber risk is rapidly evolving and has limited historical loss data, which makes pricing and reserving difficult.

A single event could affect many of our insureds at once. Examples include:

- a vulnerability in widely used software;
- a successful attack on a cloud service provider, managed service provider, or other critical vendor; and
- a coordinated campaign, including one sponsored or supported by a nation-state.

Such an event could produce aggregate losses that exceed our modeled expectations and our reinsurance protection. Cyber aggregation models are relatively immature, and the market for cyber reinsurance and alternative capital may not provide adequate protection on acceptable terms.

The application of war and infrastructure exclusions to state-sponsored cyberattacks is uncertain and may be the subject of litigation. Cyber events may also produce losses under coverages not specifically designed to cover cyber risk, sometimes referred to as silent or non-affirmative cyber exposure.

***Business written through managing general agents, program administrators, and other parties with delegated authority exposes us to additional risks.***

Our Programs unit, and to a lesser extent other units, rely on managing general agents, program administrators, and other third parties that have authority to underwrite, bind coverage, issue policies, and in some cases handle claims on our behalf. We depend on these parties to adhere to our underwriting guidelines and authority limits, to price and select risks appropriately, to provide accurate and timely data, to handle claims properly, and to remit premiums.

Despite our oversight and audit procedures, a delegated authority partner could exceed its authority, write business outside our appetite, misreport exposures or losses, mishandle claims, or misappropriate funds. We may not detect such conduct promptly. We could be bound by policies issued in excess of authority and could incur regulatory penalties or reputational harm for the partner's conduct. Exiting a program can be costly and can produce adverse loss development as the program runs off.

***We are exposed to credit risk in our loss-sensitive programs, surety business, and premium receivables.***

In our National Accounts business, we write large deductible, retrospectively rated, and fronted programs. Under these programs, policyholders reimburse us for losses within deductibles or pay additional premiums based on their loss experience. We remain obligated to pay claims even if a policyholder fails to reimburse us. Although we generally require collateral, it may be insufficient, may decline in value, or may be difficult to access, particularly if the policyholder enters bankruptcy. Our captive and fronting arrangements create similar exposure to the credit of captive insurers and reinsurers.

In our surety business, we generally expect to be indemnified by principals for losses we pay. An economic downturn, rising interest rates, or industry-specific stress, particularly in the construction sector, could increase contractor defaults while simultaneously impairing the ability of principals and indemnitors to reimburse us. Individual surety losses can be large and are difficult to predict.

We are also exposed to the credit risk of policyholders, agents, and brokers for premiums receivable.

***Economic conditions could adversely affect our group benefits business.***

Group benefits premiums depend significantly on employment levels and wage growth at our customers. An economic downturn could reduce covered lives and payroll, reduce sales of voluntary benefits, and increase the rate at which employers terminate or fail to renew coverage.

Economic conditions can also affect disability claim experience. Claim incidence has historically tended to rise, and recovery rates to decline, during periods of high unemployment. The growing number and complexity of state paid family and medical leave programs create opportunities for our absence management business. They also require significant investment in compliance and administration and expose us to the risk of administrative errors and related penalties. Federal disability claims regulations under ERISA impose procedural requirements, and changes in those requirements could increase our costs and litigation exposure.

### Risks Related to Reinsurance and Counterparties

***Reinsurance may not be available or affordable, or may not adequately protect us against losses.***

We use reinsurance to limit our exposure to large individual losses and catastrophe accumulations, reduce earnings volatility, manage capital, and support underwriting capacity. The availability, cost, and terms of reinsurance depend on market conditions outside our control, including the capital of reinsurers and alternative capital providers, industry loss experience, inflation, model revisions, and investor appetite for insurance-linked securities.

As described in Item 1, property catastrophe reinsurance market conditions were particularly difficult in the fiscal 2023 renewal cycle. Reinsurers increased pricing, raised attachment points, and tightened terms. If these conditions persist or worsen, we may be unable to obtain the protection we seek at acceptable cost. As a result, we may be required to:

- retain more risk, increasing the volatility of our results and our capital requirements;
- reduce writings in exposed lines and geographic areas;
- accept coverage with narrower terms, lower limits, or broader exclusions; or
- raise prices to policyholders, which may not be achievable because of competition or rate regulation.

Our reinsurance contracts may not respond to losses as we expect. Disputes regarding the interpretation of reinsurance contracts, including event definitions, hours clauses, aggregation provisions, and exclusions, could reduce our recoveries or delay payment. Collateralized protection obtained through the insurance-linked securities market is subject to the terms of the underlying instruments, which may differ from traditional reinsurance and may result in basis risk.

***Reinsurers and other counterparties may fail to pay amounts owed to us.***

Reinsurance does not relieve us of our primary obligations to policyholders. If a reinsurer is unable or unwilling to pay amounts owed to us, we remain liable for the underlying claims. Reinsurers' financial condition may deteriorate as a result of catastrophe losses, investment losses, reserve deficiencies, or other factors, particularly following a major event that affects many reinsurers at once. Our reinsurance recoverables related to long-tail lines and our runoff operations may remain outstanding for many years, increasing our exposure to changes in reinsurer credit quality over time.

We are also exposed to the credit risk of other counterparties, including:

- derivative counterparties and central clearinghouses;
- issuers of securities and borrowers on loans in our investment portfolio;
- banks that provide credit facilities and letters of credit; and
- the federal government and other parties to arrangements such as the National Flood Insurance Program's "Write Your Own" program.

Collateral and other credit protections may prove insufficient. The failure of a significant counterparty could materially affect our results and financial condition.

***We are subject to assessments and mandatory participation in residual markets, which could adversely affect our results.***

As a licensed insurer, we are subject to assessments by state insurance guaranty associations to pay certain claims of insolvent insurers. Assessments are based on our premium writings in the relevant states and lines. They cannot be reasonably predicted and could be significant if a large insurer, or a number of insurers, become insolvent, for example following a severe catastrophe or a period of reserve deficiencies or investment losses in the industry.

We are also required to participate in residual market mechanisms, including automobile assigned risk plans, workers' compensation pools, FAIR plans, and coastal windstorm pools. These mechanisms frequently operate at a loss and may grow when voluntary market capacity contracts. A major catastrophe affecting a state property insurance pool could produce significant assessments on participating insurers, including us. Our ability to recover assessments through policyholder surcharges or premium tax offsets varies by state and may be limited or delayed.

### Risks Related to Our Investments and Financial Markets

***Adverse changes in the value of our investments could have a disproportionate effect on our stockholders' equity.***

Like other insurers, we hold substantial assets to support our obligations to policyholders and maintain a capital structure in which liabilities greatly exceed equity. As of the end of fiscal 2023, we had:

- total assets of $535.4 billion;
- total liabilities of $480.5 billion, representing approximately 89.8% of total assets; and
- total stockholders' equity of $54.9 billion, representing approximately 10.2% of total assets.

Our ratio of total liabilities to total stockholders' equity was approximately 8.8 to 1, and our ratio of total assets to total stockholders' equity was approximately 9.8 to 1.

Because of this structure, relatively modest declines in the value of our assets, if not offset by corresponding changes in the measurement of our liabilities, could have a large effect on our equity. For illustration only, a hypothetical decline of 1% in the carrying value of our total assets would equal approximately $5.4 billion. That is approximately 9.8% of our total stockholders' equity, before tax effects. Declines in asset values could result from rising interest rates, widening credit spreads, defaults and impairments, declines in real estate or equity values, or illiquidity in markets for the assets we hold.

Significant reductions in equity could adversely affect our financial strength and credit ratings, the statutory capital of our insurance subsidiaries, our ability to pay dividends and repurchase shares, our compliance with financial covenants, and the trading price of our securities.

***Changes in interest rates could adversely affect our investment results, insurance liabilities, sales, and profitability.***

Interest rates affect us in many ways that may not offset one another.

**Rising interest rates**, such as those that prevailed during fiscal 2023, generally:

- reduce the fair value of fixed maturity securities purchased at lower yields, resulting in unrealized losses that reduce stockholders' equity through accumulated other comprehensive income;
- increase the risk of annuity surrenders and policy loans, which may require us to sell securities at a loss to fund withdrawals;
- increase competitive pressure on crediting rates for in-force annuities and universal life policies;
- increase our borrowing costs; and
- may reduce the value of commercial real estate and the ability of borrowers to refinance.

**Declining interest rates** generally:

- reduce the yields available on new investments and on the reinvestment of maturing and prepaid securities, which reduces net investment income over time;
- compress spreads on products with minimum guaranteed crediting rates;
- increase the value of liabilities for future policy benefits and disability claim reserves, which may increase statutory reserve requirements;
- increase the cost of guaranteed benefits and hedging programs; and
- may accelerate prepayments on mortgage-backed securities and callable bonds.

Under current accounting standards, the discount rate used to measure liabilities for future policy benefits on traditional long-duration contracts is updated each period based on upper-medium grade fixed income yields. The effect is recognized in other comprehensive income. Changes in these discount rates may not move in tandem with changes in the fair value of the assets supporting those liabilities, creating volatility in stockholders' equity that may not reflect underlying economic changes.

Our asset-liability management and hedging programs may not effectively mitigate interest rate risk, particularly in the event of rapid or unexpected rate movements or changes in the shape of the yield curve. Asset and liability durations may not be well matched, and liability cash flows may differ from our projections because of policyholder behavior.

***We are exposed to credit risk in our investment portfolio, and defaults, downgrades, and widening credit spreads could reduce our income and capital.***

Our portfolio consists predominantly of fixed maturity securities, including corporate bonds, municipal bonds, mortgage-backed and other asset-backed securities, and private placement debt, as well as commercial mortgage loans. Issuers and borrowers may default or experience deteriorating credit quality as a result of economic recession, industry-specific stress, rising interest rates, fiscal stress in state and local governments, or other factors.

Defaults and downgrades could:

- result in realized losses and allowances for credit losses that reduce our earnings;
- reduce net investment income;
- increase the capital we are required to hold under regulatory and rating agency capital models, which assign higher charges to lower-rated assets; and
- reduce the market value of our portfolio.

Widening credit spreads, even without defaults, reduce the fair value of our fixed maturity securities and may reduce our liquidity. Our portfolio may have concentrations of exposure to particular issuers, industries, sectors, or geographic areas that magnify the effects of adverse developments. Structured securities may perform worse than expected because of the behavior of underlying collateral, the structure of the securities, or the actions of servicers and other parties.

***Our commercial mortgage loans and real estate-related investments are exposed to deterioration in commercial real estate markets.***

We invest in commercial mortgage loans, commercial mortgage-backed securities, real estate equity, and real estate funds. As noted in Item 1, commercial real estate markets have been under pressure, particularly for office properties, as a result of changes in workplace occupancy patterns, rising interest rates, and refinancing challenges.

Declines in property values, occupancy, or rental income could impair the ability of borrowers to make payments or refinance at maturity. This could lead to delinquencies, defaults, loan modifications, extensions, foreclosures, and increases in our allowance for credit losses. Foreclosure and the subsequent ownership and sale of real estate may be costly and time consuming, and we may realize less than the carrying value of the loan. Other property types, including retail and certain multifamily markets, may also experience stress. Physical climate risks could affect the value of properties in exposed areas.

***Our investments in private and alternative assets are illiquid and difficult to value, and their returns may be volatile.***

We invest in private placement debt, private equity and private credit funds, real estate, and other alternative investments, particularly in portfolios supporting long-dated life and retirement liabilities. These investments may be difficult to sell at their carrying values, or at all, particularly during periods of market stress when we may need liquidity.

Their values may depend on valuation methodologies that use significant unobservable inputs and management judgment, and reported values may lag changes in market conditions. Returns on private equity and similar funds are often reported on a delayed basis and can fluctuate significantly from period to period, contributing to volatility in net investment income. We have commitments to fund additional capital to certain partnerships, which may be called at times that are inconvenient for us.

Regulators and rating agencies have increased their scrutiny of insurers' investments in complex, structured, and private assets. Changes in their capital treatment or valuation requirements could increase the capital we must hold or limit our ability to invest in these assets.

***Equity market declines and volatility could adversely affect our results.***

Our exposure to equity markets arises from:

- equity securities and alternative investments in our portfolio;
- index-crediting obligations under our indexed universal life and fixed indexed annuity products, which we hedge with options and other derivatives;
- guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefits, which are measured at fair value as market risk benefits and whose value increases when equity markets decline or volatility rises;
- fee income from our investment management business, which depends in part on the value of assets under management; and
- sales of products whose attractiveness depends on market conditions.

Our hedging programs may not fully offset changes in the value of our obligations because of basis risk, model limitations, changes in policyholder behavior, liquidity constraints in derivative markets, or counterparty failure. The cost of hedging may increase significantly in periods of high volatility. Derivative positions also require us to post collateral, which can increase our liquidity needs during market dislocations.

***We could face liquidity constraints that force us to sell assets at unfavorable prices or limit our ability to meet obligations.***

We need liquidity to pay claims, policy benefits, surrenders, withdrawals, operating expenses, interest, and dividends, and to post collateral under derivative, reinsurance, and other arrangements. Liquidity needs can increase suddenly and significantly as a result of:

- a severe catastrophe or series of catastrophes;
- elevated annuity surrenders in a rising interest rate environment;
- collateral calls on derivatives or other contracts following market movements or a ratings downgrade;
- disruptions in the capital and credit markets; or
- the termination of credit facilities or the inability to renew maturing debt.

Our ability to meet these needs depends on cash flows from operations, the liquidity of our investment portfolio, and access to external sources of funding. Some of our assets, including private placements, commercial mortgage loans, and alternative investments, are relatively illiquid. If we are required to sell assets quickly, we may realize losses or be unable to sell at all. Regulatory restrictions on transfers among our subsidiaries may prevent us from deploying liquidity where it is needed. Certain large life insurance groups are subject to a liquidity stress test framework adopted by the NAIC, and regulatory expectations regarding liquidity risk management continue to evolve.

***The determination of allowances for credit losses, impairments, and fair values involves significant judgment and may change.***

We evaluate our investments for credit losses each reporting period and measure many assets at fair value. These determinations require judgment about the likelihood of recovery, the timing and amount of expected cash flows, the creditworthiness of issuers and borrowers, our intent and ability to hold securities, and the appropriate valuation inputs and methods.

As described in Item 1, we have unrealized losses on fixed maturity securities purchased at lower yields, which we generally have the ability and intent to hold until recovery. If our intent or ability to hold these securities changes, for example because of unexpected liquidity needs or a change in investment strategy, we could be required to recognize losses in earnings. Our assessments of credit losses and fair values may prove incorrect, and future allowances or impairments may be material.

### Risks Related to Capital, Ratings, and Our Holding Company Structure

***As a holding company, we depend on dividends and other payments from our subsidiaries, which are subject to regulatory and other restrictions.***

Clearwater Technologies Co. has no significant operations of its own. Its ability to pay interest and principal on its debt, pay dividends to shareholders, repurchase shares, and meet other obligations depends principally on dividends and other permitted payments from its subsidiaries.

State insurance laws limit the dividends our insurance subsidiaries may pay without prior regulatory approval, generally based on statutory surplus and statutory net income or investment income. Regulators may also restrict ordinary dividends if they determine that payment would be harmful to policyholders. Our New York-domiciled insurers are subject to the oversight of the NYDFS, which applies rigorous standards to dividends and affiliate transactions. Catastrophe losses, reserve increases, investment losses, or changes in statutory accounting could reduce our subsidiaries' capacity to pay dividends.

Our subsidiaries may also need to retain capital to maintain their ratings, support growth, or meet rating agency expectations, even if regulatory limits would permit larger distributions. Our non-insurance subsidiaries, including our broker-dealer and investment adviser, are subject to their own regulatory capital requirements.

If our subsidiaries cannot pay sufficient dividends, we may be unable to meet our obligations or maintain shareholder dividends and repurchases, or may be required to raise capital on unfavorable terms. In addition, the claims of holders of our debt and equity securities are structurally subordinated to the claims of policyholders and creditors of our subsidiaries.

***A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could adversely affect our business and financial condition.***

Financial strength ratings are important to our competitive position. Many distribution partners, commercial customers, and benefit plan sponsors consider ratings in selecting insurers, and some impose minimum rating requirements. Credit ratings of Clearwater Technologies Co. affect our cost of and access to capital.

Rating agencies may downgrade our ratings, or place them on review, because of:

- catastrophe or investment losses;
- adverse reserve development;
- declines in capital or increases in leverage;
- changes in our business mix, risk profile, or strategy;
- a significant acquisition or other transaction; or
- changes in rating agency methodologies or capital models.

Rating agencies may also take actions affecting the insurance industry as a whole or specific sectors.

A downgrade could cause us to lose business to competitors with higher ratings, reduce new sales and increase surrenders of annuities and other products, and require us to post additional collateral or terminate arrangements under derivative, reinsurance, and other agreements. A downgrade could also increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to capital markets, impair our ability to act as a fronting carrier or surety, and adversely affect relationships with distribution partners. These effects could reinforce one another and could be difficult to reverse.

***Changes in regulatory or rating agency capital requirements could require us to hold more capital or limit our flexibility.***

Our insurance subsidiaries are subject to risk-based capital requirements, and our insurance group is subject to group capital analysis by our lead state regulator. Rating agencies use their own capital models in assessing our ratings. These requirements and models are periodically revised. Areas of recent or ongoing attention include charges for catastrophe risk, structured and private investments, interest rate risk, and the treatment of affiliated reinsurance.

International standard-setting bodies have developed a global insurance capital standard, and U.S. regulators have developed an aggregation-based approach that they seek to have recognized as comparable. The ultimate effect of these developments on our capital requirements is uncertain.

Changes that increase required capital could require us to raise additional capital, reduce dividends and repurchases, purchase additional reinsurance, change our investment strategy, or reduce or exit certain businesses. Each of these outcomes could reduce our returns. If any of our insurance subsidiaries' capital falls below specified levels, regulators could take escalating corrective actions, up to and including placing the insurer under regulatory control.

***We may be unable to obtain financing on acceptable terms when needed, and our debt agreements impose restrictions.***

We may need additional capital to support our insurance subsidiaries following losses, to fund growth, to refinance maturing debt, or for other purposes. Our access to capital depends on market conditions, our financial performance, our ratings, and investor perceptions of the insurance industry. Disruptions in the capital and credit markets, such as those that have occurred in periods of financial stress, could prevent us from raising capital or force us to do so on unfavorable terms, including through the issuance of equity that dilutes existing shareholders.

Our credit facilities and other debt agreements contain covenants, including financial covenants relating to leverage and net worth, and events of default. Failure to comply could result in the acceleration of our debt and the termination of our credit facilities.

***We may be required to write down goodwill, intangible assets, or deferred tax assets.***

We have recorded goodwill and other intangible assets in connection with acquisitions, as well as deferred tax assets. We test goodwill and indefinite-lived intangibles for impairment at least annually and evaluate other intangibles when circumstances warrant. Declines in the performance of a reporting unit, changes in market conditions, or a sustained decline in our market capitalization could result in impairment charges.

The realization of deferred tax assets depends on our ability to generate sufficient taxable income of the appropriate character in future periods. Significant unrealized investment losses, operating losses, or changes in tax law could cause us to record a valuation allowance. Impairments and valuation allowances would reduce our earnings and equity and could reduce regulatory and rating agency capital.

### Risks Related to Regulation and Legal Proceedings

***We are subject to extensive regulation, and changes in regulation or its interpretation could adversely affect our business.***

Our businesses are subject to extensive regulation by state insurance regulators, the SEC, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, the U.S. Department of Labor, and other authorities. Insurance regulation is designed primarily to protect policyholders and the public, not shareholders or creditors. Regulation governs nearly every aspect of our business, including:

- licensing;
- rates and forms;
- underwriting and claims practices;
- marketing and sales practices;
- capital, reserves, and investments;
- transactions with affiliates;
- dividends; and
- data use and privacy.

Regulators have broad discretion in administering these laws and may interpret them in ways that differ from our interpretation. They conduct periodic financial and market conduct examinations that may result in findings, fines, restitution, or required changes in our practices. We may be unable to obtain or maintain necessary licenses and approvals.

Laws and regulations change frequently, and new requirements may be adopted in response to catastrophe events, consumer complaints, political developments, or industry practices. As described in Item 1, areas of current regulatory focus include:

- the use of artificial intelligence and external consumer data;
- climate-related financial risk;
- property insurance availability and affordability;
- private capital ownership of insurers and the treatment of complex investments;
- cybersecurity; and
- annuity sales practices.

We cannot predict the content or timing of future regulatory changes. Changes could increase our compliance costs, restrict our products or practices, require us to hold more capital, or otherwise adversely affect our results. Although the federal government does not directly regulate most aspects of insurance, federal legislation or regulation could affect our business, and we could become subject to federal supervision if designated by the Financial Stability Oversight Council.

***Regulation of artificial intelligence, predictive models, and external consumer data could limit our ability to execute our strategy and expose us to liability.***

Our strategy emphasizes the use of data, predictive analytics, machine learning, and, in selected applications, generative artificial intelligence in underwriting, pricing, claims, fraud detection, marketing, and customer service. Regulators and legislators are increasingly focused on whether these tools and the data they use produce unfairly discriminatory outcomes, including outcomes that disproportionately affect protected classes even when protected characteristics are not used directly.

Several states and the NAIC have adopted or proposed requirements governing insurers' use of these tools, including:

- written governance and risk management programs;
- documentation, testing, and validation requirements;
- restrictions on particular data sources and rating variables;
- requirements to explain adverse decisions to consumers; and
- obligations to oversee third-party data and model vendors.

These requirements vary across jurisdictions and continue to evolve. Complying with them may increase our costs, slow the deployment of new models, require us to abandon or modify models or data sources that improve the accuracy of our pricing and underwriting, and place us at a competitive disadvantage relative to insurers subject to fewer restrictions.

If regulators, courts, or the public conclude that our use of these tools is unfair, inaccurate, or discriminatory, we could face enforcement actions, litigation, restitution, and reputational harm. Because "Technologies" is part of our name and our identity, any such conclusion could be especially damaging to our brand.

***Privacy and data protection laws impose significant obligations and could restrict our use of data.***

We collect, use, store, and share large volumes of personal information about customers, claimants, insureds' employees, beneficiaries, and our own employees. That information includes financial, health, driving, and location data. We are subject to numerous laws governing the collection, use, disclosure, and protection of this information, including:

- the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act;
- HIPAA;
- the Fair Credit Reporting Act;
- the Telephone Consumer Protection Act;
- state insurance data security laws;
- the NYDFS cybersecurity regulation;
- state data breach notification laws; and
- comprehensive consumer privacy laws enacted by a growing number of states.

These laws are numerous, sometimes inconsistent, and changing rapidly. The scope of exemptions for data subject to existing federal frameworks varies among state laws. Some laws provide private rights of action or statutory damages, which have encouraged class action litigation, including litigation relating to tracking technologies on websites and mobile applications, telephone and text marketing, and biometric data.

Compliance requires significant investment and could restrict our use of data in underwriting, telematics, marketing, and claims. Violations could result in regulatory penalties, litigation, and reputational harm. The amended NYDFS cybersecurity regulation imposes enhanced governance, technical control, certification, and reporting obligations, and failure to meet them could result in significant penalties.

***Litigation and regulatory proceedings could result in significant liabilities and adversely affect our business.***

In the ordinary course of business, we are party to numerous lawsuits, arbitrations, and regulatory proceedings, including:

- claims-related litigation, including disputes over coverage and allegations of bad faith or unfair claims practices, which may seek extracontractual or punitive damages;
- class actions relating to claims handling, rating practices, policy terms, sales practices, cost of insurance charges, premium rate increases on long-term care policies, and the use of data and technology;
- disputes with reinsurers, distribution partners, vendors, and other counterparties;
- employment-related claims; and
- securities and shareholder litigation.

Litigation outcomes are inherently unpredictable, and large verdicts and settlements may exceed our estimates. Courts may interpret policy language in ways we did not anticipate, expanding coverage beyond our intent and affecting large numbers of policies. Regulatory investigations may lead to fines, restitution, and required changes in practices, and may prompt related private litigation.

Accruals for litigation require significant judgment, and actual liabilities could exceed amounts accrued. Even meritless claims can be costly to defend and can divert management attention and harm our reputation.

***Our life, retirement, and investment management businesses are subject to sales practices, suitability, and fiduciary standards that could increase our costs and liability.***

The sale of life insurance and annuities is subject to state suitability and best interest standards. Registered products are also subject to securities regulation. Clearwater Securities LLC is subject to broker-dealer regulation, and Clearwater Investment Management LLC is subject to fiduciary and other obligations under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940. The U.S. Department of Labor regulates persons providing investment advice to retirement plans and individual retirement accounts and has periodically sought to expand the scope of fiduciary obligations.

Changes in these standards could increase our compliance costs, require changes to product design and distributor compensation, reduce sales, and increase our liability for the conduct of distributors, many of whom are not our employees. Allegations of improper sales practices could result in regulatory action, litigation, and reputational harm.

***Changes in accounting standards could adversely affect our reported results and financial condition.***

Our financial statements are prepared in accordance with U.S. generally accepted accounting principles, and our insurance subsidiaries prepare statutory financial statements in accordance with statutory accounting principles. Both are subject to change.

The targeted improvements to the accounting for long-duration insurance contracts significantly changed the measurement of our liabilities for future policy benefits, market risk benefits, and deferred acquisition costs. These changes have increased the sensitivity of our reported results and equity to changes in interest rates, market conditions, and assumptions. Future changes in accounting standards, including changes in statutory reserving requirements such as principle-based reserving, could affect our reported results, capital, dividend capacity, and product pricing, and could require significant changes to our systems and processes.

***Changes in tax laws could adversely affect our results and the attractiveness of our products.***

We are subject to federal, state, and local taxes, including income taxes, premium taxes, and other taxes. Changes in tax laws or their interpretation could increase our tax expense or reduce the value of our deferred tax assets. Potential changes include:

- changes in corporate tax rates;
- application of the corporate alternative minimum tax;
- changes to the deductibility of loss reserves; and
- changes to the taxation of insurance companies generally.

Many of our life insurance and annuity products benefit from favorable tax treatment, including the tax deferral of inside buildup and the exclusion of death benefits from income. Changes that reduce or eliminate these benefits, or that make competing products more attractive, could reduce demand for our products and increase surrenders.

***We are subject to sanctions, anti-money laundering, and anti-corruption laws, and violations could result in significant penalties.***

We are subject to economic sanctions programs administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control, anti-money laundering requirements applicable to our life insurance and annuity operations, and anti-corruption laws, including the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. Sanctions programs change frequently and can apply to coverage of foreign exposures, marine and cargo business, claims payments, and investments.

Although we maintain compliance programs, we may not detect all prohibited transactions or conduct, including conduct by distribution partners or local insurers that issue policies on our behalf in other countries. Violations could result in substantial fines, criminal penalties, and reputational harm.

### Risks Related to Technology, Cybersecurity, and Operations

***Cybersecurity incidents could disrupt our operations, compromise sensitive information, and result in significant liability and reputational harm.***

We depend on information technology systems to conduct nearly every aspect of our business. Our systems, and those of our vendors, distribution partners, and other third parties, are subject to cyberattacks and other security threats, including:

- ransomware and other malware;
- phishing and social engineering;
- credential theft and account takeover;
- denial-of-service attacks;
- exploitation of software vulnerabilities, including previously unknown vulnerabilities;
- supply chain attacks through third-party software and services;
- insider threats; and
- attacks by sophisticated criminal organizations and nation-state actors.

The threat environment is evolving rapidly. Attackers increasingly use artificial intelligence to scale and refine their techniques.

As an insurer that holds large volumes of personal, financial, and health information, and as a provider of cyber insurance, we may be a particularly attractive target. A successful attack could result in:

- disruption of our ability to quote, bind, service policies, and pay claims;
- theft, corruption, or unauthorized disclosure of sensitive data;
- fraudulent payments or diversion of funds;
- regulatory investigations, penalties, and remediation obligations under the NYDFS cybersecurity regulation, state insurance data security laws, HIPAA, and other laws;
- litigation, including class actions;
- costs of investigation, notification, credit monitoring, and system restoration; and
- loss of customer and distribution partner confidence.

Our security measures may not prevent or detect every incident, and we may not discover an incident for an extended period. Many of our employees work in hybrid arrangements, which may increase certain security risks. Our insurance coverage for cyber incidents may be insufficient to cover all losses. Additional information regarding our cybersecurity risk management is presented in Item 1C.

***Failures or disruptions of our information technology systems, or difficulties in our platform modernization program, could adversely affect our business.***

We are executing a multi-year program to replace legacy policy administration, billing, claims, and group benefits and life administration systems with configurable, cloud-enabled platforms. Large-scale system transformations are complex and involve significant execution risk, including:

- delays and cost overruns;
- failure to achieve expected functionality, efficiency, or cost savings;
- data conversion errors that affect policy, billing, claims, or financial records;
- disruptions to service for customers and distribution partners during transitions;
- the need to maintain legacy and new systems in parallel for extended periods; and
- the diversion of management and technical resources.

Errors or disruptions during conversion could affect the accuracy of premium billing, claim payments, statistical reporting, and the data used in pricing and reserving. Changes in claims systems and processes may affect the comparability of historical data used in actuarial analysis, increasing the uncertainty of reserve estimates.

Independent of the modernization program, our systems may fail or be disrupted because of software or hardware failures, power or telecommunications outages, natural disasters, human error, or failures at the cloud and data center providers on which we increasingly depend. Prolonged outages could prevent us from conducting business and could result in regulatory consequences and loss of customers.

***Our reliance on models and data exposes us to model risk.***

We use models extensively in underwriting, pricing, catastrophe management, claims, reserving, asset-liability management, hedging, financial reporting, capital management, and risk management. Models may be flawed because of errors in design, coding, or implementation, inappropriate assumptions, inaccurate or incomplete data, or use outside the purposes for which they were designed. Models calibrated on historical data may not perform well when conditions change.

Our model risk management framework may not identify all model weaknesses, and validation may not keep pace with the volume and complexity of the models we deploy, particularly machine learning models whose behavior may be difficult to interpret. Model failures could lead to mispricing, poor risk selection, inadequate reserves, ineffective hedging, inaccurate financial statements, and regulatory or legal consequences.

***Our use of generative artificial intelligence and other emerging technologies presents new and evolving risks.***

We have begun to deploy generative artificial intelligence tools in controlled applications, including summarizing claim files and medical records, extracting information from underwriting submissions, supporting customer service representatives, and assisting software developers. These tools may produce inaccurate, incomplete, biased, or misleading output. They may also expose confidential or personal information, infringe the intellectual property of others, or introduce security vulnerabilities into our software.

Although we require human oversight and do not permit these tools to make final determinations on coverage, claims, or underwriting decisions without appropriate human review, reviewers may rely excessively on tool output or fail to detect errors. The legal and regulatory framework governing artificial intelligence is developing rapidly and may impose requirements that limit our use of these tools or increase our costs.

Conversely, if we fail to adopt these technologies as effectively as our competitors, we may be at a disadvantage in efficiency, customer experience, and risk selection. We may also not realize the benefits we expect from our investments in these technologies.

***We depend on third parties for important services, and their failure to perform could disrupt our business.***

We rely on third parties for many important functions, including:

- cloud computing and data center services;
- software, including core platforms;
- data used in underwriting and claims;
- catastrophe models;
- claims adjusting, particularly after catastrophes;
- policy administration and claims handling by delegated authority partners;
- investment management for certain asset classes;
- custody;
- payment processing; and
- contact center and other operational support.

Some of these providers are concentrated, so a failure at a single provider could affect many companies, including us and our insureds, at the same time.

If a third party fails to perform, experiences a security incident, ceases operations, or substantially increases its prices, our operations could be disrupted, our data could be compromised, and we could incur additional costs to find alternatives. Regulators hold us responsible for the conduct of our vendors in many respects, and our oversight may not detect deficiencies. The loss of access to certain third-party data sources or models could impair our pricing and underwriting capabilities.

***If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, the accuracy and timeliness of our financial reporting could be affected.***

Our financial reporting depends on complex processes, systems, actuarial estimates, and models, many of which are undergoing change as part of our modernization program. We may identify material weaknesses or significant deficiencies in our internal control over financial reporting. Control deficiencies could result in misstatements in our financial statements, require restatements, cause us to fail to meet reporting obligations, and erode investor confidence. Remediation could be costly and time consuming.

***Catastrophes, pandemics, and other events could disrupt our own operations.***

Events that cause losses to our insureds may also disrupt our own operations, including natural disasters, pandemics, terrorism, and infrastructure failures affecting our offices, technology infrastructure, employees, or vendors. Our principal executive offices are in New York, New York, and we have concentrations of employees and operations in certain locations.

Disruption could impair our ability to respond to claims at precisely the time demand for claims services is greatest. Our business continuity and disaster recovery plans may not be adequate for every scenario, and our ability to rely on remote work could be limited by widespread telecommunications or power disruptions.

### Risks Related to Our Strategy and Business Environment

***We face intense competition, and our failure to compete effectively could reduce our market share and profitability.***

The insurance industry is highly competitive. We compete with a large number of stock and mutual insurers, reciprocal exchanges, risk retention groups, captive insurers, alternative capital providers, and, in certain lines, government programs and self-insurance. Many competitors are larger than we are and have greater financial, marketing, or technological resources, higher ratings, lower costs, or stronger distribution relationships.

Competitive pressures vary by segment:

- In **personal lines**, large national carriers invest heavily in advertising and direct distribution, and digital comparison shopping has increased price transparency.
- In **small commercial**, competition increasingly centers on digital speed and ease of use.
- In **specialty lines**, managing general agents backed by diverse capacity providers have expanded.
- In **group benefits**, broker-driven marketing processes place a premium on competitive pricing.
- In **life and retirement**, insurers affiliated with or capitalized by alternative asset managers compete aggressively on crediting rates.

Competitors may price aggressively to gain share, accept broader terms, or invest more in technology and distribution than we do. Alternative capital may enter markets in which we participate and depress pricing. If we maintain our pricing discipline in the face of competition, we may lose business. If we do not, our profitability may suffer.

***The cyclical nature of the insurance industry could cause our results to fluctuate.***

The property and casualty industry has historically been cyclical. Soft markets, characterized by intense price competition and loosened terms, alternate with hard markets, characterized by rising prices and tightened terms. Cycles are influenced by industry capital levels, catastrophe and loss experience, investment returns, reinsurance costs, and competitor behavior, much of which is beyond our control.

Many lines experienced rate increases during fiscal 2023, and E&S markets benefited from risks migrating from the admitted market. These conditions may not persist. A softening market could reduce our premium volume and margins, particularly if we choose to decline business that does not meet our return thresholds. Rate increases achieved in recent periods may prove insufficient if loss trends continue to accelerate.

***Consolidation among distribution partners and changes in distribution could adversely affect our business.***

We depend on independent agents and brokers, wholesale brokers, managing general agents, employee benefits brokers and consultants, independent marketing organizations, banks, and broker-dealers for most of our business. Large brokerage firms have grown substantially through consolidation, increasing their negotiating leverage over carriers, including with respect to commissions, contingent compensation, service requirements, and data access. Broker consolidation could also concentrate our business among fewer firms and increase the potential effect of the loss of any single relationship.

Some distribution partners own or are affiliated with insurers, managing general agents, or alternative capital vehicles that compete with us. Distribution partners may favor carriers with higher ratings, broader appetites, better technology, or more attractive compensation. Regulatory changes affecting producer compensation or disclosure could require changes to our distribution arrangements. Our direct-to-consumer channel relies on digital marketing and lead-generation platforms whose costs, availability, and rules may change.

***Technological change and new business models could disrupt our industry and our competitive position.***

The insurance industry is being affected by technological change, including:

- advances in data and analytics;
- the growth of embedded insurance distributed at the point of related transactions;
- the entry of technology-focused insurers and distributors;
- vehicle safety technology and the potential long-term development of autonomous vehicles; and
- the use of connected devices to prevent and detect losses.

Some of these developments may reduce the demand for certain products. For example, improvements in vehicle safety could reduce accident frequency and personal automobile premiums over time, and could shift liability from drivers toward manufacturers and technology providers. Others may increase competition or change the economics of distribution.

We are investing in embedded and digital partnerships and in modern platforms, but these investments may not succeed, and our competitors may adapt more quickly or effectively. Partners on digital platforms may develop their own insurance capabilities, demand more favorable economics, or terminate relationships.

***Our success depends on our ability to attract, develop, and retain talented employees.***

Our success depends on the skills and judgment of our employees, including underwriters, actuaries, claims professionals, data scientists, engineers, cybersecurity professionals, investment professionals, and senior management. Competition for talent is intense, particularly for technical roles in which we compete with other financial services and technology companies.

Demographic trends, including the retirement of experienced professionals in underwriting and claims, may make it more difficult to maintain institutional expertise. Our hybrid workplace model may affect our ability to recruit, train, and develop employees, and to maintain our culture, in ways that are difficult to predict. Wage inflation may increase our costs. The loss of key personnel, or failure to plan effectively for succession, could adversely affect our operations and the execution of our strategy.

***Acquisitions, dispositions, and other strategic transactions may not achieve their intended results.***

We evaluate acquisitions, portfolio transfers, reinsurance transactions, and dispositions as part of our capital management strategy. Acquisitions involve risks, including:

- inaccurate assessment of the acquired business's reserves, investments, liabilities, or controls;
- difficulties integrating operations, systems, personnel, and cultures;
- failure to retain key employees, customers, and distribution relationships;
- the diversion of management attention; and
- the need for regulatory approvals that may be delayed or conditioned.

Dispositions and reinsurance transactions to transfer legacy or runoff exposures may result in losses, ongoing indemnification obligations, or continued exposure if counterparties fail to perform. We may not identify transactions on acceptable terms, and our competitors may be willing to pay more for attractive opportunities.

***Damage to our reputation could adversely affect our business.***

Our reputation with customers, distribution partners, regulators, employees, investors, and the public is important to our business. It could be harmed by:

- cybersecurity incidents;
- claims handling practices perceived as unfair, particularly after catastrophes;
- withdrawals from markets or non-renewals in catastrophe-exposed areas;
- premium rate increases, including long-term care rate increases;
- regulatory actions or litigation;
- allegations regarding the use of data or artificial intelligence;
- misconduct by employees or distribution partners; and
- the positions we take, or do not take, on public issues.

Negative publicity can spread rapidly through social media, regardless of its accuracy. Reputational harm could reduce sales and retention, impair relationships with distribution partners and regulators, increase litigation, and make it more difficult to attract talent.

***Divergent stakeholder expectations regarding environmental, social, and governance matters could expose us to additional risks.***

Customers, investors, regulators, employees, and other stakeholders have differing and sometimes conflicting expectations regarding our approach to climate risk, underwriting and investment in particular industries, workforce practices, and other environmental, social, and governance matters. Some jurisdictions have adopted laws that penalize financial institutions for considering certain of these factors, while others encourage or require it.

Our decisions on these matters may be criticized by some stakeholders regardless of the course we choose. This could lead to reputational harm, loss of business, exclusion from certain public sector business, regulatory scrutiny, or litigation. Compliance with evolving disclosure requirements may increase our costs and could expose us to liability if our disclosures are alleged to be inaccurate.

***General economic and political conditions could adversely affect our business.***

Our business is affected by conditions in the U.S. economy and financial markets. An economic downturn could:

### Risks Related to Our Common Stock

***Insurance holding company laws and provisions of our governing documents could delay or prevent a change in control.***

Under the insurance holding company laws of the states in which our insurance subsidiaries are domiciled, including New York, no person may acquire control of Clearwater Technologies Co., and thereby indirectly control our insurance subsidiaries, without the prior approval of the applicable insurance regulators. Control is generally presumed to exist when a person acquires, directly or indirectly, a specified level of our voting securities. These requirements may discourage potential acquisition proposals and may delay, deter, or prevent a change in control, including transactions that some shareholders might consider desirable.

Provisions of our certificate of incorporation and by-laws, and of applicable corporate law, may have similar effects. These may include provisions regarding:

- the issuance of preferred stock without shareholder approval;
- advance notice requirements for shareholder proposals and director nominations; and
- limitations on the ability of shareholders to call special meetings or act by written consent.

These provisions could limit the price that investors are willing to pay for our common stock.

***Our ability to pay dividends and repurchase shares is not assured.***

The payment of dividends and the repurchase of shares are subject to the discretion of our Board of Directors. Board decisions depend on our results of operations, financial condition, capital requirements, rating agency considerations, regulatory restrictions on subsidiary dividends, and other factors. Catastrophe losses, reserve increases, investment losses, or changes in capital requirements could cause us to reduce or suspend dividends or repurchases. Any reduction or suspension could adversely affect the trading price of our common stock.

***The trading price of our common stock may be volatile.***

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors, including:

- catastrophe losses;
- reserve developments;
- changes in interest rates and financial markets;
- ratings actions;
- regulatory developments;
- results that differ from analyst expectations;
- announcements by us or our competitors; and
- general economic and market conditions.

Many of these factors are beyond our control. Broad market and industry fluctuations may adversely affect the trading price of our common stock regardless of our actual operating performance.
