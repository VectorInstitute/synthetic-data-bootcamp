## ITEM 1A. RISK FACTORS

An investment in our securities involves risk. Before making an investment decision, you should carefully consider the risks and uncertainties described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. That information includes Part I, Item 1, "Business"; Part II, Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations"; Part II, Item 7A, "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk"; and our consolidated financial statements and the accompanying notes in Part II, Item 8, "Financial Statements and Supplementary Data."

Any of the risks described below could materially and adversely affect our business, results of operations, financial condition, liquidity, cash flows, financial strength and debt ratings, or the trading price of our common stock and other securities. Additional risks and uncertainties that we do not currently know about, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also impair our business. The risks below are grouped into categories for convenience. Many of them are interrelated, and a single event or trend can give rise to several of them at once. For example, a major catastrophe could cause large losses, raise the cost of reinsurance, force the sale of investments at a loss, and lead to a rating action, all in the same period. The order in which the risks are presented does not indicate their relative likelihood or the magnitude of their potential effect.

This Item 1A contains forward-looking statements. Please read the "Cautionary Note Regarding Forward-Looking Statements" at the end of Part I, Item 1, and the similar discussion in Part II, Item 7.

As of the end of fiscal 2024, we had total assets of approximately $60.0 billion, total liabilities of approximately $47.6 billion, and total stockholders' equity of approximately $12.4 billion. Total liabilities therefore equaled approximately 79.3% of total assets, and the ratio of total liabilities to total stockholders' equity was approximately 3.8 to 1. As with most property and casualty insurers, our liabilities consist largely of estimates of future claim payments, and our assets consist largely of investments whose values change with financial market conditions. Because of this balance sheet structure, relatively small percentage changes in the estimated value of our liabilities or in the fair value of our assets can have a proportionally much larger effect on our stockholders' equity. Several of the risk factors below illustrate this sensitivity.

### Risks Relating to Our Insurance Operations

***Our reserves for losses and loss adjustment expenses are estimates and may prove inadequate, which could materially reduce our earnings and stockholders' equity.***

We establish reserves for the estimated ultimate cost of claims incurred through the balance sheet date, including claims that have not yet been reported to us. Reserves are not a precise measurement of our liability. They reflect management's best estimate at a given point in time, based on actuarial analyses of historical loss experience and on assumptions about future developments. Those assumptions include claim frequency and severity, inflation in medical, repair, construction, and legal costs, the outcome of litigation, judicial interpretations of policy language, changes in the legal and regulatory environment, and the timing of claim reporting and payment. Many of these factors cannot be measured directly. Their effect on ultimate losses may not become apparent for many years, and the actual cost of claims may differ materially from our estimates.

Reserves for losses and loss adjustment expenses are the largest component of our total liabilities of approximately $47.6 billion. Because our stockholders' equity of approximately $12.4 billion is substantially smaller than our total liabilities, even modest percentage changes in our reserve estimates can have a large effect on our financial position. To illustrate this sensitivity: an increase in our liability estimates equal to 1% of total liabilities, or approximately $475.6 million, would reduce stockholders' equity by approximately 3.8% before giving effect to income taxes and reinsurance. An increase equal to 5% of total liabilities, or approximately $2.4 billion, would reduce stockholders' equity by approximately 19.1% on the same basis. These examples are hypothetical and are not predictions of future reserve development. They do show how strongly our financial results depend on the accuracy of our reserve estimates.

Uncertainty in reserve estimates is greatest in our long-tail lines. These include general liability, workers' compensation, commercial automobile liability, umbrella and excess liability, excess and surplus casualty, and professional and management liability. In these lines, years or decades may pass between the occurrence of a loss, its reporting, and its final resolution. During that time, economic conditions, medical treatment patterns, legal doctrines, jury attitudes, legislation, and claimant behavior may change in ways we did not foresee. Recent accident years are especially uncertain because relatively few claims have been reported or settled, so our estimates for those years rely more heavily on judgment and on expected loss ratio assumptions that may prove inaccurate.

Changes in our own operations can also reduce the reliability of historical data as a guide to future development. These include changes in our mix of business, underwriting standards, policy terms and conditions, claims handling practices, case reserving philosophy, claims systems, and the speed with which claims are reported or settled. Our ongoing core systems modernization program and changes in our claims organization may alter historical patterns of case reserving and payment in ways that are difficult to separate from true changes in loss experience. If our actuaries misinterpret such changes, our reserve estimates could be materially misstated.

When we determine that reserves for prior accident years are insufficient, we must increase them and record a charge to earnings in the period in which we identify the deficiency. Such charges can be large, can occur without warning, and can recur across multiple periods as adverse trends emerge. Significant reserve strengthening could reduce our statutory surplus and risk-based capital ratios, lead to adverse rating agency action, restrict our insurance subsidiaries' ability to pay dividends to the holding company, damage our credibility with investors, distribution partners, and regulators, and require us to raise capital on unfavorable terms. Conversely, favorable reserve development that has benefited our results in some prior periods may not continue, and we cannot assure that it will recur.

***Catastrophe events could cause large losses and materially affect our results of operations, financial condition, and liquidity.***

As a property and casualty insurer, we are exposed to losses from natural and man-made catastrophes. These include hurricanes, tropical storms, severe convective storms (including tornadoes, hail, and straight-line winds), winter storms and freezes, earthquakes, wildfires, floods, volcanic activity, terrorism, cyber events, explosions, industrial accidents, civil unrest, and pandemics. Catastrophes can affect many lines of business at once, including commercial property, homeowners, personal and commercial automobile physical damage, inland and ocean marine, workers' compensation, and business interruption coverages. A single event can generate a large number of claims across multiple segments and geographic areas.

The incidence and severity of catastrophes are inherently unpredictable. Our catastrophe losses in any given period could exceed historical averages, our modeled expectations, and the amounts contemplated in our pricing. Their extent depends on the number and value of insured exposures in the affected area, the severity and duration of the event, local building codes and construction quality, demand surge in labor and materials after the event, the speed and effectiveness of local recovery, and the legal environment in which claims are adjusted and litigated. Several moderate events in a short period can be as damaging to our results as a single large event, particularly when the aggregate losses fall below the retentions of our per-occurrence catastrophe reinsurance. In recent years, losses from severe convective storms, wildfires, and secondary perils have become a larger share of industry catastrophe losses. These events are often less well captured by catastrophe models and less effectively protected by traditional catastrophe reinsurance structures.

We manage our catastrophe exposure through underwriting guidelines, geographic diversification, deductibles and policy terms, exposure monitoring against Board-approved risk tolerances, and reinsurance. These measures may not be effective. Our exposure data may be incomplete or inaccurate, particularly for business written through program administrators or on a surplus lines basis. Our reinsurance may be insufficient, may not respond as we expect, or may be exhausted by one or more events. Regulatory restrictions may limit our ability to reduce exposure in high-hazard areas or to charge rates that we believe are adequate for the underlying risk.

Catastrophe losses can also affect our liquidity. A major event could require us to pay large amounts of claims within a short period, before we have collected the related reinsurance recoverables. That could force us to sell investments at unfavorable prices, including at a time when financial markets are themselves disrupted. A severe catastrophe or series of catastrophes could reduce our statutory surplus, lead to rating downgrades, and impair our ability to write new business or to obtain reinsurance on acceptable terms. In addition, catastrophes can trigger assessments by state residual market mechanisms, windstorm pools, and guaranty associations, as discussed below.

***Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of weather-related losses and may adversely affect our business in other ways.***

Changing climate conditions, including rising average temperatures, rising sea levels, changes in precipitation patterns, and changes in the frequency, intensity, and geographic distribution of extreme weather events, may increase the frequency and severity of catastrophe and non-catastrophe weather losses over time. These changes may also raise the uncertainty in our loss estimates. Historical loss experience, on which our pricing and catastrophe models partly depend, may become a less reliable guide to future losses. Continued population growth and property development in coastal, wildland-urban interface, and other hazard-prone areas, together with rising property values and replacement costs, may amplify these effects.

Climate change may affect our business in other ways as well:

- We may be unable to obtain rate increases sufficient to reflect increased weather-related risk, particularly in personal lines and in states with restrictive rate regulation. Regulatory restrictions on cancellation, non-renewal, and market withdrawal may prevent us from reducing exposure as quickly as we would like.
- The cost of property catastrophe reinsurance may continue to rise, and reinsurers may reduce capacity for perils and regions they consider most affected.
- The value of investments in our portfolio, including municipal bonds, commercial mortgage loans, commercial mortgage-backed securities, and corporate securities of issuers in carbon-intensive or climate-exposed industries, may decline as a result of physical climate impacts or the economic transition to a lower-carbon economy.
- Our insureds and others may face litigation alleging contributions to, or failure to disclose or adapt to, climate change. Such litigation could result in claims under liability, directors' and officers', and other policies we have issued.
- Regulators, rating agencies, investors, and other stakeholders have increased their focus on how insurers identify, manage, and disclose climate-related risks. New or divergent disclosure, stress testing, capital, or underwriting requirements could raise our compliance costs, limit our business practices, or expose us to criticism or litigation regardless of the approach we take.

The ultimate effects of climate change on our business are uncertain and difficult to predict, and they may be material.

***Economic inflation and social inflation may cause our loss costs to rise faster than our pricing, reducing our underwriting margins.***

Our premiums are generally set before losses occur, and for long-tail lines often many years before claims are paid. If loss costs rise faster than we anticipated when we priced our policies and established our reserves, our underwriting results will suffer. Economic inflation affects the cost of repairing and replacing buildings, vehicles, and other property, the cost of medical care for injured workers and other claimants, wage replacement benefits under workers' compensation, and the legal and other costs of investigating and defending claims. In recent periods, the cost of construction labor and materials, automobile parts and repairs, and medical services has risen significantly. These increases have affected our property, automobile, and workers' compensation results, and they may continue or accelerate. Tariffs, supply chain disruptions, and labor shortages could add to these pressures.

We are also exposed to what is often called "social inflation." The term describes increases in liability claim costs beyond general economic inflation, driven by factors such as:

- larger jury verdicts and settlements, including so-called nuclear verdicts in excess of $10 million;
- increased attorney involvement in claims and aggressive attorney advertising;
- third-party litigation funding, which can extend litigation and raise settlement demands;
- legal tactics designed to create bad faith or extra-contractual exposure for insurers;
- expanded theories of liability and the erosion of tort reforms;
- legislative changes that extend or revive statutes of limitations, including for claims alleging sexual abuse or molestation; and
- public attitudes toward corporations and insurers.

Social inflation has particularly affected commercial automobile liability, general liability, umbrella and excess liability, and certain professional and management liability lines. These trends are difficult to measure and predict, and their effect on recent accident years may not emerge fully for several years. If economic or social inflation exceeds the assumptions underlying our pricing and reserves, we may experience adverse reserve development, reduced underwriting profitability, and the need for additional rate increases that we may not be able to obtain in competitive markets or from regulators.

***Our pricing, underwriting, and catastrophe models, and the data on which they rely, may not accurately predict future losses.***

We rely heavily on actuarial analyses, predictive models, catastrophe models, and other analytical tools in pricing our products, selecting risks, managing exposures, purchasing reinsurance, establishing reserves, and allocating capital. These tools depend on historical data, assumptions, and judgment. They have inherent limitations. Historical data may not reflect future conditions, particularly in a changing climate, inflationary, or legal environment. Data obtained from distribution partners, policyholders, program administrators, and third-party vendors may be incomplete, inaccurate, or outdated. The assumptions in our models may be wrong, and errors in model design, coding, implementation, or use may go undetected.

Third-party catastrophe models, which we use to estimate potential losses from natural perils, are developed by vendors and incorporate assumptions about event frequency, severity, vulnerability, and demand surge that are subject to significant scientific uncertainty. Model vendors periodically revise their models, sometimes materially changing estimated losses for a given portfolio. Some perils, including cyber events, pandemics, certain man-made events, and some secondary perils, are not well represented in available models.

Our use of predictive analytics, machine learning, and other advanced techniques in our small commercial and personal lines automated underwriting and rating systems, and as decision support in our middle market and specialty operations, exposes us to additional risks. Models may perform worse than expected in practice, may degrade over time as conditions change, or may be susceptible to adverse selection if competitors use more effective tools. If our models cause us to underprice risks, to accept risks we should have declined, or to misjudge our aggregate exposure, our results could suffer materially. Models may also attract regulatory scrutiny or litigation if they are alleged to produce unfairly discriminatory outcomes, as discussed below.

***The property and casualty insurance industry is cyclical, and competitive conditions may reduce our premium volume and underwriting profitability.***

Historically, the property and casualty insurance industry has experienced alternating periods of "soft" markets, characterized by intense price competition, broader coverage terms, and declining margins, and "hard" markets, characterized by rising prices, tighter terms, and improved margins. These cycles are driven by the industry's underwriting results, the availability of capital, investment returns, reinsurance market conditions, and catastrophe and other large loss events. They have historically been difficult to predict. Pricing conditions vary by line of business and geography and can change quickly.

When pricing is inadequate, our strategy of underwriting discipline may lead us to reduce our writings in affected lines, lose accounts to competitors, and accept lower premium volume. That could reduce our ability to cover our fixed expenses and adversely affect our relationships with distribution partners who expect a consistent market. Conversely, if we fail to maintain underwriting discipline in a soft market, we may write business at prices that prove inadequate. Capital that has recently entered the excess and surplus lines market, including newly formed carriers and managing general agencies backed by third-party capital, could increase competition and erode the favorable pricing conditions that have supported growth in that market. Directors' and officers' liability pricing has already been competitive in recent periods.

We compete with a large number of insurers, reinsurers, risk retention groups, captives, and self-insurance programs. Some of these competitors have greater financial, marketing, technological, and management resources than we do, lower costs of capital, lower return expectations, or different strategic objectives. In personal lines, we compete with large national carriers that distribute directly or through captive agents. These carriers have substantial marketing budgets and scale advantages and have historically gained share from carriers that distribute through independent agents. In small commercial lines, competitors that offer digital distribution and rapid quoting may attract business that has historically been placed through our agents. New entrants with technology-focused business models may disrupt established distribution and pricing practices. If we cannot compete effectively on price, coverage, service, technology, and distribution relationships, our premium volume, retention, and profitability could decline.

***We depend on independent agents, brokers, wholesale brokers, and program administrators, and consolidation in insurance distribution could adversely affect our business.***

We distribute substantially all of our products through independent agents, regional and national brokers, wholesale brokers, and program administrators and managing general agents. These distribution partners are not our employees, generally represent multiple insurers, and are free to place business with our competitors. Our success depends on our ability to attract and retain high-quality distribution partners, to remain a preferred market for them, and to compensate them competitively without eroding our margins.

The insurance distribution industry has consolidated significantly. Large national brokerage firms and private equity-backed aggregators have acquired many independent agencies. Although no single distribution partner accounted for a material portion of our gross written premiums in fiscal 2024, certain large national brokerage firms, through their numerous offices and subsidiaries, represent a meaningful share of our commercial and specialty business. Continued consolidation could increase the bargaining power of large distribution partners. They could demand higher commissions, contingent compensation, or other concessions, steer business toward a smaller number of carriers or toward their own facilities and affiliated underwriting capacity, or terminate their relationships with us. An agency we have a strong relationship with could be acquired by a firm with which our relationship is less developed. The loss of, or a significant reduction in business from, one or more of our large distribution partners could adversely affect our premium volume and results.

Producer compensation practices are subject to regulatory scrutiny and evolving disclosure requirements. Changes in the regulation of contingent or supplemental compensation could affect our ability to align producer incentives with our objectives. In addition, distribution partners may act in ways that harm us. They may misrepresent our products, fail to remit premiums they collect, place business outside our appetite, or fail to comply with applicable laws, and we could be held responsible for their conduct in certain circumstances.

***Our use of program administrators and managing general agents with delegated authority exposes us to additional underwriting, claims, and compliance risks.***

In our Specialty Insurance segment, we write certain classes of business through program administrators and managing general agents. These parties market, underwrite, and in some cases administer claims for defined books of business under underwriting guidelines and authority limits that we establish. Although we retain ultimate underwriting authority and conduct due diligence, ongoing monitoring, and periodic audits, we depend on these third parties to underwrite risks consistent with our guidelines, to price business adequately, to report accurate and timely data, to handle claims appropriately, and to comply with applicable laws.

A program administrator may exceed its authority, write business outside our guidelines, misreport exposures or losses, mishandle claims, or misappropriate premiums or claim funds. Our audits may not detect such conduct promptly. Because program business is often concentrated in particular industries or affinity groups, adverse developments affecting a program's target market can produce concentrated losses. Program administrators' compensation often includes contingent or profit-sharing features. These are intended to align incentives, but they may encourage aggressive loss reporting or premium growth that is not in our interest. Terminating an underperforming program may be difficult or costly, may require a run-off period during which we remain exposed, and may harm our relationships with other distribution partners. The program business market has also seen increased regulatory and rating agency attention, as well as notable failures involving delegated authority arrangements across the industry. Those developments could lead to heightened oversight requirements or reputational concerns that affect our program operations.

***Emerging claim and coverage issues, mass torts, and expanded theories of liability could increase our losses in ways we did not anticipate when we wrote our policies.***

As industry practices, legal doctrines, judicial interpretations, and social, economic, technological, and environmental conditions change, unexpected and unintended issues relating to claims and coverage may emerge. These issues may extend coverage beyond our underwriting intent, increase the number or size of claims, or allow claims to be asserted long after the policy period. Courts may interpret policy language, including exclusions, more broadly than we intended or may disregard exclusions on public policy grounds. Legislatures may enact laws that retroactively expand liability or coverage, such as statutes that revive time-barred claims or mandate coverage for losses that policies were not designed to cover.

Examples of emerging issues that could affect our results include, among others:

- claims relating to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances and other chemicals, microplastics, and other substances alleged to cause bodily injury or environmental damage;
- claims alleging sexual abuse or molestation, including claims under revived statutes of limitations;
- claims relating to opioids and other pharmaceutical products;
- claims involving biometric privacy, data collection practices, and the use of tracking technologies;
- claims arising from the development and use of artificial intelligence;
- claims relating to wildfires allegedly caused by utility equipment or other insureds' operations;
- claims arising from climate change litigation or from alleged misrepresentations concerning environmental, social, or governance matters;
- claims relating to traumatic brain injury in sports and recreation;
- disputes over whether property policies cover business interruption losses arising from pandemics, civil authority orders, or other non-physical events; and
- claims relating to products and technologies whose long-term health effects are not yet understood.

The effects of these and other emerging issues are difficult to predict. Because many of our casualty policies were written on an occurrence basis, we may remain exposed to such claims for many years after the policies expire. Losses from emerging issues may not be captured in our historical data, may not be reflected in our reserves, and may not be fully recoverable from reinsurers. They could materially affect our results of operations and financial condition.

***Our run-off asbestos and environmental liabilities are subject to significant uncertainty, and adverse developments could require us to increase reserves.***

Our Corporate and Other category includes reserves for asbestos and environmental claims arising mainly from general liability policies issued many years ago, as well as liabilities from other lines of business we no longer write. Estimating these liabilities is subject to uncertainty beyond that inherent in other lines of business. Reasons include long reporting delays; unresolved coverage issues, such as the allocation of losses across policy periods, the number of occurrences, and the applicability of aggregate limits; the effect of bankruptcy proceedings and trusts established for asbestos claimants; the identification of new defendants and new categories of claimants; changes in medical science and in the epidemiology of asbestos-related diseases; changes in environmental cleanup standards and the identification of new contaminants; and the outcome of coverage litigation.

Traditional actuarial reserving methods are of limited value for these exposures, and our estimates rely heavily on specialized analyses and judgment. We have experienced adverse development on these exposures in the past, and we may experience it again. Commutation and settlement opportunities may not be available on acceptable terms. Legislative or judicial developments could significantly expand our liability. Any such adverse development could require us to increase reserves and record charges against earnings, potentially in significant amounts.

***Our cyber liability business and our exposure to cyber events under other policies could produce large, correlated losses.***

We write cyber liability insurance that covers first-party costs and third-party liabilities arising from data breaches, network security failures, ransomware events, and privacy violations. Cyber risk evolves rapidly as threat actors develop new methods, as businesses adopt new technologies, and as legal and regulatory requirements change. Ransomware attacks, business email compromise, and attacks on widely used software, cloud service providers, and other technology infrastructure could affect a large number of our insureds at once, producing correlated losses across our portfolio. An attack on critical infrastructure, such as the power grid, telecommunications networks, or financial market infrastructure, could cause widespread losses that exceed our expectations.

Cyber exposures may also arise under policies not specifically designed to cover them, often called "silent" or "non-affirmative" cyber exposure. Examples include property policies that may respond to physical damage caused by a cyber event, general liability policies that may respond to bodily injury or property damage claims, crime policies that may respond to funds transfer fraud, and directors' and officers' policies that may respond to claims alleging failure to manage cyber risk. Although we have taken steps to clarify our policy language and manage aggregate exposure, courts may interpret our policies differently than we intend. Cyber catastrophe modeling is still developing and is subject to significant uncertainty, and reinsurance for cyber catastrophe risk may be limited or expensive. A major cyber event could materially affect our results.

***We are exposed to losses from terrorism, and our exposure could increase if the federal terrorism risk insurance program expires or is modified.***

Our commercial property, workers' compensation, and other commercial lines are exposed to losses from acts of terrorism. Federal law requires us to make terrorism coverage available to commercial policyholders, and state workers' compensation laws generally do not permit us to exclude terrorism losses. A major terrorist attack, particularly one involving nuclear, biological, chemical, or radiological weapons, could produce losses that are extremely large and difficult to model. Our concentration of commercial and workers' compensation exposures in urban areas, including in the Northeastern United States, increases our potential exposure.

The federal Terrorism Risk Insurance Program shares with insurers the losses from certified acts of terrorism, but we retain significant exposure through our insurer deductible and co-participation obligations, and losses from events that are not certified are not covered by the program. The program is currently authorized only through a scheduled expiration date. If it is not extended, or is extended with higher deductibles, higher co-participation, a higher program trigger, or other changes that reduce federal participation, our exposure could increase significantly. In that case, we might need to reduce our writings in certain lines or locations, seek additional reinsurance, which may be unavailable or costly, or accept greater retained risk. Private reinsurance for terrorism risk is limited, particularly for nuclear, biological, chemical, and radiological perils.

***Our workers' compensation business is subject to particular risks, including statutory benefit changes, medical cost inflation, and accumulation risk.***

Workers' compensation benefits are prescribed by state statutes and are subject to change by legislatures, regulators, and courts. Changes that expand eligibility, increase benefit levels, lengthen benefit durations, or expand compensable conditions, such as presumptions for certain occupational diseases or mental health conditions, can increase our losses on policies already written. These changes may apply to claims arising from prior periods. Workers' compensation claims can remain open for decades, and medical costs on those claims are subject to inflation, changes in treatment patterns, increasing life expectancy of injured workers, and the cost of pharmaceuticals and new medical technologies.

We are exposed to accumulation risk in workers' compensation because a single event, such as an industrial accident, building collapse, natural catastrophe, terrorist attack, or pandemic, could injure many employees of one or more insureds at the same time. In many states, workers' compensation rates are subject to prior approval or are heavily influenced by rate-making organizations, which may limit our ability to respond quickly to adverse trends. Economic conditions also affect our workers' compensation business. Changes in employment levels and payrolls affect our premium base, and economic downturns can increase claim frequency and duration as injured workers face fewer opportunities to return to work.

***Our surety business exposes us to concentrated losses from the failure of individual principals and to economic downturns.***

Through Highmark Indemnity Company, we write contract and commercial surety bonds. Surety losses tend to be infrequent but can be large, and they are often concentrated in particular principals or industries. A single large contractor's default could produce a significant loss. Surety losses are strongly correlated with economic conditions, particularly conditions in the construction industry, and may increase sharply during recessions, periods of rising interest rates or tight credit, or periods of significant increases in construction material and labor costs. Although we generally have indemnity rights against principals and their owners, and in some cases hold collateral, these rights may be of limited value if the principal and indemnitors are insolvent. Recoveries can take years and involve costly litigation. Our surety underwriting depends on our evaluation of principals' financial statements and other information, which may prove inaccurate or misleading.

***Our personal lines business faces particular risks from regulatory constraints, inflation, catastrophe exposure, and competition.***

Our Personal Insurance segment is subject to especially intensive regulation of rates, underwriting practices, rating variables, cancellation and non-renewal, and claims handling. Regulators in some states have delayed, reduced, or denied rate increases that we believe are actuarially justified. Rate inadequacy in personal automobile and homeowners lines has been a challenge across the industry in recent periods, driven by elevated loss cost inflation and weather losses. If we cannot obtain adequate rates in a timely manner, our personal lines underwriting results could suffer, and we may be forced to restrict new business or withdraw from certain markets, which may itself be restricted by regulation.

Homeowners insurance is heavily exposed to catastrophe losses, including from severe convective storms, winter storms, hurricanes, and wildfires. Some states have imposed moratoriums on cancellations and non-renewals following catastrophe events or in areas exposed to wildfire. Those measures limit our ability to manage exposure. Personal automobile results are affected by the increasing cost and complexity of vehicle repairs, including for vehicles with advanced driver assistance systems and electric vehicles, by trends in driving behavior and distracted driving, and by changes in medical costs and litigation. In the longer term, advances in vehicle safety technology and autonomous vehicles could reduce demand for personal automobile insurance or shift liability from drivers to manufacturers and technology providers, changing the economics of the personal automobile market. Our strategy of serving customers who value independent agent advice may not be successful against direct and captive agent competitors with larger marketing budgets and scale.

***Our excess and surplus lines business is subject to risks relating to its distribution model, regulatory status, and competitive dynamics.***

Highmark Specialty Insurance Company writes business on a non-admitted basis, mainly through wholesale brokers, for risks that standard carriers are unwilling or unable to write. These risks often have unusual characteristics, adverse loss histories, hazardous operations, or significant catastrophe exposure, and they may therefore produce more volatile results than standard business. The growth of the excess and surplus lines market in recent periods has been driven partly by standard carriers tightening underwriting appetite. If standard carriers expand their appetite, if new capital continues to enter the market, or if competition intensifies, business could move back to the admitted market or pricing could soften, reducing our premium volume and margins.

Surplus lines insurers are subject to eligibility requirements, and surplus lines placements are subject to the laws of the insured's home state, including diligent search and premium tax requirements. Changes in these laws, or in the treatment of surplus lines business by regulators, could adversely affect our excess and surplus lines operations. Our dependence on a select group of wholesale brokers exposes us to the risk that consolidation among wholesale firms, or the loss of a key wholesale relationship, could reduce our access to business.

***Geographic and industry concentrations in our business could amplify the effect of regional events or industry downturns.***

Although we write business throughout the United States, we have significant concentrations of business in certain states and regions, including our home state of Connecticut and the broader Northeastern United States. We also have meaningful concentrations in certain industries served by our industry practice groups and specialty operations, including construction, manufacturing, real estate, and transportation. Adverse developments affecting a region where we are concentrated could have a disproportionate effect on our results. Such developments include catastrophe events, unfavorable regulatory or legislative changes, adverse judicial trends, or local economic downturns. Similarly, adverse developments affecting an industry where we are concentrated, such as a construction downturn, a wave of litigation against an industry, or technological disruption, could reduce our premium volume, increase our losses, or both.

***Pandemics and other widespread health events could adversely affect our business.***

A pandemic or other widespread health event could adversely affect our business in several ways. It could increase claims under workers' compensation, event cancellation, directors' and officers' liability, employment practices liability, and other coverages. It could produce litigation seeking to establish coverage for business interruption losses under property policies, regardless of policy language and exclusions. It could reduce premium volume because of lower economic activity, payrolls, and sales. It could disrupt our operations and those of our distribution partners and vendors. It could cause volatility in financial markets that impairs our investment portfolio. Governmental responses, including legislation or regulatory orders requiring insurers to extend premium payment periods, suspend cancellations, or cover losses not contemplated by our policies, could further adversely affect our results. The effects of any future pandemic are inherently difficult to predict.

### Risks Relating to Reinsurance

***Reinsurance may not be available on acceptable terms, which could increase our net exposure, reduce our underwriting capacity, or raise our costs.***

We use reinsurance to limit our exposure to large individual losses and catastrophe events, to reduce volatility in our results, to protect our capital, and to support our underwriting capacity. Most of our principal treaty reinsurance agreements renew annually, and the cost, availability, and terms of reinsurance can vary significantly from year to year. They depend on reinsurers' loss experience, the capital available to the reinsurance industry, investment returns, the perceived risk of particular perils and regions, and other market conditions.

In recent years, property catastrophe reinsurance pricing has risen and terms have tightened across the market. Reinsurers have raised attachment points, reduced coverage for secondary perils and aggregate covers, and narrowed terms. We have responded by adjusting the structure and retentions of our reinsurance program. As a result, a larger share of catastrophe losses may be retained by us, particularly losses from frequent, moderate events. If reinsurance costs continue to rise or capacity contracts further, we may be forced to retain more risk, pay higher reinsurance costs that reduce our margins, reduce our writings in certain lines or regions, or accept terms that are less favorable to us. We may be unable to pass these higher costs on to policyholders, particularly in regulated lines.

A major catastrophe, a series of catastrophes, or a significant disruption in the capital markets could cause reinsurers to withdraw from the market or sharply raise prices, possibly mid-year if we need to purchase reinstatement or replacement coverage after an event. The alternative capital that has become an important source of reinsurance capacity in recent years, including catastrophe bonds, collateralized reinsurance, and sidecars, could withdraw if investors experience losses or find more attractive opportunities elsewhere. Some coverages, such as terrorism involving nuclear, biological, chemical, or radiological weapons, systemic cyber events, and pandemic, may be unavailable or available only in limited amounts at any price.

***We are exposed to the credit risk of our reinsurers, and reinsurance may not fully protect us against losses.***

Reinsurance does not relieve us of our obligations to policyholders. If a reinsurer fails to pay amounts owed to us, whether because of insolvency, financial distress, a dispute over coverage, or an unwillingness to pay, we remain liable for the full amount of covered losses. Our reinsurance recoverables, including amounts related to paid losses and to reserves for unpaid losses, can be substantial and may increase significantly after a major catastrophe or as long-tail casualty losses develop. The financial condition of reinsurers can deteriorate quickly, particularly after large catastrophe losses or adverse developments affecting the reinsurance industry as a whole. A large event that triggers losses across our reinsurance panel could also weaken many of our reinsurers at the same time.

Although our Reinsurance Security Committee approves our reinsurers and monitors their financial condition, and we require collateral from certain reinsurers, these measures may not prevent losses. Collateral may be insufficient or may decline in value. Reinsurers in jurisdictions with reduced collateral requirements may become unable or unwilling to pay. Our allowance for credit losses on reinsurance recoverables may prove inadequate.

We may also have disputes with reinsurers over coverage, contract interpretation, the scope of a covered event, or the allocation of losses. Such disputes may be resolved through arbitration or litigation that is time-consuming and costly, and the outcome may be unfavorable to us. Reinsurance agreements may contain terms, such as sublimits, exclusions, event definitions, hours clauses, and aggregate limits, that limit recovery in ways we did not anticipate. In addition, our long-tail casualty reinsurance recoverables may remain outstanding for many years, during which the reinsurer's financial condition may change materially.

### Risks Relating to Our Investment Portfolio

***Changes in interest rates could reduce the value of our investment portfolio, our investment income, or both.***

Our investment portfolio consists mainly of fixed maturity securities, and invested assets represent a substantial portion of our total assets of approximately $60.0 billion. The fair value of fixed maturity securities generally moves inversely to interest rates. An increase in interest rates generally reduces the fair value of our existing fixed maturity holdings, which, under GAAP, reduces stockholders' equity through accumulated other comprehensive income for securities classified as available for sale. Because our total assets are approximately 4.8 times our stockholders' equity, each 1% decline in the carrying value of our total assets, approximately $599.9 million, would reduce stockholders' equity by approximately 4.8%, absent offsetting changes in liabilities or taxes. Unrealized losses could also limit our flexibility to reposition the portfolio, because selling securities with unrealized losses would convert those losses into realized losses that reduce net income and, for statutory purposes, surplus.

Conversely, a sustained decline in interest rates would reduce the yields available on new investments and on reinvestment of maturing securities and investment income, reducing our net investment income over time. Lower investment income would require us to achieve better underwriting results to earn the same returns on capital, which may not be possible in competitive markets. Declining rates could also increase prepayments on mortgage-backed and other callable securities, forcing us to reinvest at lower yields.

We seek to manage the duration of our portfolio relative to the expected payout pattern of our liabilities. However, our estimates of claim payment patterns may prove inaccurate, particularly following a large catastrophe or a change in claim settlement trends. If we need to sell securities to fund unexpected claim payments during a period of rising rates, we could realize losses. Interest rates are affected by many factors beyond our control, including monetary policy, inflation, fiscal policy, economic conditions, and domestic and international political developments.

***We are exposed to credit risk in our investment portfolio, and defaults, downgrades, or widening credit spreads could reduce the value of our investments.***

Our fixed maturity portfolio includes corporate bonds, municipal bonds, residential and commercial mortgage-backed securities, asset-backed securities, and other securities subject to credit risk. Issuers may default on principal and interest payments, or their credit ratings may be downgraded. Credit spreads may widen because of economic conditions, sector-specific developments, or changes in investor risk appetite, reducing the fair value of our holdings even in the absence of defaults. Economic downturns, rising interest rates, and inflation could increase defaults and downgrades, particularly among lower-rated issuers and in sectors under stress.

Our municipal bond holdings are exposed to the fiscal condition of state and local governments and other municipal issuers, which may be adversely affected by economic downturns, declines in tax revenues, pension funding obligations, natural catastrophes, and climate-related risks. Our commercial mortgage loans and commercial mortgage-backed securities are exposed to the commercial real estate market. Certain property types, including office properties affected by the shift toward remote and hybrid work, have experienced declining occupancy and valuations. Such declines could produce losses on loans and securities collateralized by these properties. Concentrations in particular issuers, sectors, or regions could amplify losses.

We record an allowance for credit losses on certain investments and recognize impairments when the fair value of a security has declined and we determine that we intend to sell it or will likely be required to sell it before recovery. These determinations require significant judgment. Additional impairments and increases in our allowance for credit losses may be required in future periods, and these could materially reduce our net income and stockholders' equity. Credit downgrades could also increase the capital charges applied to our investments under risk-based capital and rating agency capital models, even if the securities do not default.

***Our investments in equity securities, limited partnerships, private credit, and other alternative assets expose us to additional volatility, valuation uncertainty, and illiquidity.***

In addition to our fixed maturity portfolio, we hold publicly traded equity securities, limited partnership interests in private equity and private credit funds, and commercial mortgage loans. Changes in the fair value of equity securities are recognized in net income under GAAP, which can make our reported results more volatile. Limited partnership interests are generally illiquid, may be subject to restrictions on transfer and redemption, and often require us to fund capital commitments over time, including at times when we may prefer to preserve liquidity. These investments are typically valued based on information provided by fund managers, often on a lag, and the values may not reflect the amounts we could realize in a sale.

Private credit and other privately placed investments may be difficult to value because observable market prices are not available. Their valuations rely on models and assumptions that require significant judgment. Insurance regulators, the NAIC, and rating agencies have increased their scrutiny of insurers' investments in private credit, structured securities, and other complex assets. Changes in regulatory treatment, including changes to risk-based capital charges, designation processes, or admissibility rules, could increase the capital required to support these investments or force us to reduce our allocations at unfavorable prices. Returns on alternative investments can vary significantly from period to period and may be negative.

***We may be unable to sell investments at fair prices when we need liquidity, and disruptions in financial markets could impair our portfolio.***

We maintain a significant portion of our portfolio in highly liquid securities to meet unexpected cash needs. However, financial market disruptions can reduce liquidity even for securities that are normally actively traded. In periods of market stress, bid-ask spreads may widen, trading volumes may fall, and some markets may cease to function effectively. If we need to raise significant cash quickly, for example after a major catastrophe, to pay a large judgment, or to meet collateral requirements, we may be forced to sell securities at prices below their carrying values, realizing losses.

The valuation of some of our investments, particularly those classified within Level 3 of the fair value hierarchy, depends on unobservable inputs and management judgment. During periods of market disruption, more of our investments may become difficult to value, and our estimates of fair value may differ materially from the prices we could realize in a sale. Changes in methodology or assumptions could also result in significant changes in reported values.

Our investment results also depend on the performance of external investment managers and our investment advisory subsidiary. They may fail to achieve their benchmarks, violate our investment guidelines, or suffer operational failures. Changes in tax laws affecting the relative attractiveness of municipal bonds or other investments could also reduce our after-tax investment income.

### Risks Relating to Capital, Liquidity, and Ratings

***A downgrade in the financial strength ratings of our insurance subsidiaries, or in the credit ratings of Highmark Industries Co., could adversely affect our business, liquidity, and cost of capital.***

Financial strength ratings are an important competitive factor in the property and casualty insurance industry. Many agents, brokers, and policyholders, particularly commercial customers and buyers of surety bonds and excess and surplus lines coverage, consider an insurer's ratings when deciding where to place business. Some customers, lenders, landlords, and contracting parties require their insurers or sureties to maintain ratings at or above specified levels. Rating agencies evaluate our capital adequacy, operating performance, business profile, enterprise risk management, reserve adequacy, investment risk, and reinsurance protection. They periodically revise their methodologies and capital models in ways that could adversely affect our ratings even if our financial condition does not change.

A downgrade, or the placement of our ratings on negative outlook or credit watch, could reduce our ability to write new business and retain existing business, particularly in our commercial, specialty, and surety operations. It could cause distribution partners to move business to competitors, trigger termination or collateral provisions in reinsurance, program, and other agreements, increase our borrowing costs, reduce our access to capital markets, and lower the market price of our securities. Events that could lead to a downgrade include significant catastrophe losses, adverse reserve development, investment losses, deterioration in underwriting results, increases in financial leverage, changes in rating agency methodologies, and adverse developments affecting the industry generally. Because our insurance subsidiaries participate in an intercompany pooling arrangement and generally share consistent ratings, a downgrade could affect all of our principal insurance subsidiaries simultaneously. We cannot assure that we will maintain our current ratings.

***Highmark Industries Co. is a holding company that depends on dividends and other distributions from its insurance subsidiaries, which are subject to regulatory restrictions.***

Highmark Industries Co. has no direct insurance operations. Its principal assets are the capital stock of its subsidiaries and its holdings of cash and invested assets. Its ability to pay dividends to shareholders, repurchase shares, pay interest and principal on its debt, and meet its other obligations depends largely on dividends and other distributions from its insurance subsidiaries. State insurance laws, including those of Connecticut, restrict the amount of dividends an insurer may pay without the prior approval of its domiciliary regulator. Ordinary dividends generally may be paid only from earned surplus. Dividends exceeding specified thresholds based on statutory surplus and net income require prior approval. Regulators may also restrict or prohibit dividends if they determine that a payment would impair an insurer's financial condition, even if it would otherwise be permitted.

If our insurance subsidiaries experience significant losses, adverse reserve development, or investment losses that reduce their statutory surplus or net income, their capacity to pay dividends could decline sharply. Rating agency capital requirements, which may exceed regulatory requirements, may further limit the amounts our insurance subsidiaries can prudently distribute. If the holding company cannot receive sufficient dividends, it may be unable to pay dividends to shareholders, repurchase shares, service its debt, or meet other obligations without borrowing or selling assets. In addition, in the event of the insolvency, liquidation, or rehabilitation of an insurance subsidiary, the claims of policyholders and other creditors of that subsidiary would be satisfied before any assets were available to Highmark Industries Co. as a shareholder.

***We may need additional capital in the future, and it may not be available on acceptable terms.***

Our future capital needs depend on many factors. These include our ability to establish adequate reserves, the frequency and severity of catastrophe and other losses, our growth, our reinsurance purchasing decisions, the performance of our investment portfolio, and regulatory and rating agency capital requirements. We may need to raise capital through the issuance of debt, equity, or hybrid securities, or through other arrangements, to replenish capital after significant losses, to support growth, to maintain our ratings, or to refinance existing debt. Capital may not be available when we need it, particularly after a catastrophe or during periods of financial market disruption, when many insurers may be seeking capital at the same time. Even if available, it may be available only on terms that are unfavorable to us or that dilute existing shareholders. If we cannot obtain adequate capital, we may be required to reduce our writings, retain less risk, sell assets at unfavorable prices, or accept rating downgrades. Any of these could adversely affect our business.

***Our indebtedness and related obligations could limit our flexibility and adversely affect our financial condition.***

Highmark Industries Co. has outstanding debt obligations and may incur additional indebtedness in the future. Our debt agreements and any credit facilities contain covenants that may limit our ability to incur additional debt, create liens, engage in mergers and asset sales, or take other actions. They also include financial covenants, such as requirements to maintain minimum net worth or maximum leverage ratios. A significant reduction in stockholders' equity, for example from catastrophe losses, reserve strengthening, or investment losses, could cause us to fail to comply with such covenants, which could result in an event of default and acceleration of our obligations. Our debt service obligations reduce the cash available for other purposes, and our leverage is a factor that rating agencies consider in assigning ratings. If we need to refinance debt during a period of high interest rates or tight credit, our interest costs could increase significantly.

***Our insurance subsidiaries are subject to minimum capital and surplus requirements, and failure to meet them could subject them to regulatory action.***

Our insurance subsidiaries must maintain minimum levels of capital and surplus under state insurance laws, including risk-based capital requirements. Although the total adjusted capital of each of our insurance subsidiaries exceeded the levels at which regulatory action would be triggered as of the end of fiscal 2024, significant losses, adverse reserve development, investment losses, or changes in the risk-based capital formula could reduce their capital ratios below required levels. If an insurance subsidiary's total adjusted capital falls below specified levels, regulators may require it to submit a corrective action plan, may restrict its operations, or may place it under regulatory control. Rating agencies generally expect insurers to maintain capital well above regulatory minimums, so a decline in capital ratios could adversely affect our ratings before any regulatory threshold is reached. Changes to the risk-based capital framework, the group capital calculation, or other capital standards, including changes that may be influenced by international developments, could increase the capital we must hold.

### Legal and Regulatory Risks

***We are subject to extensive regulation, and changes in laws and regulations, or in their interpretation and enforcement, could adversely affect our business.***

Our insurance operations are subject to extensive regulation and supervision, principally by state insurance regulators. The primary purpose of insurance regulation is to protect policyholders, not shareholders or other investors. State regulators have broad authority over nearly every aspect of our business, including licensing, rates and policy forms, underwriting and claims practices, capital and surplus, investments, transactions with affiliates, dividends, and the ability to cancel or non-renew policies or withdraw from markets. Compliance with these requirements is costly and time-consuming, and the requirements may limit our ability to respond to market conditions, to grow our business, or to achieve our profitability objectives.

Laws and regulations governing insurance, and the interpretation and enforcement of those laws by regulators and courts, change frequently. Changes could increase our costs, restrict our operations, require changes to our products and pricing, or expose us to liability. Regulators may adopt new requirements in response to catastrophes, affordability concerns, political pressure, or perceived market failures. Possible measures include mandates to offer certain coverages, restrictions on underwriting or rating factors, limits on rate increases, or requirements to participate in new residual market mechanisms. Regulators also conduct periodic financial and market conduct examinations, which may result in findings, fines, required corrective actions, or other enforcement measures.

Although the federal government generally does not directly regulate insurance, federal legislation and agencies affect our business in many ways. Such legislation includes laws relating to terrorism risk, flood insurance, financial services regulation, taxation, privacy, sanctions, and the Medicare Secondary Payer Act. Proposals to expand federal involvement in insurance regulation, to create a federal catastrophe reinsurance program, or to change federal programs on which we rely could adversely affect us. International standard-setting bodies may also influence the development of U.S. regulation, including capital standards and climate risk supervision.

***Rate regulation and restrictions on our ability to manage exposure may prevent us from charging adequate prices or from reducing exposure where we believe it is warranted.***

For admitted lines of business, state laws generally require us to file rates, rating rules, and policy forms with regulators. In many states and lines, regulators must approve rate filings before they take effect. Regulators may delay, reduce, or deny rate increases that we believe are necessary to reflect loss cost trends, particularly in personal automobile, homeowners, and workers' compensation lines, where affordability concerns are often prominent. Delays in implementing rate changes can be especially damaging during periods of rapid loss cost inflation, because policies written at inadequate rates continue to generate losses until rate increases take effect and are earned over the policy term.

State laws may also restrict our ability to cancel or non-renew policies, to withdraw from lines of business or geographic markets, or to reduce exposure in high-hazard areas. Some states require regulatory approval of withdrawal plans or impose lengthy notice periods. Following catastrophe events, some states have imposed moratoriums on cancellations and non-renewals in affected areas. These restrictions may force us to maintain exposure in markets where we believe rates are inadequate or catastrophe risk is excessive, which could adversely affect our results and our ability to manage catastrophe exposure within our risk tolerances.

***We may be required to pay assessments to state guaranty associations, residual market mechanisms, and catastrophe funds, which could be significant.***

As a condition of doing business, our insurance subsidiaries are members of state insurance guaranty associations that pay covered claims of insolvent insurers. We are subject to assessments based on our share of premiums written in the relevant states and lines. The amount and timing of these assessments depend on insurer insolvencies, which are unpredictable and may increase after major catastrophes or during periods of industry stress. Although many states allow insurers to recover assessments through premium tax offsets or policy surcharges, recovery may be delayed or incomplete.

We also must participate in residual market mechanisms, including automobile insurance plans, workers' compensation assigned risk pools, FAIR plans, and state-sponsored windstorm and property insurance pools. These mechanisms often charge inadequate rates and can incur large deficits, particularly after major catastrophes. Those deficits are funded through assessments on participating insurers. In certain coastal states, residual property markets have grown significantly in recent years, increasing the potential for large post-event assessments. Assessments may not be fully recoverable through policyholder surcharges or otherwise, and they could materially affect our results in the period in which they are levied.

***Regulation of data, privacy, artificial intelligence, and algorithmic decision-making could restrict our practices and increase our costs.***

We collect, use, store, and share large amounts of personal and other sensitive information about policyholders, claimants, employees, and others. We are subject to federal and state privacy and data security laws, including insurance data security laws based on the NAIC's model law that Connecticut and other states have adopted, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and a growing number of comprehensive state consumer privacy laws. These laws impose requirements on how we collect, use, disclose, retain, and protect personal information. They also require notification of breaches and, in some cases, give individuals rights to access, correct, or delete their information or to opt out of certain uses. The requirements vary by state, change frequently, and may be inconsistent, increasing our compliance costs and the risk of inadvertent violations.

Regulators and legislators have increased their focus on insurers' use of external consumer data, predictive models, artificial intelligence, and algorithms in marketing, underwriting, pricing, and claims handling. Some states have adopted or proposed laws and regulations requiring insurers to test such tools for unfair discrimination, to establish governance and risk management programs, to provide explanations to consumers, or to obtain regulatory approval before using certain data or models. Some states have restricted or prohibited the use of credit-based insurance scores and other rating factors. Additional restrictions could reduce the effectiveness of our pricing and underwriting models, require costly changes to our systems and processes, increase the risk of adverse selection relative to competitors in states with fewer restrictions, or expose us to regulatory action, fines, or litigation alleging unfair discrimination or other violations. Our usage-based insurance programs and other uses of telematics and behavioral data may draw particular scrutiny.

***We are subject to litigation and regulatory proceedings, which could result in significant liabilities and reputational harm.***

In the ordinary course of business, we are party to many lawsuits and arbitrations, most of which involve claims under our insurance policies. We are also subject to lawsuits, regulatory investigations, and other proceedings arising from our business practices. These can include matters relating to claims handling, sales and marketing, underwriting and pricing, producer compensation, employment, contracts, and intellectual property. Some of these proceedings may seek large compensatory, punitive, or extra-contractual damages, or may be brought as class actions. Plaintiffs may allege bad faith in our handling of claims, which can expose us to liability beyond policy limits. Class actions and other aggregate litigation, including litigation challenging our use of data and algorithms, the valuation of total loss vehicles, depreciation practices, or premium refunds, could result in substantial liabilities.

The outcome of litigation and regulatory proceedings is inherently unpredictable, and it may be affected by changes in judicial attitudes, jury composition, and the broader social and political environment. Our reserves for litigation and regulatory matters are estimates and may prove inadequate. Even when we prevail, legal proceedings can be costly and time-consuming, can divert management attention, and can harm our reputation. Regulatory investigations could result in fines, restitution, required changes to our business practices, or restrictions on our licenses.

***Changes in tax laws or their interpretation could adversely affect our results and financial condition.***

We are subject to federal, state, and local income, premium, and other taxes. Changes in tax laws, regulations, or their interpretation could increase our tax expense, reduce the value of our deferred tax assets, reduce the after-tax return on our investments, or make our products less attractive. Possible changes include changes in corporate income tax rates, the rules governing property and casualty insurance company reserves, the treatment of municipal bond interest, the corporate alternative minimum tax, premium tax rates, or the taxation of investment income. The realization of our deferred tax assets depends on our ability to generate sufficient future taxable income of the appropriate character. If we determine that some portion of our deferred tax assets is not realizable, we would need to establish a valuation allowance, reducing net income and stockholders' equity. Tax authorities may also challenge positions we take on our tax returns, and the resolution of such challenges could result in additional tax, interest, and penalties.

***Changes in accounting standards could adversely affect our reported results and financial condition.***

Our consolidated financial statements are prepared in accordance with GAAP, and our insurance subsidiaries' statutory financial statements are prepared under statutory accounting principles prescribed or permitted by their domiciliary regulators. The Financial Accounting Standards Board, the SEC, the NAIC, and state regulators periodically change accounting standards and interpretations. Changes in accounting standards, including changes relating to the measurement of insurance liabilities, the recognition of credit losses, the valuation of investments, or disclosure requirements, could change how we report our results and financial condition, could require significant changes to our systems and processes, and could affect our statutory capital and dividend capacity. Such changes may be applied retrospectively. They may also affect how investors, rating agencies, and regulators perceive our financial condition even if our underlying economics do not change.

***Our participation in federal programs, including the National Flood Insurance Program, exposes us to the risk of changes in those programs.***

We participate in the National Flood Insurance Program through its "Write Your Own" arrangement, under which we issue and service flood policies on behalf of the federal government and receive an expense allowance. Although the federal government bears the underwriting risk on these policies, we are exposed to operational and compliance risks in administering the program, to the risk that the expense allowance does not cover our costs, and to the risk of program lapses or changes that disrupt our ability to offer flood coverage to customers. A lapse in the program's authorization could harm our relationships with distribution partners and policyholders who rely on us for flood coverage. Changes in the program's pricing or structure could affect the demand for, and competitive dynamics of, private flood and homeowners insurance.

***Failure to comply with sanctions, anti-corruption, and anti-money laundering laws could result in significant penalties.***

We are subject to economic sanctions laws administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control and to other laws that prohibit dealings with certain countries, entities, and individuals, as well as to applicable anti-corruption and anti-money laundering requirements. Although our operations are conducted mainly in the United States, our policyholders, claimants, vendors, and reinsurers may have international connections, and our marine, cargo, and certain specialty coverages may involve international exposures. Violations of these laws, even if inadvertent, could result in significant fines, penalties, and reputational harm. Sanctions regimes change frequently and can be complex to apply, particularly during periods of geopolitical conflict.

### Operational and Technology Risks

***Cyberattacks, data breaches, and other information security incidents could disrupt our operations, expose us to liability, and harm our reputation.***

Our business depends heavily on information technology systems, including systems for underwriting, policy administration, billing, claims handling, financial reporting, and communications with distribution partners and policyholders. We store and process large amounts of confidential and personal information, including health information about injured workers and claimants, financial account information, and personal identifiers. We and our vendors face constant and evolving cyber threats from criminal organizations, nation-state actors, hacktivists, insiders, and others. These threats include ransomware, phishing and social engineering, business email compromise, malware, denial-of-service attacks, attacks on software supply chains, and exploitation of vulnerabilities in widely used software. The increasing sophistication of attackers, including through the use of artificial intelligence to craft convincing phishing messages and deepfakes, increases these risks.

Despite our information security program, which includes administrative, technical, and physical safeguards, third-party assessments, and incident response planning, our systems and those of our vendors may be breached or disrupted. Security measures cannot eliminate all risk, and some attacks may go undetected for extended periods. Our hybrid work model, our reliance on cloud service providers and other third parties, and our ongoing core systems modernization increase the number of potential points of vulnerability.

A successful cyberattack or data breach could have many consequences:

- disruption of our ability to quote and issue policies, process payments, or handle claims, including during a catastrophe when timely claims service is most critical;
- the theft, loss, corruption, or unauthorized disclosure of confidential and personal information;
- fraudulent payments or transfers of funds;
- significant costs for investigation, remediation, notification, credit monitoring, and system restoration;
- regulatory investigations and fines under state insurance data security laws, privacy laws, and other requirements, including requirements to notify regulators within short time periods;
- litigation, including class actions, by affected individuals, distribution partners, or others;
- damage to our reputation and loss of business; and
- increased costs for security measures and insurance.

Our own insurance coverage for cyber incidents may be insufficient to cover all losses. We are also subject to SEC rules requiring disclosure of material cybersecurity incidents, which may require us to disclose incidents before we have fully assessed or remediated them. Additional information about our cybersecurity program appears in Part I, Item 1C, "Cybersecurity."

***Our multi-year core systems modernization program may not be completed successfully, on schedule, or within budget, and may disrupt our operations.***

We are engaged in a multi-year program to modernize our core policy administration, billing, and claims systems. Large-scale technology projects of this kind are complex and carry significant execution risk. The program may cost more or take longer than anticipated, may not deliver the expected improvements in speed to market, efficiency, or data quality, and may disrupt our operations during implementation. Data migration from legacy systems may result in errors, data loss, or inconsistencies that affect underwriting, billing, claims handling, reserving, or financial reporting. Implementation problems could impair service to distribution partners and policyholders, causing them to move business to competitors. They could also cause errors in policy issuance, premium billing, or claim payments, or weaken our internal controls over financial reporting. The program depends on third-party vendors and on scarce technology talent, and vendor failures or staff turnover could delay it.

During the transition, we must maintain both legacy and new systems, which increases costs and operational complexity. Changes in claims handling and data capture associated with new systems may alter historical patterns used in our actuarial analyses, increasing the uncertainty of our reserve estimates. If we fail to complete the program successfully, or if competitors modernize more effectively, our competitive position could suffer, and we could be required to write off capitalized costs.

***Our use of artificial intelligence, including generative artificial intelligence, may expose us to operational, legal, regulatory, and reputational risks.***

We are exploring and deploying artificial intelligence technologies, including machine learning and generative artificial intelligence, to improve underwriting and claims productivity, document processing, and customer service. These technologies are rapidly evolving and present significant risks. Artificial intelligence tools may produce inaccurate, incomplete, or biased outputs. They may fail in unpredictable ways, may be difficult to explain or audit, and may be manipulated by malicious actors. The use of generative artificial intelligence may result in the inadvertent disclosure of confidential or personal information, the infringement of third-party intellectual property, or the creation of content that is misleading or inappropriate. Reliance on third-party artificial intelligence providers exposes us to their operational, security, and compliance risks.

The legal and regulatory framework governing artificial intelligence is developing rapidly and inconsistently across jurisdictions. Insurance regulators have issued guidance and requirements on the use of artificial intelligence by insurers. Additional laws and regulations could restrict our use of these technologies, require costly governance and testing, or expose us to liability. Although we have established a governance framework to manage these risks, it may not be effective. If we fail to adopt artificial intelligence technologies as effectively as our competitors, our competitive position could also suffer.

***We depend on third-party vendors and service providers, and their failures could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.***

We rely on third parties for many important functions. These include cloud computing and data hosting, software, catastrophe models and other analytical tools, data and analytics, independent adjusters, defense counsel, medical management and pharmacy benefit management, repair networks, printing and mailing, payment processing, and certain investment management services. These vendors may fail to perform as expected, may experience financial difficulties, cybersecurity incidents, or operational disruptions, may fail to comply with applicable laws or our contractual requirements, or may terminate their relationships with us. Some vendors provide services that are difficult or costly to replace quickly, and some serve many insurers, so that a single vendor failure could affect the industry broadly. Our ability to oversee vendors, including their subcontractors, is limited. We may be held responsible for their failures under regulatory requirements, including state insurance data security laws requiring oversight of third-party service providers. Increases in vendor costs, including for catastrophe models, data, and technology services, could also adversely affect our expenses.

***Business disruptions, including natural disasters, pandemics, and infrastructure failures, could impair our ability to operate.***

Our operations could be disrupted by events affecting our facilities, systems, personnel, or vendors. Such events include natural catastrophes, power outages, telecommunications failures, pandemics, terrorist attacks, civil unrest, and cyberattacks. Significant concentrations of our employees work at our Connecticut headquarters and in our regional and claims offices, and an event affecting one of these locations could disrupt critical functions. Catastrophes that generate large numbers of claims may coincide with disruptions to our own operations or those of our vendors and distribution partners in the affected area. Although we maintain business continuity and disaster recovery plans and test them periodically, they may not work as intended in an actual event, particularly a severe or prolonged one. Disruptions could impair our ability to handle claims, issue policies, and conduct other critical business functions, damaging our relationships with policyholders and distribution partners and exposing us to regulatory action.

***Our success depends on our ability to attract, develop, and retain qualified personnel, including our senior management team.***

Our business depends on the expertise and judgment of our underwriters, claims professionals, actuaries, risk engineers, catastrophe modelers, data scientists, technology professionals, investment professionals, and senior management. Competition for these professionals is intense, both within the insurance industry and, for technology and data roles, from other industries. The insurance industry faces demographic challenges, including the retirement of experienced professionals and difficulty attracting younger workers to insurance careers. The loss of key personnel, or our inability to attract and retain qualified talent, could impair our underwriting and claims performance, our technology initiatives, and our ability to execute our strategy. Our hybrid work model may affect our ability to develop and mentor employees, to maintain our culture, and to compete for talent with employers offering different arrangements. Increases in compensation costs to attract and retain talent could adversely affect our expense ratio. Although we maintain succession plans for key positions, the transition of leadership responsibilities may not be smooth.

***Our enterprise risk management framework and internal controls may not be effective in identifying and mitigating all risks.***

We have established an enterprise risk management framework to identify, measure, monitor, and mitigate the principal risks we face, including insurance, catastrophe, reserving, investment, credit, operational, and strategic risks. This framework relies on models, data, assumptions, and judgment that may prove inaccurate. It may fail to identify emerging risks or to anticipate the correlation of risks that seemed unrelated. Our risk tolerances may not be set appropriately, and our monitoring may not detect exposures that exceed them. Human error, misconduct, or failures of oversight could cause losses despite our controls.

We are required to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting and disclosure controls and procedures. Our controls are designed to provide reasonable, not absolute, assurance, and they may not prevent or detect all errors or fraud. If we identify a material weakness in our internal control over financial reporting, or if we fail to maintain effective controls, including during the transition to new core systems, we could be required to restate our financial statements, could face regulatory action, and could lose investor confidence, which could adversely affect the market price of our securities.

***Fraud by policyholders, claimants, distribution partners, vendors, or employees could increase our losses and costs.***

Insurance fraud is a persistent problem in the property and casualty industry. It includes fraudulent applications, misrepresentation of exposures, staged accidents, inflated or fabricated claims, provider billing fraud, and organized fraud rings. Fraud often increases after catastrophes, during economic downturns, and in states with legal environments that encourage it. Advances in technology, including generative artificial intelligence capable of producing fabricated documents, images, and voices, may make fraud more difficult to detect. Although we maintain a special investigations unit and use analytical tools to detect fraud, we cannot detect or prevent all fraud. Fraud by distribution partners, program administrators, vendors, or our own employees, including misappropriation of premiums or claim funds, could also cause losses and reputational harm.

### General and Strategic Risks

***Adverse economic conditions could reduce demand for our products, increase our losses, and impair our investments.***

Our business is sensitive to general economic conditions in the United States. An economic downturn could reduce demand for our products because businesses reduce payrolls, sales, inventories, vehicle fleets, and property values on which premiums are based, and because individuals buy fewer vehicles and homes. Economic stress could increase claim frequency and duration in certain lines, including workers' compensation, surety, directors' and officers' liability, employment practices liability, and crime and fidelity, and could increase fraud. It could also increase premium payment defaults and reduce collections, impair the creditworthiness of the issuers of securities we hold, reduce the value of our investments, and increase the risk that reinsurers or other counterparties fail to meet their obligations.

Conversely, periods of economic growth accompanied by high inflation could increase our loss costs faster than our pricing, as discussed above. Monetary policy designed to control inflation could raise interest rates and reduce the fair value of our fixed maturity portfolio. Changes in fiscal policy, government shutdowns, uncertainty about the federal debt limit, and changes in trade policy, including tariffs that raise the cost of repairing and replacing insured property, could adversely affect economic conditions, financial markets, and our results.

***Geopolitical events and global instability could adversely affect financial markets, our investments, and our insurance exposures.***

Geopolitical events, including military conflicts, terrorism, trade disputes, sanctions, and political instability, could disrupt financial markets, increase market volatility, raise energy and commodity prices, and contribute to inflation. These developments could reduce the value of our investments, increase our loss costs, and affect economic conditions in ways that reduce demand for our products. They could also increase cyber threats, including state-sponsored attacks on U.S. businesses and infrastructure, which could affect our own operations and produce claims under policies we have written. Our marine, cargo, and transportation coverages may be affected by disruptions to global trade and shipping routes. Political developments in the United States could result in significant changes in tax, regulatory, trade, and other policies affecting our business.

***Acquisitions, dispositions, and other strategic initiatives may not achieve their intended results and could expose us to unanticipated liabilities.***

From time to time we may pursue acquisitions of businesses, books of business, or distribution capabilities. We may also enter into strategic partnerships or dispose of businesses or exit lines of business. These transactions involve significant risks. We may overpay for acquired businesses, fail to integrate them successfully, fail to retain key personnel, distribution partners, or customers, or fail to realize anticipated benefits. Acquired businesses may have reserve deficiencies, compliance problems, or other liabilities that we did not identify in due diligence and that may not be covered by indemnification. Transactions may require regulatory approvals that are delayed, conditioned, or denied. Dispositions and exits may result in losses, ongoing indemnification obligations, or retained liabilities, and they may harm our relationships with distribution partners. Run-off of exited lines may be more costly than anticipated.

In addition, our strategic initiatives may not succeed. These include our efforts to grow selectively in excess and surplus lines and specialty markets, to expand our use of predictive analytics, to improve our digital capabilities, and to manage catastrophe exposure. We may also misjudge market conditions, competitive dynamics, or customer preferences in ways that harm our results.

***Damage to our reputation could adversely affect our business.***

Our reputation with distribution partners, policyholders, regulators, rating agencies, employees, and investors is important to our success. It could be damaged by many events, including:

- poor claims handling, particularly after a catastrophe when many policyholders are affected and public attention is high;
- cybersecurity incidents or data breaches;
- regulatory actions, litigation, or allegations of misconduct;
- non-renewals or market withdrawals in catastrophe-exposed areas;
- financial difficulties, rating downgrades, or significant reserve charges;
- misconduct by employees, distribution partners, program administrators, or vendors; and
- negative publicity, including through social media, which can spread rapidly regardless of its accuracy.

Our positions or perceived positions on environmental, social, governance, and other public policy matters, including our underwriting and investment practices regarding particular industries, may also attract criticism from stakeholders with differing views. Some stakeholders may criticize us for not doing enough on climate and social issues, while others may criticize us for considering such issues at all. Federal and state laws and regulations regarding these matters are also changing and in some cases conflicting. Damage to our reputation could reduce our ability to attract and retain business, distribution partners, and employees, and could increase regulatory scrutiny.

***Our intercompany pooling arrangement and holding company structure concentrate risk across our insurance subsidiaries.***

Our admitted insurance subsidiaries participate in an intercompany pooling arrangement under which premiums, losses, and expenses are combined and reallocated according to fixed participation percentages. This arrangement allows us to manage capital efficiently and supports consistent ratings, but it also means that significant losses in one subsidiary's business affect all participants in proportion to their shares. Changes to the pooling arrangement require regulatory approval, which may not be obtained or may be conditioned. Regulators of the states in which our subsidiaries are domiciled may impose requirements or restrictions on intercompany transactions, including reinsurance, service agreements, and cost-sharing arrangements, that limit our flexibility to allocate capital and resources efficiently across our group.

### Risks Relating to Our Common Stock

***The market price of our common stock may be volatile, and you could lose all or part of your investment.***

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors. These include actual or anticipated variations in our results of operations, catastrophe losses, reserve developments, changes in our ratings, changes in analysts' estimates or recommendations, announcements by us or our competitors, regulatory or legislative developments affecting the insurance industry, conditions in the financial markets generally, and the other risks described in this Item 1A. The stock market has experienced extreme price and volume fluctuations that have often been unrelated or disproportionate to the operating performance of particular companies. Because property and casualty insurers' results can change abruptly due to catastrophes and reserve developments, our results may fall below the expectations of investors and analysts, which could cause our stock price to decline sharply. Securities class action litigation has often been brought against companies following declines in the market price of their securities, and such litigation could result in substantial costs and divert management's attention.

***Insurance laws and certain provisions of our organizational documents and applicable corporate law may delay, deter, or prevent a change of control.***

The insurance holding company laws of our insurance subsidiaries' domiciliary states, including Connecticut, require prior regulatory approval of any acquisition of control of a domestic insurer or of a company that controls one. Control is generally presumed to exist if a person directly or indirectly owns, controls, or holds with the power to vote a specified percentage of the voting securities of the insurer or its parent company. As a result, any person seeking to acquire a controlling interest in Highmark Industries Co. would need prior approval from the insurance regulators of the states in which our insurance subsidiaries are domiciled, and investors whose holdings would cross the relevant threshold may need to obtain approval or file disclaimers of control. The regulatory approval process may be lengthy, may result in conditions on the transaction, and may not result in approval. In addition, certain provisions of our organizational documents and of the corporate law of our state of incorporation may make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us or to effect a change in our Board of Directors or management. These laws and provisions may delay, deter, or prevent a change of control, including a transaction that some or all of our shareholders consider desirable, and may reduce the price investors are willing to pay for our common stock.

***We cannot assure that we will continue to pay dividends or repurchase shares at current levels or at all.***

The declaration and payment of dividends and the repurchase of shares are at the discretion of our Board of Directors. They depend on our results of operations, financial condition, capital requirements, rating agency considerations, the ability of our insurance subsidiaries to pay dividends to the holding company under applicable insurance laws, restrictions in our debt agreements, and other factors our Board considers relevant. Significant catastrophe losses, adverse reserve development, investment losses, regulatory restrictions, or the need to preserve capital to support our ratings or growth could cause our Board to reduce or suspend dividends or share repurchases. Any reduction or suspension could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

***Future issuances of equity or equity-linked securities could dilute existing shareholders.***

We may issue additional shares of common stock, preferred stock, or securities convertible into or exchangeable for common stock to raise capital, including after significant losses, to finance acquisitions, or in connection with employee compensation programs. Such issuances could dilute the ownership interests of existing shareholders, could reduce earnings per share and book value per share, and could adversely affect the market price of our common stock. Preferred stock or other senior securities could have rights, preferences, and privileges senior to those of our common stock, including with respect to dividends and distributions upon liquidation.
