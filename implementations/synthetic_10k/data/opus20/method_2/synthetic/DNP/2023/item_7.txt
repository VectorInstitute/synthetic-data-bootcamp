## Executive Overview

### Our Business

Silverlake is a diversified manufacturer of rigid packaging. We produce:

- aluminum beverage cans and ends;
- steel and aluminum food containers;
- aerosol and specialty metal containers;
- glass bottles and jars; and
- rigid plastic bottles, jars, preforms, tubs and closures.

We also provide related design, decoration and supply-chain services. Our customers include:

- global and regional beverage companies, brewers and spirits producers;
- food processors and pet food manufacturers;
- household and personal care products companies;
- healthcare and nutritional products companies; and
- co-packers, distributors and industrial customers.

We are headquartered in Colorado and operate a network of manufacturing facilities, decorating centers, technical centers and distribution sites in North America and in selected international markets.

We manage our business through three reportable segments, each organized around a primary packaging substrate:

- **Metal Packaging**
- **Glass Packaging**
- **Rigid Plastics**

Corporate expenses and certain centrally managed activities, including portions of our research and development and sustainability functions, are not allocated to the segments. Segment results are presented in Note 18, "Segment Information," to our consolidated financial statements.

Our business is characterized by:

- recurring, high-volume demand that tends to track consumer staples consumption;
- significant capital intensity;
- meaningful exposure to raw material, energy and freight costs, which is moderated, but not eliminated, by contractual pass-through mechanisms; and
- a customer base that, particularly in beverage cans, is concentrated among large, sophisticated purchasers.

These characteristics shape our financial profile. They are the reason we focus closely on operating efficiency, capital discipline, cash generation and balance sheet strength.

### How We Evaluate Performance

Management uses a combination of financial and operating measures to evaluate our performance, allocate resources and make compensation decisions. The principal financial measures include the following.

- **Net revenues.** Net revenues reflect volumes, pricing, product and customer mix, and the pass-through of raw material and certain other costs. Because pass-through mechanisms can cause net revenues to rise or fall with input costs without a corresponding change in underlying profitability, we evaluate net revenues together with volume trends and margin measures.
- **Operating income and operating margin.** Operating income is our primary consolidated measure of operating profitability. Operating margin can be affected by changes in pass-through revenue. Higher pass-through revenue, for example, tends to reduce margin percentages even when absolute operating income is unchanged.
- **Segment operating income.** We evaluate segment performance based on segment operating income, which excludes unallocated corporate expenses and certain items that management does not consider representative of ongoing segment performance. See Note 18, "Segment Information."
- **EBITDA.** We use EBITDA, a non-GAAP measure, to evaluate operating performance before the effects of capital structure, income taxes and depreciation and amortization. This is particularly useful in a capital-intensive business with significant depreciation.
- **Cash provided by operating activities and free cash flow.** We view cash generation as a core indicator of the health of our business and of our capacity to invest, reduce obligations and return capital. Free cash flow, a non-GAAP measure, reflects cash available after the capital investment needed to maintain and grow our asset base.
- **Capital expenditures.** We monitor capital spending relative to depreciation and amortization, relative to net revenues and against approved project budgets and return thresholds.
- **Trade working capital.** We monitor accounts receivable, inventories and accounts payable, both in absolute terms and relative to net revenues, because they are significant drivers of operating cash flow and are affected by seasonality and input-cost movements.

We also track operating measures through the Silverlake Production System. These include:

- safety performance;
- line efficiency and uptime;
- spoilage;
- energy intensity;
- customer service and on-time delivery; and
- quality performance.

Improvements in these measures are important contributors to our cost position and to our relationships with customers.

## Business Environment and Factors Affecting Our Results

Our results are affected by a number of external and internal factors, many of which are described in Item 1, "Business," and Item 1A, "Risk Factors." The factors management considers most significant are discussed below.

### Customer Demand and Category Trends

Demand for our products depends on demand for the beverages, foods, household goods, personal care products and other items our customers package in our containers. Underlying demand for rigid packaging generally tracks consumer staples consumption. It is therefore less sensitive to economic cycles than discretionary spending, although it is not immune to them.

Within that overall pattern, demand varies meaningfully by substrate, category and package format:

- **Beverage cans.** Demand is influenced by brand owners' new product launches, including energy drinks, sparkling waters, ready-to-drink cocktails and other innovative beverages, and by shifts of existing products from other substrates into cans. Certain categories, such as hard seltzers and other emerging beverages, have experienced rapid growth followed by periods of slowing or declining demand.
- **Specialty formats.** Demand for slim, sleek and larger-format cans has grown in importance alongside standard sizes. This requires us to manage our line configurations and conversion investments carefully.
- **Food cans.** Demand in developed markets is relatively mature. It is affected by harvest conditions, promotional activity, consumer preferences for fresh and frozen alternatives and macroeconomic conditions. Pet food remains a notable area of demand.
- **Glass.** Demand for premium and super-premium spirits bottles and for distinctive designs supports our Glass Packaging segment. Premium categories may be more sensitive to reductions in discretionary spending.
- **Rigid plastics.** Demand reflects consumption trends in water, carbonated soft drinks, dairy, household chemicals and personal care. It is also affected by regulatory and consumer focus on plastic waste and by brand owner commitments to recycled content.

Our multi-substrate portfolio allows us to participate in our customers' growth wherever their portfolios shift. However, a shift in demand from one substrate to another may not be offset in timing, amount or profitability.

### Raw Material Costs and Contractual Pass-Through

Raw materials represent a significant portion of our cost of products sold. Our principal raw materials include:

- aluminum coil and end stock;
- tinplate, tin-free steel and other steel products;
- silica sand, soda ash, limestone and cullet;
- PET, HDPE, PP and post-consumer recycled resins; and
- coatings, inks and other materials.

Many of our customer agreements contain mechanisms that pass changes in key raw material costs through to customers. For aluminum, these mechanisms typically address the underlying metal price and, in many cases, regional premiums. For steel, they typically provide for periodic price adjustments. For resin, they are typically based on published indices with a lag.

These mechanisms help moderate the effect of input-cost volatility on our margins over time, but they are imperfect:

- **Timing differences.** Pass-throughs implemented with a lag can cause us to absorb cost increases before recovering them, or to reduce prices before our own costs decline.
- **Incomplete coverage.** Conversion fees, certain regional premiums, coatings, freight, labor and, in many contracts, energy may be excluded or only partially covered.
- **Index mismatch.** The indices or formulas in our contracts may not correspond precisely to our actual costs.
- **Contract coverage.** Not all of our sales are made under contracts with pass-through mechanisms.

Pass-through mechanisms also affect how our results should be interpreted. When raw material costs rise, pass-through increases our net revenues and our cost of products sold by similar absolute amounts. That tends to reduce our margin percentages even when absolute profitability is unchanged. The reverse is true when costs fall. For this reason, management evaluates margin percentages together with absolute operating income, volumes and mix.

Movements in raw material prices also affect our working capital. Higher prices increase the carrying value of inventories and the amount of receivables. They also generally increase the amount of accounts payable.

### Energy Costs

Our operations consume significant amounts of natural gas and electricity. Glass manufacturing is highly energy intensive, and energy represents one of the largest cost components of our Glass Packaging segment. Our metal decorating and curing operations and our plastics molding operations also consume substantial energy.

We manage energy cost exposure through:

- fixed-price and indexed supply contracts;
- financial hedging in certain cases;
- contractual energy pass-through or surcharge mechanisms with customers where obtainable; and
- energy efficiency investments, including increased cullet use and electric boosting in our glass furnaces.

These measures do not fully protect us. Energy pass-through mechanisms are not available in all customer arrangements, and where available they may be lagged or incomplete. Energy cost movements therefore have a more direct effect on Glass Packaging margins than raw material cost movements, which are more comprehensively addressed by pass-through provisions.

### Industry Capacity, Pricing and Contract Terms

Industry capacity relative to demand significantly influences pricing, utilization and contract terms in our markets. As described in Item 1, certain segments of the beverage can industry have experienced capacity additions in recent periods. Combined with changes in demand, these additions affected the supply-demand balance in some regions.

In periods of excess capacity:

- competitors may pursue volume at the expense of price;
- customers may seek to renegotiate contracts or shift volumes; and
- utilization and fixed-cost absorption may decline.

In periods of tight capacity, we may be unable to fully serve demand. We may then incur premium costs to supply customers from more distant facilities.

We add capacity primarily when supported by customer commitments, and we favor conversions of existing lines and brownfield expansions over greenfield construction where economically sensible. We also continue to evaluate opportunities to consolidate older, higher-cost facilities and rationalize underperforming lines, consistent with our portfolio optimization strategy.

### Manufacturing Performance and Fixed-Cost Absorption

Our plants operate high-speed, technically demanding processes in which small changes in line speed, uptime, spoilage and material usage have meaningful effects on cost. Because a substantial portion of our manufacturing cost base is fixed or semi-fixed, utilization strongly influences profitability. Utilization is affected by demand, planned maintenance, furnace rebuilds, line conversions and unplanned outages.

Our results are also affected by the costs associated with:

- commissioning new lines and conversions;
- start-up inefficiencies at new or expanded operations; and
- transferring production between facilities in connection with consolidations.

Glass furnace rebuilds, which we plan to minimize customer disruption, temporarily reduce production capacity at affected facilities. They also require significant capital expenditure.

### Labor Costs and Workforce Availability

A significant portion of our hourly workforce is represented by labor unions under collective bargaining agreements with varying expiration dates. Labor costs are affected by:

- collective bargaining outcomes;
- wage inflation in the communities where we operate;
- the availability of skilled maintenance and technical personnel;
- overtime requirements; and
- health and welfare benefit costs.

Shortages of skilled labor can increase costs and affect productivity, spoilage and safety, particularly when we start up new capacity.

### Freight and Logistics

Our products are bulky relative to their value, and glass is heavy and fragile. Freight is therefore a significant cost, and our ability to serve customers depends on reliable transportation. Freight rates, carrier capacity, driver availability and rail service affect our cost to serve. Our supply agreements allocate freight costs between us and our customers in varying ways, and we may be unable to recover all increases.

### Sustainability and Packaging Regulation

Customer, investor and regulatory expectations regarding recyclability, recycled content and carbon intensity increasingly influence package format decisions and supplier selection.

Packaging-related laws can affect demand for particular substrates and our costs for recycled inputs. These include extended producer responsibility programs, such as the law enacted in Colorado, as well as deposit return systems, recycled-content mandates and restrictions on certain packaging.

We believe aluminum cans and glass containers are well positioned in many markets because of their established recycling systems. Rigid plastics are undergoing rapid change as converters increase their use of post-consumer recycled resin, and recycled resin can at times cost more than virgin resin.

Climate-related regulation may also increase our costs, particularly in Glass Packaging, because of the energy intensity of glass melting and process emissions from carbonate raw materials.

### Trade Measures

Tariffs on steel and aluminum imports under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, and antidumping and countervailing duty proceedings relating to steel and aluminum products, affect the price and availability of materials we purchase. These measures can raise domestic prices for affected materials and increase aluminum regional premiums. Those costs may not be fully recoverable through pass-through mechanisms. Trade measures affecting imported packaging can also influence competitive conditions in certain of our markets.

### Foreign Currency

Our international operations expose us to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates. Changes in exchange rates affect:

- the translated value of our foreign revenues, earnings, assets and liabilities;
- the cost of raw materials and equipment purchased in foreign currencies; and
- the competitiveness of imported products in our markets.

Translation effects on our net assets are recorded in accumulated other comprehensive income (loss).

### Seasonality

Demand for many of our products is seasonal:

- beverage packaging demand is generally higher in warmer months and ahead of major holidays;
- food can demand is influenced by fruit and vegetable harvest seasons; and
- demand for premium glass and decorative tins is often higher ahead of year-end holiday periods.

We build inventory during lower-demand periods. As a result, our working capital requirements typically increase ahead of peak seasons and then decline as inventories are sold and receivables are collected. Results for interim periods are not necessarily indicative of results for a full year.

### Inflation and Interest Rates

General inflation affects the costs of labor, materials, energy, freight, services and capital equipment. Our contractual pass-through mechanisms and inflation-indexed pricing provisions in certain agreements help us recover a portion of these increases, but not all of them, and often with a lag.

Changes in interest rates affect the cost of any variable-rate borrowings, the cost of refinancing our obligations, the discount rates used to measure our pension and other postretirement obligations, and the discount rates used in our impairment assessments.

## Strategic Priorities and Fiscal 2023 Actions

Our strategy is to be the preferred rigid packaging partner for leading brand owners, delivering sustainable packaging solutions with superior quality, service and value, and generating attractive returns for our shareholders. During fiscal 2023, our operating and capital decisions were guided by the following priorities.

**Growing with customers in attractive categories.** We directed capital toward categories with favorable long-term demand, including:

- beverage cans for innovative beverage categories;
- specialty can sizes;
- premium glass for spirits and non-alcoholic beverages; and
- rigid plastic packaging incorporating higher levels of recycled content.

Consistent with our approach, we prioritized conversions of existing lines and investments supported by customer commitments.

**Optimizing our portfolio and footprint.** We continued to evaluate our product lines, customers and facilities to direct capital and management attention toward the most attractive opportunities. Footprint actions, including consolidations of higher-cost capacity and conversions of lines to higher-demand formats, are an ongoing element of our operating model. Other investing activities in fiscal 2023 provided $26.7 million of cash, including proceeds from dispositions of property and other investing items.

**Driving productivity and cost leadership.** Through the Silverlake Production System, we continued to pursue improvements in:

- safety;
- line efficiency;
- spoilage;
- energy usage;
- maintenance effectiveness; and
- labor productivity.

We continued deploying predictive and condition-based maintenance, automation, advanced inspection and data analytics across our plants. We also used our scale in procurement of aluminum, steel, resin, soda ash, coatings, energy and logistics.

**Leading in sustainable packaging.** We continued investing to:

- increase recycled content across our substrates;
- increase cullet use and electric boosting in glass melting;
- advance our LakeLite lightweight bottle and closure designs; and
- reduce the energy and emissions intensity of our operations.

Several of these investments also reduce our costs. For example, higher cullet use reduces furnace energy requirements, and lightweighting reduces material usage.

**Investing in innovation.** Research and development expenses were $38.0 million in fiscal 2023. This spending was directed toward lightweighting, new package formats, decoration, coatings and materials qualification, process technology and recyclability. In addition, a significant portion of our engineering and technical center activity is embedded in operating and capital spending rather than in research and development expense.

**Maintaining a prudent balance sheet.** Given the level of our total liabilities relative to stockholders' equity, we emphasized cash generation and balance sheet discipline in fiscal 2023. We paid no dividends and limited share repurchases to $2.4 million. Cash and cash equivalents increased by $313.2 million.

### Net Revenues

Net revenues for fiscal 2023 were $9,578.9 million. Our net revenues are generated primarily from the sale of containers, closures and related products under multi-year supply agreements, annual arrangements and purchase orders. A smaller portion comes from design, decoration and related services.

Net revenues are affected by the following principal factors:

- **Volume.** Shipments reflect our customers' demand for their own products, our share of customers' requirements, new business awards, contract renewals and losses, and our available capacity in the relevant formats and regions. Seasonal patterns concentrate beverage shipments in warmer months and food can shipments around harvest periods.
- **Pass-through of input costs.** A substantial portion of our sales is made under agreements that adjust prices for changes in aluminum, steel, resin and, in certain cases, energy costs. These adjustments directly affect net revenues. Because aluminum is the most significant raw material for our beverage can business, changes in the aluminum benchmark price and regional premiums have a particularly meaningful effect on Metal Packaging net revenues.
- **Price and contract terms.** Base pricing reflects negotiated contract terms, competitive conditions and the industry supply-demand balance in each market. In regions where beverage can capacity additions affected that balance, pricing for new or renewed business reflected those conditions.
- **Product and customer mix.** Specialty can sizes, premium decoration, proprietary glass designs, and recycled-content and lightweight plastic packaging generally carry different pricing than standard products. Mix shifts across substrates, customers and regions therefore affect net revenues.
- **Foreign currency.** Net revenues from our international operations are translated into U.S. dollars and are affected by exchange rate movements.

Our customer base is more concentrated in Metal Packaging, particularly beverage cans, than in our other segments. Sales to our largest customers are generally made under multi-year supply agreements. These provide good visibility into demand, but their terms, including volume commitments expressed as supply-share percentages or requirements arrangements, mean that actual volumes depend on our customers' own sales performance.

We do not report backlog because our products are generally manufactured and shipped with relatively short lead times in response to customer orders and forecasts.

### Other Operating Expenses

Other operating expenses were $8,017.0 million in fiscal 2023, or 83.7% of net revenues. These expenses consist primarily of cost of products sold and include the substantial majority of our depreciation and amortization.

The principal components of these expenses and the factors that affected them are as follows:

- **Raw materials.** Raw materials are the largest component of cost of products sold. Our raw material costs are driven by:
  - aluminum benchmark prices, regional premiums and conversion fees;
  - steel prices;
  - soda ash and other glass batch material costs;
  - virgin and recycled resin prices; and
  - coatings and other materials.

  To the extent these costs are passed through to customers, changes in them are largely offset in net revenues, subject to the timing and coverage limitations described above. Where pass-through is incomplete or lagged, raw material cost movements directly affect our margins.
- **Energy.** Natural gas and electricity costs are most significant in Glass Packaging. Energy costs not covered by customer surcharges, hedges or fixed-price contracts directly affect segment margins.
- **Manufacturing labor and overhead.** These costs reflect wage rates, staffing levels, overtime, benefits, maintenance, utilities other than process energy, and plant-level support functions. Because many of these costs are fixed or semi-fixed, cost per unit is sensitive to utilization.
- **Freight and logistics.** Outbound freight to customers and inbound freight for raw materials are meaningful costs, particularly for glass.
- **Depreciation and amortization.** Total depreciation and amortization was $463.4 million, or 4.8% of net revenues. It reflects our substantial base of manufacturing equipment, glass furnaces and facilities, as well as amortization of intangible assets from prior acquisitions. The substantial majority is recorded in other operating expenses.
- **Restructuring and other operating items.** These include costs associated with facility consolidations, line rationalizations and closures, such as severance, accelerated depreciation and asset-related charges, and other operating gains and losses.

Productivity improvements under the Silverlake Production System, lightweighting, increased cullet use and procurement initiatives help offset inflation in labor, energy, freight and other non-pass-through costs. Start-up costs associated with line conversions and capacity additions, together with the temporary capacity effects of furnace rebuilds, work in the opposite direction.

### Selling, General and Administrative Expenses

Selling, general and administrative ("SG&A") expenses were $412.6 million in fiscal 2023, or 4.3% of net revenues. SG&A expenses include:

- the costs of our direct sales organizations and key account teams;
- graphic services, customer service and design center activities not classified as research and development;
- corporate functions, including finance, treasury, tax, legal, internal audit, human resources and enterprise information technology;
- incentive and stock-based compensation; and
- professional fees and other administrative costs.

SG&A expenses represent a relatively modest proportion of our net revenues. This is consistent with a business model in which a concentrated base of large customers is served through key account relationships, and in which most costs are incurred in manufacturing and distribution. We continue to invest in commercial capabilities, including:

- key account management;
- pricing and contract management; and
- digital tools for order management, forecasting collaboration, artwork approval workflows and shipment visibility.

We also continue to invest in modernizing and integrating our information systems across our segments.

### Research and Development Expenses

Research and development expenses were $38.0 million in fiscal 2023, or 0.4% of net revenues. We expense research and development costs as incurred. Our research and development activities focus on:

- lightweighting;
- new package formats;
- decoration technologies;
- coatings and materials qualification, including alternatives to substances under regulatory or consumer scrutiny;
- process technology, including furnace technology, electric boosting, heat recovery and alternative melting approaches in glass; and

Although research and development expense is not material to our consolidated results, we consider these activities important to our competitive position and to our customers' sustainability objectives.

### Income Tax Expense

Income tax expense was $629.4 million in fiscal 2023, compared with income before income taxes of $1,111.4 million. This represents an effective tax rate of 56.6%.

Applying the U.S. federal statutory rate of 21% to our income before income taxes would have resulted in income tax expense of approximately $233.4 million. Our recorded income tax expense therefore exceeded that amount by approximately $396.0 million. Income tax expense also exceeded our net income: income tax expense was $629.4 million, compared with net income of $482.0 million.

Our effective tax rate differs from the U.S. federal statutory rate as a result of a number of factors, including:

- **State and local income taxes**, net of federal benefit, in the U.S. jurisdictions in which we operate.
- **The jurisdictional mix of our earnings.** Our international operations are subject to tax rates and regimes that differ from those in the United States, and certain foreign earnings are also subject to U.S. taxation.
- **Valuation allowances.** We may not be able to recognize the tax benefit of losses or other deferred tax assets in certain jurisdictions or entities where realization is not more likely than not.
- **Nondeductible expenses** and other permanent differences between book and taxable income.
- **Changes in reserves for uncertain tax positions**, including the effects of tax examinations, settlements and the expiration of statutes of limitations.
- **Tax effects of restructuring, portfolio and legal entity actions**, including the treatment of gains, losses and charges that are not deductible, or are deductible at different rates, in the relevant jurisdictions.

## Segment Overview

We manage and report our business through three reportable segments: Metal Packaging, Glass Packaging and Rigid Plastics. Our chief operating decision maker evaluates segment performance and allocates resources based principally on segment net revenues and segment operating income.

Segment operating income excludes:

- unallocated corporate expenses, including treasury, tax, legal, internal audit, enterprise information technology, and portions of research and development and sustainability functions; and
- certain items that management does not consider representative of ongoing segment performance, such as certain restructuring and other operating items.

Segment net revenues, segment operating income, depreciation and amortization, capital expenditures and reconciliations to our consolidated results are presented in Note 18, "Segment Information," to our consolidated financial statements. The discussion below describes the operating characteristics and principal performance drivers of each segment.

### Metal Packaging

Our Metal Packaging segment manufactures:

- aluminum beverage cans and ends;
- steel and aluminum food cans and ends;
- aerosol containers; and
- specialty metal packaging, such as decorative tins, metal closures, pails and other industrial containers.

Key factors affecting Metal Packaging results include the following.

- **Beverage can demand and format mix.** Segment volumes are driven by our customers' beverage sales, new product introductions in cans, and conversions of existing products to cans. Demand for specialty sizes, including slim, sleek and larger formats, requires ongoing line conversions. Category dynamics, including the maturation of certain fast-growing beverage categories, affect demand for particular formats and the utilization of the capacity that serves them.
- **Aluminum cost pass-through.** Most of our beverage can supply agreements pass through changes in the underlying aluminum price and, in many cases, regional premiums. Segment net revenues therefore move substantially with aluminum prices. The timing and completeness of pass-through, and the treatment of conversion fees, freight and other cost elements, affect segment margins.
- **Industry capacity.** Capacity additions in certain segments of the beverage can industry, combined with changes in demand, affected the supply-demand balance in some regions. This influenced pricing on new and renewed contracts and the utilization of our network.
- **Food can and aerosol dynamics.** Food can volumes are influenced by harvest conditions, retailer and brand-owner promotional activity, pet food demand and consumer preferences. Steel cost movements are addressed through periodic contractual price adjustments, with timing and coverage that vary by contract. Food can and aerosol markets face competition from imports in certain regions, influenced by trade measures.
- **Operating efficiency.** High-speed can lines are sensitive to uptime, spoilage and changeover performance. Our lightweighting programs for can bodies and ends reduce metal content and costs. Expanding predictive maintenance and advanced vision inspection supports line performance.

The Metal Packaging segment's customer base is the most concentrated of our three segments. Several of its largest customers have purchased from us for extended periods under successive multi-year supply agreements. The renewal of these agreements, and the terms on which they are renewed, are significant factors in the segment's long-term performance.

### Glass Packaging

Our Glass Packaging segment manufactures glass containers for beer, spirits, wine, non-alcoholic beverages and food. It also provides related design and decoration services.

Key factors affecting Glass Packaging results include the following.

- **Energy costs.** Glass melting is highly energy intensive, and energy is one of the largest cost components of the segment. Energy costs not recovered through customer surcharges, hedges or fixed-price contracts directly affect segment margins. Increased cullet use and electric boosting reduce energy consumption and emissions.
- **Furnace rebuilds and utilization.** Glass furnaces operate continuously for extended campaigns and require periodic rebuilds. Rebuilds temporarily reduce capacity and require significant capital expenditures. We use them as opportunities to:
  - improve energy efficiency and emissions control;
  - increase electric boosting; and
  - improve forming line capacity and flexibility.

  Because furnaces operate continuously, utilization and pack-to-melt performance have a significant effect on segment profitability.
- **Premium category demand.** Demand for premium and super-premium spirits bottles, custom molds and high-end decoration supports segment mix. Premium categories may be more sensitive to reductions in discretionary consumer spending.
- **Regional market structure.** Because freight costs limit economic shipping distances, our glass plants primarily serve customers within their regional markets. Local supply-demand conditions and competition from imported glass in certain categories therefore affect segment results.
- **Raw materials and cullet.** Soda ash is the most significant glass batch material from a cost perspective. Cullet availability and quality vary significantly by region. Higher-quality cullet supports both cost reduction and sustainability objectives.
- **Climate-related regulation.** The segment is the most exposed of our segments to greenhouse gas regulation and carbon costs, because of the energy intensity of glass melting and process emissions from carbonate raw materials.

### Rigid Plastics

Our Rigid Plastics segment manufactures:

- PET bottles and preforms;
- HDPE and PP bottles;
- jars, tubs and specialty containers; and
- closures.

Its end markets include beverages, food, household chemicals, personal care and healthcare.

Key factors affecting Rigid Plastics results include the following.

- **Resin cost pass-through.** Most of the segment's customer arrangements include resin pass-through mechanisms, typically based on published industry indices with a lag. Rising resin costs therefore tend to compress margins temporarily, and falling resin costs tend to expand them temporarily, until pass-through adjustments take effect.
- **Recycled content.** Brand owner commitments and regulatory mandates are increasing demand for food-grade post-consumer recycled PET and HDPE. Recycled resin can at times be more expensive than virgin resin, and its availability depends on collection and processing infrastructure. Our ability to secure recycled resin and recover its incremental cost affects segment margins and competitiveness.
- **Lightweighting and design.** Our LakeLite lightweight bottle and closure designs reduce resin usage while maintaining performance, supporting both customer cost and sustainability objectives.
- **Through-the-wall operations.** Many of the segment's operations are located near or on customers' filling sites, particularly in dairy, water and household chemicals. These arrangements support long-standing relationships and reduce freight. However, segment utilization at those sites depends on individual customers' volumes.
- **Competitive environment.** The rigid plastics industry is fragmented, barriers to entry are generally lower than in metal or glass, and certain customers manufacture some of their own containers or preforms. Pricing discipline and service are therefore important to segment performance.
- **Regulation.** Restrictions on certain single-use plastic packaging, plastic packaging taxes, recycled-content mandates and design requirements, such as closures that remain attached to bottles, could affect demand and require capital investment.

The segment's customer base is broader and less concentrated than that of Metal Packaging.

## Critical Accounting Estimates

The preparation of our consolidated financial statements in accordance with GAAP requires management to make estimates and assumptions. These affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenues and expenses, and the disclosure of contingent assets and liabilities. We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions and assumptions we believe are reasonable. Actual results could differ from these estimates.

We consider the following accounting estimates to be critical because they:

- involve significant judgment and uncertainty; and
- could have a material effect on our financial condition or results of operations if actual outcomes differ from our estimates or if different reasonable assumptions were used.

Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements.

### Revenue Recognition and Variable Consideration

We recognize revenue when control of our products transfers to customers, generally upon shipment or delivery depending on the contractual terms. Certain arrangements involve customer-specific products for which we have an enforceable right to payment. Revenue under those arrangements is evaluated to determine whether it should be recognized over time as products are manufactured.

Our supply agreements frequently include variable consideration, such as:

- volume-based rebates and incentives;
- pass-through price adjustments based on raw material indices or other formulas;
- price adjustments for prompt payment; and
- provisions related to quality claims and product returns.

We estimate variable consideration using the expected value or most likely amount method, depending on the nature of the arrangement. We include variable consideration in the transaction price only to the extent it is probable that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue will not occur. Estimates of rebates and incentives require judgment regarding customers' expected purchase volumes over the measurement period. Estimates of pass-through adjustments require judgment regarding the application of contractual formulas and indices.

Because of the scale of our net revenues, which were $9,578.9 million in fiscal 2023, relatively small percentage changes in these estimates could have a meaningful effect on reported revenue and operating income. We monitor our estimates regularly and adjust them as new information becomes available. Historically, adjustments to prior-period estimates have not been material.

### Goodwill and Indefinite-Lived Intangible Assets

Goodwill was $3,231.1 million at the end of fiscal 2023, representing 22.1% of total assets and approximately 124.1% of total stockholders' equity. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level at least annually, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the fair value of a reporting unit may be below its carrying value. Our reporting units are at or below the reportable segment level.

We may first perform a qualitative assessment to determine whether it is more likely than not that the fair value of a reporting unit is less than its carrying amount. If we bypass the qualitative assessment, or if it indicates a potential impairment, we perform a quantitative test comparing the fair value of the reporting unit with its carrying value. We generally estimate fair value using a combination of:

- an income approach based on discounted projected cash flows; and
- a market approach based on valuation multiples of comparable companies.

The quantitative assessment requires significant judgments, including estimates of:

- future net revenues, which depend on volumes, pricing, pass-through levels and mix;
- future operating margins, which depend on input-cost recovery, energy costs, productivity and industry capacity conditions;
- capital expenditure requirements, including glass furnace rebuild cycles;
- terminal growth rates; and
- discount rates reflecting the risks of each reporting unit.

Reporting units with significant exposure to the following conditions are generally more sensitive to changes in these assumptions:

- energy costs and climate-related regulation, particularly in glass;
- regulatory pressure on plastic packaging; or
- excess industry capacity in particular formats or regions.

We did not record a goodwill impairment charge in fiscal 2023. Several developments could cause us to record impairment charges in future periods, including:

- a sustained decline in demand;
- the loss of significant customers;
- adverse regulatory developments;
- prolonged excess industry capacity;
- an inability to recover cost increases;
- increases in discount rates; or
- a significant decline in our market capitalization.

Such charges, while non-cash, could be material to our results of operations and stockholders' equity.

We also test indefinite-lived intangible assets, such as certain trade names, for impairment at least annually by comparing their estimated fair values with their carrying values. We generally estimate those fair values using a relief-from-royalty method.

### Long-Lived Assets, Including Property, Plant and Equipment and Finite-Lived Intangible Assets

Our long-lived assets include property, plant and equipment, which is included within other assets of $7,568.1 million, and finite-lived intangible assets, which are included within intangible assets, net of $830.0 million. Depreciation and amortization was $463.4 million in fiscal 2023.

Significant judgments related to long-lived assets include:

- **Useful lives.** We depreciate property, plant and equipment using the straight-line method over estimated useful lives. Estimating useful lives requires judgment regarding wear, technological change and expected use. Glass furnace rebuilds require judgment regarding the expected length of furnace campaigns. Changes in estimated useful lives, including as a result of decisions to consolidate or close facilities, can result in accelerated depreciation.
- **Capitalization.** We capitalize expenditures that extend the useful life or increase the capacity of assets, including glass furnace rebuilds. We expense routine maintenance and repairs. Distinguishing capital from maintenance expenditures requires judgment, which is particularly relevant given the scale of our capital program.
- **Impairment.** We evaluate long-lived assets for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying values may not be recoverable. Examples include:
  - a decision to idle, consolidate or close a facility or line;
  - the loss of a significant customer at a dedicated or co-located facility;
  - sustained operating losses at an asset group; or
  - adverse changes in regulation.

  Recoverability is assessed by comparing the carrying value of an asset group with its undiscounted projected cash flows. If the carrying value is not recoverable, we measure an impairment loss as the excess of carrying value over fair value. These assessments require judgment regarding the grouping of assets, projected cash flows, remaining useful lives and fair values.

Customer-dedicated and through-the-wall facilities may be particularly susceptible to impairment if the related customer relationship is lost or significantly reduced.

### Income Taxes

Income tax expense was $629.4 million in fiscal 2023, an effective tax rate of 56.6%. Accounting for income taxes requires significant judgment, including:

- **Valuation allowances.** We record valuation allowances to reduce deferred tax assets to amounts that are more likely than not to be realized. Assessing realizability requires judgment regarding several factors:
  - future taxable income by jurisdiction;
  - the reversal of existing taxable temporary differences;
  - available tax planning strategies; and
  - the expiration periods of tax attributes.

  Changes in these assessments could significantly affect our effective tax rate.
- **Uncertain tax positions.** We recognize the benefit of a tax position only if it is more likely than not to be sustained upon examination based on its technical merits. We measure the benefit as the largest amount that is more likely than not to be realized upon ultimate settlement. We are regularly examined by tax authorities, and resolution of examinations may differ from our estimates.
- **Jurisdictional mix and international tax provisions.** Our effective tax rate is affected by the mix of earnings among jurisdictions with different tax rates and by the application of U.S. and foreign international tax provisions. These include provisions that subject certain foreign earnings to U.S. taxation and the global minimum tax rules being implemented by various jurisdictions.
- **Indefinite reinvestment.** We assess whether earnings of certain foreign subsidiaries are indefinitely reinvested. Changes in that assertion could result in the recognition of additional tax liabilities, including foreign withholding taxes.

Given the magnitude of our income tax expense relative to our pretax income in fiscal 2023, changes in these judgments could have a significant effect on our reported net income in future periods.

### Pension and Other Postretirement Benefits

Our defined benefit pension and other postretirement benefit obligations and expenses are determined using actuarial assumptions. The most significant are:

- **Discount rates.** These are determined by reference to yields on high-quality corporate bonds with maturities matching the expected timing of benefit payments. Lower discount rates increase our obligations and generally increase our future expense.
- **Expected long-term rates of return on plan assets.** These are based on target asset allocations and expected returns for each asset class. Lower expected returns increase pension expense.
- **Mortality and other demographic assumptions.** These are based on published mortality tables, adjusted where appropriate for plan experience.
- **Health care cost trend rates.** These apply to other postretirement benefit plans.

Differences between actual experience and our assumptions, and changes in assumptions, are recorded in accumulated other comprehensive income (loss) and amortized into expense over future periods to the extent they exceed specified thresholds. They can also affect required funding contributions. Facility closures and workforce reductions may result in curtailment or settlement accounting, which can accelerate the recognition of previously deferred amounts.

### Environmental and Other Contingencies

We accrue for environmental remediation obligations, legal proceedings and other contingencies when it is probable that a liability has been incurred and the amount can be reasonably estimated. Environmental accruals require judgment regarding:

- the extent of contamination;
- appropriate remediation methods;
- the timing of expenditures;
- the allocation of costs among potentially responsible parties; and
- the financial condition of those parties.

These estimates may change as a result of new information about site conditions, changes in remediation standards, the regulation of emerging contaminants such as PFAS, and the outcome of negotiations with regulators and other parties.

Legal contingency accruals require judgment regarding the likelihood of unfavorable outcomes and the range of possible losses. Product liability and quality claims require similar judgments. Actual outcomes could materially exceed our accruals.

### Inventories

Inventories were $1,333.9 million at the end of fiscal 2023. Inventories are stated at the lower of cost and net realizable value, or, for inventories measured using the last-in, first-out method, the lower of cost and market. Judgment is required to determine:

- allowances for obsolete, slow-moving and excess inventories, including customer-specific printed containers and spare parts;
- the allocation of fixed production overhead to inventories based on normal capacity, with abnormal costs associated with unusually low production expensed as incurred; and
- the effects of raw material price movements on inventory valuation.

Changes in customer demand, product designs or raw material prices could result in additional inventory charges.

## Outlook

Our outlook reflects the business environment and strategic priorities described in this MD&A. As we look ahead, management's areas of focus include the following.

- **Demand and mix.** We expect demand for our products to continue to track consumer staples consumption broadly, with continued variation by category, substrate and region. We will continue to align our capacity, through conversions and selective additions supported by customer commitments, with formats and categories that offer attractive long-term demand. These include specialty can sizes, innovative beverage categories, premium glass and recycled-content plastics.
- **Cost recovery and productivity.** We will continue to manage raw material, energy, labor and freight costs through our contractual pass-through mechanisms, procurement initiatives, energy management practices and the Silverlake Production System. Our objective is to offset non-pass-through inflation through productivity, lightweighting, increased cullet use and spoilage reduction.
- **Industry capacity.** We will continue to monitor the supply-demand balance in our markets, particularly in beverage cans. Where supported by returns, we will pursue footprint optimization actions, including consolidation of higher-cost capacity.
- **Capital discipline.** Our capital expenditures of $953.4 million in fiscal 2023 reflected significant investment in growth, conversions, furnace rebuilds and sustainability. In future periods, we intend to calibrate our capital program to customer commitments, the timing of furnace rebuilds, regulatory requirements and our balance sheet priorities, while maintaining safe and reliable operations.
- **Cash generation and balance sheet strength.** We generated $1,688.6 million of cash from operations and $735.2 million of free cash flow in fiscal 2023, and we ended the year with $511.0 million of cash and cash equivalents. Given total liabilities of $12,028.9 million relative to total stockholders' equity of $2,604.5 million, we intend to continue emphasizing cash generation, working capital discipline and the management of our obligations. This includes addressing our short-term debt of $712.8 million.
- **Income taxes.** Our fiscal 2023 effective tax rate of 56.6% significantly reduced the conversion of operating income into net income. We will continue to evaluate the factors that contributed to this rate and opportunities to improve the efficiency of our tax structure, consistent with applicable law. We will also monitor developments in global minimum tax rules and other tax legislation.
- **Sustainability and regulation.** We will continue to invest in recycled content, decarbonization and recyclable package design. We will also monitor and adapt to extended producer responsibility programs, recycled-content mandates, climate-related regulation and food-contact material requirements, which may affect demand for particular substrates and our costs.
- **Shareholder returns.** Decisions regarding dividends and share repurchases will continue to be made by our Board of Directors in light of our results, cash flows, financial condition, capital requirements, debt covenants and balance sheet priorities.

These expectations are forward-looking statements and are subject to the risks and uncertainties described in Item 1A, "Risk Factors," and elsewhere in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. Actual results may differ materially.
