# ITEM 1A. RISK FACTORS

An investment in our common stock involves a high degree of risk. You should carefully consider the risks and uncertainties described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. That information includes Item 1, "Business," Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8.

The occurrence of any of the following risks, alone or in combination, could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows, and reputation, and could cause the trading price of our common stock to decline. The risks described below are not the only risks we face. Additional risks and uncertainties not currently known to us, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also impair our business. Many of the risks discussed below are interrelated, and the occurrence of one risk may increase the likelihood or severity of others.

The order in which these risks appear is not intended as an indication of their relative likelihood or the magnitude of their potential consequences. Certain statements below are forward-looking statements. Please refer to the "Cautionary Note Regarding Forward-Looking Statements" in Item 1.

## Risks Related to U.S. Government Contracting and Defense Spending

**We depend heavily on U.S. Government spending. Reductions, delays, or shifts in defense, space, and homeland security budgets could materially reduce our revenues and profitability.**

A substantial portion of our revenues is derived, directly or indirectly, from programs funded by the U.S. Government. These programs are principally funded by the Department of Defense and its military services and agencies, and to a lesser extent by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration and other civil agencies. Most of our U.S. Government-related revenues arise from subcontracts with prime contractors. The underlying funding, however, depends on the same congressional appropriations and executive branch priorities that govern direct government contracts. Our revenues for fiscal year 2023 of $1,982,571,143 therefore reflect, to a significant degree, budgetary decisions over which we have no control.

U.S. Government spending levels are subject to competing national priorities and to the political dynamics of the annual budget process. Those priorities include entitlement programs, interest on the national debt, domestic discretionary spending, and deficit reduction. Future defense budgets may be flat, may decline in real or nominal terms, or may be constrained by statutory spending caps, sequestration mechanisms, or agreements reached in connection with debt ceiling negotiations. Even if aggregate defense spending is maintained or increased, the funds may be allocated in ways that do not favor the platforms, programs, and capability areas we serve.

Because many of our products are designed into specific aircraft, missiles, munitions, vehicles, ships, and spacecraft, our revenues are sensitive to decisions affecting those platforms. Such decisions include production rates, upgrade schedules, fleet sizes, flight hours, depot maintenance funding, and retirement timelines. A decision to reduce procurement quantities, delay a new start, retire a legacy fleet earlier than expected, or cancel a program could significantly reduce our revenues from that program. We might not be able to replace those revenues promptly, or at all. In addition, the loss or reduction of a significant program could leave us with underutilized facilities, excess inventory, and workforce costs that we cannot readily reduce.

**Continuing resolutions, government shutdowns, and debt ceiling impasses could delay contract awards, funding, and payments and disrupt our operations.**

Congress frequently fails to enact full-year appropriations before the start of the U.S. Government's fiscal year on October 1. It instead funds the government through one or more continuing resolutions. Under a continuing resolution, agencies generally may not initiate new programs or increase production rates above prior-year levels. These limitations can delay new program starts, production ramp-ups, and contract awards on which we depend. That includes awards associated with munitions replenishment, directed energy prototyping, and proliferated space constellations, which represent important growth opportunities for our DES and SAT segments. Prolonged or repeated continuing resolutions can also cause prime contractors to defer purchase orders to us, compress award activity into shorter periods, and increase the volatility of our quarterly results.

If appropriations lapse entirely, a government shutdown may occur. A shutdown could suspend contract administration, government inspections and acceptance activities, payments, security clearance processing, export license adjudication, and other government functions on which we rely. Some of our employees work at or alongside government facilities that may be closed during a shutdown. Our facilities may also be unable to obtain government source inspections required before shipment. A failure to raise or suspend the statutory debt limit could similarly disrupt payments to government contractors and adversely affect financial markets.

We may elect to continue work on programs in advance of funding or contract award to preserve schedules and workforce. Those costs may not be recovered if funding does not materialize. Any of these events could adversely affect our revenues, cash flows, and results of operations.

**Our U.S. Government contracts and subcontracts may be terminated for convenience or default, reduced in scope, or not renewed. Contract options may not be exercised.**

The U.S. Government may terminate its contracts, in whole or in part, for its convenience at any time. Prime contractors typically have corresponding rights under the subcontracts they award to us. If a contract is terminated for convenience, we generally may recover only allowable costs incurred, settlement expenses, and a reasonable profit on work performed. We are not entitled to profit on work not performed. Termination settlements can take substantial time to negotiate and may not fully compensate us for our investment in facilities, tooling, inventory, and personnel dedicated to the terminated program. We may also be unable to recover costs incurred in anticipation of contract funding, or costs that are determined to be unallowable.

Contracts may also be terminated for default if we fail to perform in accordance with contract requirements. A termination for default could expose us to liability for excess re-procurement costs, return of progress payments or performance-based payments, and other damages. It could also impair our ability to compete for future contracts, because past performance is a significant evaluation factor in government source selections and in prime contractors' supplier selection decisions.

Many of our programs are structured as base contracts with multiple option periods or as indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity arrangements. The government and prime contractors are not obligated to exercise options or to place orders under these arrangements. A decision not to exercise options or not to place anticipated orders could reduce our revenues below our expectations.

**As a subcontractor, we depend on the performance, priorities, and purchasing decisions of a relatively concentrated group of prime contractors, some of which compete with us.**

We sell a significant portion of our products to a relatively concentrated group of large aerospace and defense prime contractors. These contractors integrate our products into the platforms they deliver to the U.S. Government and other customers. Our revenues from a given program depend not only on the government's funding of that program, but also on the prime contractor's performance, its program priorities, and its procurement strategy. A prime contractor may experience technical, schedule, financial, or quality difficulties on a program. Such difficulties could delay or reduce its orders to us, lead to disputes over responsibility for cost growth, or contribute to program restructuring or cancellation for reasons unrelated to our performance.

Some of our prime contractor customers maintain internal capabilities in power systems, energy storage, thermal management, or spacecraft electronics that overlap with ours. These customers may decide to perform work internally that they previously purchased from us. They may also favor their own affiliated businesses when selecting suppliers for new programs or upgrades. Industry consolidation among prime contractors and tier-one suppliers could further increase the bargaining power of our customers, reduce the number of potential customers for our products, and increase the likelihood that work is brought in-house.

Prime contractors also flow down to us many of the risks they bear under their prime contracts, including termination rights, audit rights, liquidated damages, and warranty obligations. In some cases, prime contractors seek to impose terms on subcontractors that are more onerous than those in the underlying prime contract. Our ability to resist those terms may be limited, particularly when the prime contractor represents a significant share of our revenues. The loss of, or a significant reduction in purchases by, one or more of our major prime contractor customers could have a material adverse effect on our results of operations.

**We operate in a highly competitive procurement environment. We may not win new contracts, retain existing positions when programs are recompeted, or prevail in bid protests.**

We obtain much of our business through competitive procurements conducted by the U.S. Government and by prime contractors. Competitive procurements require us to devote substantial resources to proposal preparation, often without assurance of award. We may underestimate the cost of performing a contract in order to submit a competitive bid, which could lead to losses if we win. Conversely, we may lose a competition because our price is not competitive or because our technical approach, past performance, or delivery capacity is judged inferior.

Even when we win an award, competitors may file bid protests with the awarding agency, the Government Accountability Office, or the U.S. Court of Federal Claims. A protest can delay contract performance and the associated revenues for months. It could also result in the award being overturned or re-competed. We may also file protests of awards to competitors, which entails cost and uncertain outcomes.

Programs on which we have historically held sole-source positions may be opened to competition when they transition from development to production, when sustainment contracts expire, or when the government acquires sufficient technical data rights to enable alternative sources. The U.S. Government has emphasized expanding the industrial base, qualifying second sources for munitions components and other critical items, and encouraging participation by non-traditional defense contractors. These policies may create opportunities for us, but they may also introduce new competitors on programs where we are currently the qualified source. That could put pressure on our prices and market share.

**U.S. Government audits, investigations, and business system reviews could result in adverse findings, cost disallowances, payment withholds, and other sanctions.**

The Defense Contract Audit Agency, the Defense Contract Management Agency, agency inspectors general, and other government organizations routinely audit and review our performance, cost structure, indirect rates, pricing practices, and compliance with applicable laws, regulations, and standards. Prime contractors conduct similar reviews of our subcontract pricing and performance. These audits may occur several years after the costs were incurred and the revenues recognized. As a result, adjustments could affect results in periods long after the underlying transactions.

Audits may result in adjustments to amounts previously billed or recognized. Costs found to be improperly allocated or unallowable will not be reimbursed, and any such costs already reimbursed must be refunded. If we are found to have submitted cost or pricing data that was not accurate, complete, or current, the government may seek price reductions, penalties, and interest. Disagreements with the government over the interpretation of the Cost Accounting Standards or the allowability of particular costs may result in claims against us that could take years to resolve.

DFARS business system rules require us to maintain acceptable accounting, estimating, purchasing, earned value management, material management and accounting, and property management systems. If government reviewers identify significant deficiencies, contracting officers may withhold a percentage of payments on affected contracts until the deficiencies are corrected. Such deficiencies could also adversely affect our eligibility for certain contract types and our competitive position. Our acquisitions of smaller businesses with less mature government contracting infrastructure, and our consolidation of functions into shared service organizations, increase the complexity of maintaining compliant business systems across the enterprise.

An adverse audit or investigation finding could also lead to civil, criminal, or administrative proceedings, as discussed under "Legal and Regulatory Risks" below. Even allegations of impropriety could harm our reputation with customers.

**Changes in U.S. Government acquisition policies and practices could reduce our profitability, weaken our competitive positions, and increase our risks.**

U.S. Government procurement policies change frequently, and many changes are implemented with limited notice. Such changes could adversely affect our business, including:

* increased use of fixed-price contracts for development work, which shifts technical and cost risk to contractors;
* changes in profit and fee policies, including in the weighted guidelines used to establish negotiated profit objectives;
* changes in the allowability of costs, including independent research and development, compensation, and restructuring costs;
* expanded government demands for technical data and computer software rights, including data developed in part at private expense;
* increased use of other transaction authorities, commercial item determinations, and prototyping arrangements that alter traditional competitive dynamics;
* changes in contract financing policies, including progress payment rates and performance-based payment terms;
* new requirements relating to cybersecurity, supply chain transparency, domestic sourcing, labor standards, and environmental and climate-related disclosures; and
* increased emphasis on cost or pricing data and other oversight of contractor pricing for sole-source products.

Much of our competitive advantage derives from proprietary know-how, qualification data, and design practices that we protect as trade secrets. Government efforts to obtain broader rights in technical data could enable competitors to produce products that compete with ours, particularly in sustainment and spare parts markets where we have historically been the sole source. Changes in contract financing policies could increase our working capital needs. Any of these developments could adversely affect our margins, cash flows, and competitive position.

**Our backlog may not be converted into revenue in the amounts or in the periods we anticipate, or at all.**

Backlog represents the estimated value of firm orders for which work has not yet been performed. It includes both funded and unfunded amounts. Unfunded backlog depends on future appropriations that may not be enacted. Funded backlog may also be reduced because of contract terminations, scope reductions, delivery schedule changes, or modifications.

Our backlog includes long-term development and production programs that will be performed over multiple years. During that time, technical difficulties, customer requirement changes, supply chain constraints, or funding shifts may alter the timing or amount of revenues. Backlog under commercial agreements may be subject to cancellation or deferral with limited penalties. Commercial space backlog may also be affected by our customers' ability to finance their constellations.

As a result, our backlog may not be indicative of future revenues in any particular period. Investors should not place undue reliance on backlog as a measure of future performance.

**Our classified work involves restrictions on disclosure, and the loss of facility or personnel security clearances could prevent us from performing on certain programs.**

Certain of our facilities are cleared to perform classified work, and many of our employees hold security clearances. Our ability to maintain these clearances depends on our compliance with the National Industrial Security Program and related requirements, and on our continued mitigation of any foreign ownership, control, or influence. If we were to lose or fail to obtain required facility or personnel clearances, we could be unable to perform on existing classified programs or to compete for future classified work. Delays in the government's processing of clearances for new hires can also limit our ability to staff programs and grow.

Because of applicable security restrictions, we may be limited in our ability to disclose information about classified programs. That includes information regarding their technical status, risks, and financial performance. Investors may therefore have less information about these programs than about our unclassified programs. Classified programs are subject to the same funding, performance, and termination risks as our other programs.

## Risks Related to Contract Performance and Operations

**We bear the risk of cost overruns on fixed-price contracts. Fixed-price development contracts present heightened risk of reduced margins or losses.**

Most of our production contracts, and many of our development contracts with prime contractors, are fixed-price. Under firm fixed-price contracts, we receive a predetermined price regardless of our actual costs. We therefore absorb any cost overruns. Under fixed-price incentive contracts, we share cost overruns with the customer up to a ceiling price, above which we bear the full cost. Our cost estimates for these contracts reflect assumptions about many factors, including:

* labor productivity and availability;
* the complexity of the design and the maturity of the underlying technology;
* the cost and availability of materials and components;
* supplier performance;
* manufacturing yields;
* qualification and test outcomes; and
* the timing of customer approvals.

Fixed-price development contracts are particularly difficult to estimate accurately. Development of high-power generation architectures, pulsed power subsystems, advanced thermal management systems, hypersonic power solutions, and space-qualified electronics often involves technologies that have not previously been produced at the required performance levels or in the required configurations. Technical problems that emerge during design, integration, or qualification testing may require redesign, additional testing, and schedule extensions, the cost of which we may not be able to recover. Prime contractors increasingly seek to impose fixed-price terms on development subcontracts, even when the corresponding prime contract is cost-reimbursable or when requirements are not yet stable.

Many of our long-term production contracts were priced before recent increases in labor, material, energy, and freight costs. Some of those contracts do not include economic price adjustment provisions that would allow us to recover these increases. If our actual costs exceed our estimates, our profits on these contracts would decline. We could also be required to record losses, which could be material, as discussed below.

**Our results depend on our ability to accurately estimate total contract revenues and costs. Changes in these estimates could cause significant fluctuations in our results.**

We recognize revenue on a significant portion of our contracts over time as we perform. We generally measure progress using costs incurred relative to total estimated costs at completion. This method requires us to make significant judgments about the total revenues and costs of each contract, including estimates of the impact of technical risks, schedule risks, variable consideration such as award and incentive fees, contract modifications, and claims. We review these estimates regularly and recognize changes on a cumulative catch-up basis in the period in which they are identified.

When the estimated total costs of a contract exceed its estimated total revenues, we must recognize the entire anticipated loss in the period in which the loss becomes evident. Because of the size and duration of some of our programs, a change in estimated costs at completion on a single program, or on a small number of programs, can have a significant effect on our results in a particular period. Adverse cumulative catch-up adjustments and loss provisions could also cause our results to differ materially from our guidance and from analysts' expectations.

Our estimates involve inherent uncertainty. We cannot assure that our estimates will prove accurate or that future revisions will not be material. Additional information regarding our revenue recognition policies and estimates is included in Item 7 and in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

**Our strategy of pursuing early design-in positions, sometimes at modest or negative margins, may not result in the production and sustainment revenues we anticipate.**

A core element of our strategy is to win positions early in the platform lifecycle, when design decisions are made. A successful design-in can lead to sole-source production and aftermarket revenues over the life of the platform. In pursuit of these positions, we sometimes accept development contracts with modest or negative margins. We also invest company-funded independent research and development and capital in anticipation of future production.

This strategy carries significant risk. The platform for which we have developed a product may be canceled, delayed, or produced in smaller quantities than expected. The government or prime contractor may select a different architecture or a competing supplier when the program transitions to production. We may be required to compete for production work against suppliers who did not bear the development costs. The government may also acquire sufficient technical data rights to enable competitive production. Production pricing may not allow us to recover our development investment, particularly if the customer negotiates production prices based on our cost data.

The returns on design-in investments are often realized over periods of many years. If the production and sustainment revenues we expect do not materialize, or are smaller or later than expected, we may not recover the losses and investments we incurred during development.

**Emerging capability areas, including directed energy and hypersonics, may not transition to programs of record on the timelines or at the scale we expect.**

We have invested significant resources in technologies supporting directed energy weapons, hypersonic systems, proliferated space architectures, aircraft electrification, and ground vehicle electrification. These investments include our PulseBank™ pulsed power systems and ThermAxis™ thermal management systems. Our directed energy and thermal management activities are currently weighted toward development and prototyping, and our hypersonic activities are concentrated in flight test campaigns.

The transition of these capabilities from prototypes to production programs of record is uncertain. Directed energy and hypersonic systems face substantial technical challenges, and some prototype efforts may not meet performance objectives. Government funding for these areas has fluctuated, and priorities may change as the threat environment, technology maturity, and budgetary conditions evolve. Programs may take longer to reach production than we anticipate, may be produced in smaller quantities, or may be canceled. Even if these programs transition to production, we may not be selected as a supplier. The system architectures ultimately adopted may also differ from those for which our products were designed.

Competition in these emerging areas includes large defense contractors, specialized power electronics firms, and venture-backed technology companies. Some of those competitors may be willing to accept lower returns or may have access to substantial private capital. If these markets develop more slowly than we expect, or if we do not capture meaningful positions in them, we may not realize returns on our investments. Our growth prospects could be adversely affected.

**Supply chain disruptions, single-source and limited-source dependencies, and supplier quality problems could delay our deliveries, increase our costs, and harm our customer relationships.**

We depend on a large number of suppliers for raw materials, components, and subassemblies. These include metals and alloys, rare earth magnet materials, lithium-ion cells and cell materials, thermal battery and energetic materials, power semiconductors, radiation-tolerant microelectronics, castings, forgings, and precision machined parts. Some of these items are available only from a single supplier or a limited number of qualified suppliers. This is particularly true for radiation-tolerant electronics, certain energetic and thermal battery materials, specialty alloys, and space-qualified cells. Qualifying a new source can require significant time and expense and, in some cases, customer or government approval. As a result, we often cannot quickly replace a supplier that is unable or unwilling to meet our requirements.

In recent periods, the aerospace and defense industry has experienced significant supply chain challenges. These have included extended lead times, supplier capacity constraints, labor shortages, inflationary pressures, and quality escapes. These conditions affected our operations in fiscal year 2023, including through delayed deliveries of certain components, increased expediting costs, and the need to hold higher inventory levels for certain programs. These conditions may persist or worsen. Many of our suppliers are small businesses with limited financial resources. Such suppliers may be vulnerable to financial distress, workforce constraints, cybersecurity incidents, or exit from the defense market. Increased demand across the defense industrial base, including for munitions and space components, competes for the same constrained supplier capacity on which we rely.

Supplier quality escapes present particular risk in our markets. A defective or nonconforming component that is not detected before integration could require rework, retesting, or requalification of our products. It could also require recalls or field retrofits of products already delivered and lead to claims by our customers. We are also subject to regulatory requirements to detect and avoid counterfeit electronic parts. A counterfeit part in our supply chain could result in product failures, cost disallowances, and regulatory consequences.

Although we have taken steps to mitigate supply chain risk, these measures may not be sufficient. They include qualifying alternative sources, placing longer-term purchase commitments, establishing safety stock, and redesigning products to accommodate more readily available components. These measures also increase our costs and working capital requirements. Supply chain disruptions could prevent us from meeting delivery schedules, subject us to liquidated damages or default terminations, and impair our relationships with customers.

**Increases in the cost, or constraints on the availability, of raw materials and critical components could adversely affect our margins and our ability to perform.**

The prices of many of the materials we use have fluctuated significantly. These include aluminum, titanium, copper, nickel-based alloys, specialty steels, lithium and other battery materials, rare earth elements, and electronic components. Prices may continue to fluctuate in response to global demand, supply disruptions, geopolitical events, trade policies, tariffs, export restrictions imposed by other countries, and inflation. A significant portion of global production and processing capacity for certain critical minerals, rare earth magnet materials, and battery materials is located in countries that are subject to, or may become subject to, U.S. sourcing restrictions or that may restrict exports to the United States.

Under long-term fixed-price contracts that do not include economic price adjustment provisions, we generally cannot pass cost increases through to our customers. Even where escalation clauses are available, they may not fully offset our actual cost increases, or may operate with a lag. We may be unable to obtain sufficient quantities of certain materials at any price, particularly materials that must be sourced domestically or from qualified suppliers in allied countries. Any of these developments could reduce our margins, delay deliveries, and require costly redesign or requalification of our products.

**The manufacture, storage, and handling of energetic materials involve inherent hazards. An explosion, fire, or similar incident could cause injury or loss of life, damage our facilities, interrupt production, and expose us to significant liability.**

Our DES segment manufactures thermal batteries, gas generators, squibs, initiators, pyrotechnic actuators, and other energetic devices. This work involves the processing, storage, and handling of energetic materials and pyrotechnic compositions. These activities are inherently dangerous and are conducted primarily at a specialized production facility in Arizona that was designed to provide required safety separation distances and environmental controls.

Despite our safety management systems, an explosion, fire, uncontrolled release, or other incident could occur. Those systems include process hazard analyses, operating procedures, training, independent safety reviews, and controlled access. Such an incident could result in serious injury or loss of life to our employees, contractors, or members of the public. It could also cause significant damage to our facilities and equipment and lead to environmental contamination. In addition, an incident could result in investigations by regulatory authorities, including the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, and Department of Defense explosives safety organizations. Those investigations could lead to the suspension or revocation of licenses and permits, fines, and mandated changes to our operations.

Because our energetic devices are produced in specialized facilities that require extensive licensing and qualification, we may be unable to transfer production quickly to another location if a facility is damaged or shut down. Our customers' qualification requirements may also prevent rapid transfer. Many of our energetic products support U.S. and allied munitions production on a sole-source or limited-source basis. An extended production interruption could therefore harm customers and national security programs, damage our reputation, and result in the loss of qualified positions to competitors. Our insurance may not cover all losses arising from such an incident, and some losses may not be insurable at all.

**Lithium-ion and other battery products present safety risks that could result in product failures, recalls, liability, and reputational harm.**

We design and produce energy storage products using lithium-ion and other battery chemistries for aircraft, ground vehicles, naval platforms, directed energy systems, and spacecraft. These products include our CoreCell™ aircraft batteries. Lithium-ion cells can experience thermal runaway, in which a cell overheats and may ignite or vent flammable materials. Thermal runaway can propagate to adjacent cells. It may be triggered by manufacturing defects, physical damage, improper charging, environmental exposure, or design flaws.

Although our designs incorporate cell-level monitoring and thermal runaway containment features, these measures may not prevent all failures. A battery failure could cause damage to the platform on which the battery is installed, injury or loss of life, and damage to property. It could also lead to grounding of aircraft fleets, product recalls, regulatory action by the Federal Aviation Administration or other authorities, and claims against us. Battery incidents elsewhere in the industry, even those not involving our products, could also lead to new regulatory requirements, more stringent certification standards, or customer reluctance to adopt lithium-ion technologies. Those consequences could increase our costs and slow adoption of our products.

The manufacturing, storage, and transportation of lithium-ion cells and batteries at our facilities also present fire risks and are subject to hazardous materials regulations. A fire at one of our facilities could interrupt production and cause significant losses.

**Failures of our products in mission-critical applications could result in substantial warranty costs, liability claims, loss of qualification, and damage to our reputation.**

Our products perform critical functions aboard military and commercial aircraft, missiles, munitions, interceptors, ground vehicles, naval vessels, and spacecraft. Many operate in extreme environments, including high temperatures, vibration, shock, radiation, and vacuum. The failure of one of our products, whether due to design defects, manufacturing defects, supplier component defects, or misuse, could cause the failure of the larger system or platform. Such a failure could result in loss of life, personal injury, property damage, mission failure, or the loss of a costly asset.

Product failures could subject us to warranty claims, product liability claims, recall and retrofit costs, and liquidated damages. Customers could also suspend deliveries pending root cause investigations, and our products could lose qualification on affected programs. Failure investigations in our industry can be lengthy and resource-intensive. They may halt production until the cause is identified and corrected, even if our product is ultimately determined not to be responsible.

We may be unable to limit our liability through contractual terms, indemnification, or insurance. Product liability claims involving aircraft, munitions, or spacecraft can involve substantial damages. The U.S. Government's indemnification of contractors for certain unusually hazardous risks is discretionary and limited in scope. Our insurance coverage may be insufficient, unavailable, or available only at prohibitive cost for certain risks. Beyond financial losses, a significant product failure could seriously harm our reputation for reliability, which is a principal competitive factor in our markets.

**Our space business is subject to risks associated with launch failures, on-orbit anomalies, and the loss of flight heritage.**

Our SAT segment produces power management and distribution electronics, space-qualified battery assemblies, solar array regulators, and launch vehicle power products. Space hardware cannot generally be repaired after launch. Failures may result in the partial or total loss of a spacecraft or mission. Launch failures, including failures unrelated to our products, may destroy spacecraft incorporating our hardware, delay customer programs, and lead to investigations that disrupt our production.

On-orbit anomalies involving our products could result in performance penalties or loss of incentive payments under contracts that tie a portion of the price to on-orbit performance. They could also lead to warranty claims and, most significantly, damage to our flight heritage. Flight heritage is a particularly important competitive factor in space markets, and we consider our record of on-orbit performance to be a significant competitive strength. A failure attributed to our products could impair our ability to win future space programs for years.

Customers and insurers in the space industry may seek to limit their exposure to launch and in-orbit risks through contractual terms, and the availability and cost of space insurance can fluctuate significantly. We may be unable to obtain contractual protection or insurance sufficient to cover our exposure.

**Our expansion into higher-rate production for proliferated low Earth orbit constellations involves execution, pricing, and customer credit risks.**

We are investing in production capacity and standardized product offerings to serve civil, commercial, and national security constellations in low Earth orbit. This market places greater emphasis on unit cost and delivery speed than our traditional high-reliability space business. It has required us to adapt our design and production approaches, including through greater design standardization, automated test, and production line investments.

We may not succeed in achieving the cost and production-rate targets required to compete effectively in this market. Competitors include newer entrants focused on commercial constellations and spacecraft manufacturers that have vertically integrated power system production. These competitors may offer lower prices or faster delivery. Pricing pressure in this market could reduce our margins. If our standardized products do not achieve broad adoption, we may not realize returns on our capacity investments.

Commercial satellite operators often depend on access to private capital markets, government contracts, or a limited number of anchor customers to fund constellation deployment. Changes in investor sentiment, interest rates, launch availability, or the competitive landscape could cause these customers to delay, reduce, or cancel orders. Some may be unable to pay amounts owed to us. National security constellation programs may be restructured as the government refines its space architecture. Any of these developments could adversely affect our SAT segment's revenues and profitability.

**Our commercial aerospace business is subject to economic cyclicality, certification requirements, and changes in aircraft production rates.**

Our APS segment supplies business jet, regional aircraft, and commercial rotorcraft manufacturers, and provides aftermarket products and services to commercial operators and maintenance, repair, and overhaul providers. Commercial aerospace demand is influenced by general economic conditions, corporate profitability, air travel demand, fuel prices, aircraft production rates, fleet utilization, and fleet age. Economic downturns, pandemics, geopolitical events, and other disruptions to air travel have historically caused sharp declines in new aircraft deliveries and aftermarket demand.

Our commercial aerospace products are subject to airworthiness certification requirements administered by the Federal Aviation Administration and foreign civil aviation authorities. Certification of new products and changes to existing products can be time-consuming and costly, and regulators may impose additional requirements or delay approvals. Delays in the certification of the aircraft on which our products are installed could delay our revenues. Our repair stations are subject to regulatory oversight, and an adverse finding could limit their operations.

We have pricing commitments under certain long-term commercial supply agreements that extend over multiple years. These commitments may limit our ability to recover cost increases. Any of these factors could adversely affect our APS segment's results.

**Our operations are concentrated in Arizona and are exposed to extreme heat, water scarcity, utility disruptions, natural disasters, and other regional risks.**

Our corporate headquarters, principal engineering center, largest manufacturing operations, and specialized thermal battery and energetic device production facility are located in Arizona. The majority of our employees work there. Our test capabilities, including environmental, vibration, thermal vacuum, electromagnetic compatibility, and high-power electrical test facilities, are also concentrated in the state. This geographic concentration means that a single regional event could affect a large portion of our operations simultaneously.

Arizona experiences extreme summer heat, and periods of extreme heat have become more frequent and intense. Extreme heat can strain the electrical grid and increase the risk of power outages or rationing. It can also raise our facility cooling costs, affect the health and productivity of our workforce, and affect manufacturing processes and materials that are sensitive to temperature. The region also faces long-term water scarcity and reductions in allocations from regional water supplies. Those conditions could affect the availability and cost of water for our facilities, constrain the growth of the communities in which our employees live, and limit future expansion. Wildfires, monsoon storms, flash flooding, and dust storms could damage facilities, disrupt transportation, or interrupt utility service.

A significant disruption at one or more of our Arizona facilities could prevent us from producing products, conducting qualification testing, or meeting customer delivery schedules. Transferring production would be difficult and time-consuming, particularly for energetic devices, space hardware, and other products requiring specialized facilities, licenses, and customer qualification. Our business continuity plans and insurance may not be sufficient to prevent or compensate for these disruptions.

**We may not realize the expected benefits of our investments in manufacturing capacity, and capacity expansion projects may encounter delays and cost overruns.**

We are investing in manufacturing capacity, particularly in thermal batteries, energetic devices, and space power assemblies, where industry-wide capacity has been constrained. These investments are based on our assessment of the demand outlook and on customer commitments. Demand may not materialize as expected. Munitions replenishment demand may moderate once inventories are rebuilt. Constellation deployments may be delayed, and customers may qualify additional sources that compete for the same volumes.

Capacity expansion projects, especially those involving energetic materials facilities, require regulatory approvals, specialized construction, equipment procurement with long lead times, and customer qualification of new production lines. These projects may encounter delays, cost overruns, construction difficulties, or qualification setbacks. In some cases, customers or government agencies may provide funding for capacity investments, subject to conditions we may not be able to satisfy. If our capacity investments are not utilized at expected levels, we could incur underabsorbed overhead, impairment charges, and reduced returns on capital.

**We may be unable to attract, develop, and retain the highly skilled and security-cleared personnel our business requires.**

Our success depends on our ability to attract, develop, and retain qualified employees. These include engineers and scientists in electrical, mechanical, thermal, chemical, materials, systems, software, and manufacturing disciplines. They also include technicians skilled in electronics assembly, precision machining, energetic materials processing, and clean room operations, as well as program managers and quality and mission assurance specialists. Competition for these individuals is intense, particularly in the Phoenix metropolitan area. That area has experienced significant growth in semiconductor manufacturing, electric vehicle, and other technology industries that recruit from the same talent pool.

Many of our positions require U.S. citizenship because of security and export control requirements, and many require security clearances. These requirements substantially narrow the pool of eligible candidates. The process of obtaining clearances for new employees can take many months, during which those employees may be unable to perform certain work. Our competitors and customers actively recruit cleared personnel.

Certain disciplines in which we operate, such as thermal battery design and energetic materials processing, involve specialized expertise that is scarce and difficult to replicate. A meaningful portion of this expertise resides with experienced employees who may retire in the coming years. If we cannot transfer their knowledge effectively to newer employees, our ability to produce and improve these products could be impaired.

Wage inflation and competitive pressure for talent have increased our labor costs. Those costs may not be recoverable under fixed-price contracts. If we cannot attract and retain sufficient qualified personnel, we may be unable to perform on existing contracts, pursue growth opportunities, or maintain the quality and safety of our operations.

**Work stoppages, union organizing activities, or other labor disruptions could adversely affect our operations.**

Certain of our production employees at specific facilities are represented by labor unions under collective bargaining agreements. **The loss of key members of our management team could adversely affect our business.**

Our success depends in part on the continued service of our executive officers and other key personnel, including segment and program leaders. Many of these individuals have deep relationships with customers and extensive experience in our markets, and some hold security clearances that are difficult to replace. The loss of one or more key individuals, or our failure to implement effective succession plans, could disrupt our operations, impair customer relationships, and adversely affect our ability to execute our strategy. We do not maintain key person life insurance on our executive officers.

Our portfolio has been shaped in part by acquisitions of smaller, technology-focused businesses, and we continue to evaluate acquisition opportunities. Acquisitions involve numerous risks, including:

**We face intense competition from larger and better-resourced companies, from our own customers, and from new entrants. That competition could reduce our revenues and margins.**

The aerospace and defense industry is highly competitive. We compete with large, diversified aerospace and defense companies, some of which are also our customers. We also compete with specialized suppliers focused on particular product categories, with the internal capabilities of prime contractors and platform integrators, with government laboratories and depots in certain research and sustainment activities, and with emerging, often venture-backed, technology companies. Venture-backed entrants in directed energy, energy storage, and commercial space may pursue aggressive pricing or rapid innovation cycles. Technological change in our markets is rapid. Advances in energy storage chemistries, wide-bandgap semiconductors, thermal management, and power architectures could render our products less competitive or obsolete. If we fail to anticipate or respond to technological change, or if competitors develop superior products, our market share, revenues, and margins could decline.

**We may be unable to protect our intellectual property adequately, and we may be subject to claims that we infringe the intellectual property of others.**

We rely on a combination of patents, trade secrets, copyrights, trademarks, confidentiality agreements, and contractual provisions to protect our intellectual property. Much of our competitive advantage derives from proprietary know-how, manufacturing processes, design practices, qualification data, and flight and field heritage, which we protect principally as trade secrets. Trade secrets are difficult to protect. Employees, suppliers, customers, or others could misappropriate them or disclose them without authorization, including through cybersecurity breaches. Our patents may be challenged, invalidated, or circumvented, and our pending applications may not result in issued patents. Third parties may assert that our products or processes infringe their intellectual property rights. Defending such claims could be costly and time-consuming. If we were found to infringe, we could be required to pay damages, obtain licenses on unfavorable terms, or redesign our products. Although certain claims of patent infringement relating to products supplied to the U.S. Government may be directed against the government rather than the contractor under applicable law, we may have indemnification obligations to customers and may not be fully protected.

We rely on information technology systems to manage our engineering, manufacturing, supply chain, program management, financial reporting, and compliance activities. We depend on third-party providers for certain systems and services, and failures by those providers could affect our operations.

**Cybersecurity incidents could compromise sensitive information, disrupt our operations, and subject us to liability, regulatory consequences, and loss of eligibility for contracts.**

As a defense contractor, we are a frequent target of cyberattacks by sophisticated threat actors. These include nation-state actors, organized criminal groups, and hacktivists. These actors seek to gain unauthorized access to our information systems to steal classified information, controlled unclassified information, export-controlled technical data, and intellectual property, or to disrupt our operations. Threat techniques evolve continually and include phishing and social engineering, ransomware, exploitation of software vulnerabilities, supply chain compromises of software and hardware, insider threats, and attacks on operational technology used in our manufacturing processes. The increasing use of artificial intelligence by threat actors may increase the sophistication and scale of these attacks.

Our suppliers, many of which are small businesses with limited cybersecurity resources, and our customers face similar threats. A compromise of a supplier or customer could expose our information or disrupt our supply chain. We cannot fully control the cybersecurity practices of these third parties.

A successful cyberattack could result in:

* the theft or compromise of classified, controlled, or proprietary information;
* disruption of our engineering, manufacturing, and business operations;
* violations of export control and security regulations;
* loss of facility security clearances;
* claims by customers, employees, or other third parties;
* government investigations and penalties;
* remediation and recovery costs; and
* significant reputational harm.

Some incidents may go undetected for extended periods, and we may be unable to determine their full scope promptly. Our insurance may not cover all losses arising from cybersecurity incidents.

DFARS regulations require us to implement prescribed security controls to protect covered defense information, report cyber incidents within prescribed timeframes, and flow down these requirements to subcontractors. The Department of Defense is implementing the Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification program, which will require contractors to achieve specified certification levels as a condition of contract award. If we, or the businesses we acquire, fail to achieve and maintain required certifications, we could be ineligible for new contracts. Our suppliers' failure to achieve certification could also disrupt our supply chain. Alleged noncompliance with cybersecurity requirements may also give rise to claims under the False Claims Act. We have invested, and expect to continue to invest, in cybersecurity tools, personnel, and processes. These costs may not be fully recoverable. Additional information about our cybersecurity risk management is included in Item 1C, "Cybersecurity."

## Legal and Regulatory Risks

**Failure to comply with government procurement laws and regulations could result in civil, criminal, and administrative penalties, including suspension or debarment from government contracting.**

As a contractor and subcontractor to the U.S. Government, we must comply with extensive laws and regulations governing the formation, administration, and performance of government contracts. These include the Federal Acquisition Regulation, the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement, the Cost Accounting Standards, the Truthful Cost or Pricing Data statute, the False Claims Act, the Anti-Kickback Act, the Procurement Integrity Act, and regulations governing organizational conflicts of interest, gratuities, and the employment of former government personnel. These laws and regulations are complex and frequently change. Their interpretation may be uncertain.

Violations could result in fines and damages, which may be trebled under the False Claims Act. They could also lead to contract terminations, forfeiture of profits, suspension of payments, and suspension or debarment from future government contracting. Because a substantial portion of our revenues derives from U.S. Government programs, suspension or debarment of the Company or any significant business unit would have a material adverse effect on our business. The False Claims Act permits private individuals to bring qui tam actions on behalf of the government and to share in any recovery. Such actions may be brought by current or former employees and can result in substantial defense costs even when unfounded.

We are subject to mandatory disclosure obligations under the FAR when we have credible evidence of certain violations of law in connection with government contracts. Failure to make timely disclosure can itself result in suspension or debarment. Although we maintain a compliance program designed to prevent and detect violations, we cannot assure that our employees, agents, suppliers, or business partners will not engage in misconduct, or that our compliance program will detect all such conduct.

**Our failure to comply with export control and economic sanctions laws could result in significant penalties, loss of export privileges, and debarment.**

Many of our products, technologies, technical data, and services are subject to U.S. export control laws and regulations. These include the Arms Export Control Act and the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, and the Export Control Reform Act and the Export Administration Regulations. We are also subject to economic sanctions laws administered by the Department of the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control. These regulations restrict the export, re-export, and transfer of controlled items and technical data to foreign persons. That includes foreign national employees and visitors in the United States. In many cases, a license or other authorization is required before we may export products, provide defense services, or share technical data.

Export control compliance is complex. Our business involves extensive exchanges of technical data with customers, suppliers, universities, national laboratories, and international partners, many of which involve controlled information. An inadvertent unauthorized export or deemed export could occur despite our trade compliance program, which includes policies, procedures, training, classification and licensing processes, technology control plans, screening tools, and periodic audits. Violations can result in significant civil and criminal penalties, the imposition of special compliance officers or consent agreements, loss of export privileges, and debarment. They could also result in reputational harm.

Delays or denials in obtaining export licenses could prevent us from completing international sales or from collaborating with foreign partners. Changes in export control regulations could restrict our ability to sell products internationally or increase our compliance costs. Those changes include the addition of new controls on emerging technologies, such as advanced energy storage, power electronics, and hypersonic technologies, and changes in the countries to which exports are permitted.

**Violations of anti-corruption laws could result in significant penalties and reputational harm.**

We are subject to the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and similar anti-corruption laws in other jurisdictions where we do business. These laws generally prohibit companies and their intermediaries from making improper payments to government officials or others to obtain or retain business. The defense industry involves frequent interaction with government officials, and some of the countries in which we pursue business present elevated corruption risks. We may rely on foreign representatives, consultants, and partners, and we could be held liable for their conduct.

Although our code of conduct, anti-corruption policy, due diligence procedures, and training programs are designed to promote compliance, we cannot assure that our employees or intermediaries will not engage in prohibited conduct. Violations could result in substantial fines, criminal penalties, disgorgement of profits, debarment, and reputational harm. They could also result in the imposition of monitorships. Investigations of potential violations, even if no violation is ultimately found, can be costly and disruptive.

**We are subject to extensive environmental laws and regulations, and we may incur significant costs for compliance and remediation.**

Our operations are subject to federal, state, and local environmental laws and regulations. These govern, among other things, air and water discharges, the handling and disposal of hazardous materials and wastes, the remediation of contamination, and employee health and safety. Our operations involve the use of hazardous materials, including energetic materials, battery chemicals, solvents, and metals. We hold environmental permits for many of our facilities, and our failure to comply with permit conditions could result in penalties or operational restrictions.

We may be subject to liability for the investigation and remediation of contamination at properties we currently or formerly owned or operated, including properties associated with our legacy industrial and utility businesses and properties of businesses we have acquired. We may also be liable at third-party sites to which we sent wastes for disposal. Laws such as the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act and analogous state laws can impose joint and several liability without regard to fault. Contamination may be discovered in the future at sites where we have no current knowledge of environmental issues. Remediation costs could exceed our recorded liabilities, and some environmental costs may not be allowable or recoverable under our government contracts.

Regulatory attention to certain substances is increasing. These include per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, certain solvents, and other chemicals historically used in aerospace and defense manufacturing. New restrictions could limit our use of these substances, impose new remediation obligations, or expose us to claims for personal injury or property damage. They could also require us to qualify alternative materials. In aerospace and defense applications, qualifying alternative materials can require significant time, cost, and customer approval. Some substances subject to emerging restrictions may be embedded in components we purchase, and our suppliers may discontinue them. We cannot predict the timing or content of future regulations or their cost to us.

**The regulation of hazardous materials, energetics, and process safety could increase our costs and restrict our operations.**

The manufacture, storage, transportation, and handling of energetic materials, lithium-ion batteries, and other hazardous materials are subject to extensive regulation. Applicable regimes include explosives requirements administered by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, hazardous materials transportation regulations administered by the Department of Transportation, Department of Defense explosives safety requirements, OSHA process safety management standards, and state and local fire and building codes.

These requirements affect the design and siting of our facilities, the quantities of materials we may store, the methods by which we may ship products, and the qualifications of personnel who handle hazardous materials. Changes in these regulations, or in their interpretation or enforcement, could require costly facility modifications, limit production capacity, or restrict our ability to transport products to customers. The suspension or revocation of a required license or permit could halt production at the affected facility. Restrictions on air transportation of lithium-ion batteries and energetic devices could also increase shipping costs and lead times.

**Domestic sourcing requirements and restrictions on foreign-origin components could require costly changes to our supply chain and product designs.**

We are subject to laws and regulations that require or favor domestic sourcing of certain materials and products. These include the Buy American Act, specialty metals restrictions, and other domestic preference requirements in defense procurement statutes. We are also subject to restrictions on the use of certain telecommunications equipment, semiconductors, magnets, batteries, and other components originating from or produced by entities associated with certain foreign countries. These requirements are expanding, and their implementation schedules and scope have changed over time.

Compliance requires us to trace the origin of materials and components deep into our supply chain. That can be difficult, particularly for electronic components, rare earth magnet materials, and battery materials that pass through multiple tiers of suppliers. If we identify noncompliant materials in our products, we may be required to redesign products, requalify suppliers, seek waivers, or replace products already delivered. Waivers may not be available, and alternative domestic or allied sources may be more costly, less available, or not yet qualified. Noncompliance could result in contractual remedies, cost disallowances, False Claims Act liability, and harm to our customer relationships. Tariffs and other trade measures may also increase the cost of materials and components we import.

**Restrictions on foreign ownership, control, or influence could limit our access to capital and the market for our common stock.**

Our ability to maintain facility security clearances depends on our mitigation of any foreign ownership, control, or influence that could adversely affect the protection of classified information. Significant investments in our company by foreign persons could require mitigation measures, such as special security agreements or proxy arrangements, that may restrict the investor's rights. Such investments could also require review by the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States. These requirements may limit the pool of potential investors and acquirers for our company, may discourage certain investments or strategic transactions, and could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

**We are, and may become, subject to litigation, investigations, and other legal proceedings that could adversely affect our business.**

From time to time, we are involved in legal proceedings, claims, government investigations, and other matters arising in the ordinary course of business. These may include contract disputes, product liability and personal injury claims, employment matters, intellectual property disputes, environmental claims, and government inquiries. Litigation and investigations are inherently uncertain, and their outcomes cannot be predicted with assurance. An adverse outcome could require us to pay substantial damages, fines, or penalties, or to change our business practices. Even matters resolved in our favor can be costly, divert management attention, and harm our reputation. Our recorded accruals for legal matters may be insufficient to cover actual losses.

**Climate-related regulations, disclosure requirements, and stakeholder expectations could increase our costs and affect demand for our securities.**

We are subject to evolving climate-related and sustainability-related regulations and disclosure requirements at the federal, state, and international levels, and to related requirements in government procurement. These requirements may require us to measure and disclose greenhouse gas emissions, set reduction targets, assess climate-related risks, or modify our operations. Compliance could increase our costs, and inaccurate or incomplete disclosures could expose us to liability.

Some investors, lenders, and other stakeholders apply environmental, social, and governance criteria that exclude or limit investment in defense companies, particularly those producing munitions, energetic devices, or weapon system components. Other stakeholders have expressed opposition to the use of such criteria. Divergent expectations among stakeholders, and regulatory developments regarding the use of these criteria, could affect our access to capital, the market for our common stock, and our reputation. Our climate-related initiatives may not meet the expectations of all stakeholders.

**Changes in tax laws, or adverse outcomes in tax examinations, could increase our tax liabilities and adversely affect our cash flows.**

We are subject to income and other taxes in the United States at the federal, state, and local levels, and in certain foreign jurisdictions. Changes in tax laws, regulations, or their interpretation could increase our effective tax rate or our cash tax payments. This includes provisions requiring the capitalization and amortization of research and experimental expenditures rather than their immediate deduction. Given the significance of company-funded and customer-funded research and development to our business, rules governing the tax treatment of such expenditures are particularly relevant to our cash flows.

Our tax returns are subject to examination by tax authorities, which may disagree with positions we have taken. Adverse outcomes could result in additional taxes, interest, and penalties. Changes in our mix of earnings across jurisdictions, the valuation of deferred tax assets, and the availability of research and development tax credits could also affect our effective tax rate.

## Financial Risks

**Our liabilities are significant relative to our equity. Our obligations could limit our financial flexibility and increase our vulnerability to adverse developments.**

As of the end of fiscal year 2023, we had total assets of $4,049,858,186, total liabilities of $2,380,927,011, and total stockholders' equity of $1,668,931,175. Our total liabilities represented approximately 58.8% of total assets, and our ratio of total liabilities to total stockholders' equity was approximately 1.43 to 1. Our liabilities include obligations to suppliers and employees, contract liabilities such as customer advances and billings in excess of revenue recognized, accrued loss provisions, environmental and legal accruals, and other obligations, as well as any borrowings under financing arrangements.

The level and composition of our liabilities could have important consequences, including:

* requiring us to dedicate a portion of our cash flows to satisfying obligations, reducing funds available for research and development, capital expenditures, acquisitions, and returns to shareholders;
* limiting our ability to obtain additional financing on favorable terms;
* increasing our vulnerability to adverse economic, industry, or program-specific developments;
* limiting our flexibility to respond to changes in our business and markets; and
* placing us at a disadvantage relative to competitors with stronger balance sheets.

To the extent we have or incur indebtedness, the agreements governing it may contain covenants that restrict our ability to incur additional debt, grant liens, make investments, pay dividends, repurchase shares, or engage in certain transactions. A failure to comply with such covenants could result in a default that accelerates the repayment of our obligations. Our ability to meet our obligations depends on our future performance, which is subject to the risks described in this section and to economic and market conditions beyond our control.

**Fluctuations in working capital, the timing of contract payments, and changes in contract financing terms could adversely affect our cash flows and liquidity.**

Our cash flows depend significantly on the timing of contract milestones, deliveries, billings, and customer payments. Under many of our contracts, we incur significant costs before we can bill the customer. Costs may accumulate in inventory or contract assets for extended periods, particularly on development programs and long-lead production efforts. Supply chain conditions in recent periods have required us to hold higher inventory levels for certain programs, which has increased our working capital needs.

Our customers, including prime contractors, may delay payments, extend payment terms, or dispute invoices. Government payment withholds arising from business system deficiencies, audit findings, or contractual disputes could delay cash receipts. Changes in U.S. Government contract financing policies, including progress payment rates and performance-based payment terms, could increase our working capital requirements. Government shutdowns or other disruptions could delay payments. Any of these developments could adversely affect our liquidity and our ability to fund operations, investments, and other obligations.

**Our access to capital may be limited, and increases in interest rates could increase our borrowing costs.**

We may need to raise additional capital to fund capacity investments, acquisitions, working capital, or other needs. Our ability to obtain financing on acceptable terms depends on our financial performance, credit ratings, conditions in the credit and equity markets, and investor perceptions of the aerospace and defense industry. Disruptions in the financial markets, including those resulting from bank failures, monetary policy changes, or geopolitical events, could limit our access to capital or increase its cost. Increases in interest rates would increase the cost of any variable-rate borrowings and of any new borrowings we undertake. A downgrade in any credit ratings assigned to us could increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to capital.

**Inflation could adversely affect our margins, particularly on long-term fixed-price contracts.**

Recent periods have been characterized by elevated inflation in labor, materials, components, energy, freight, and other costs. Many of our long-term fixed-price contracts were priced before these increases and do not include economic price adjustment provisions. Even where escalation provisions exist, they may be based on indices that do not reflect our actual cost experience, or may adjust with a lag. On cost-reimbursable contracts, inflation may cause us to reach contract funding limits sooner and require additional customer funding that may not be forthcoming.

In addition, U.S. Government budgets are generally established in nominal terms. As a result, inflation may reduce the real purchasing power of defense appropriations and the quantities of products customers can procure. If inflation persists or increases, our margins and results of operations could be adversely affected.

**We may be required to recognize impairment charges for goodwill and other intangible assets.**

We have acquired a number of businesses, and our balance sheet includes goodwill and other intangible assets associated with those acquisitions. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. We review other long-lived assets for impairment when indicators exist.

Events that could result in impairment include declines in projected revenues or profitability, loss of significant programs or customers, adverse changes in the business climate, increases in discount rates, and sustained declines in our market capitalization. Impairment charges could be material to our results of operations in the period recognized and would reduce our stockholders' equity, which was $1,668,931,175 at the end of fiscal year 2023.

**Our results are subject to significant accounting estimates and judgments. Changes in accounting standards could adversely affect our reported results.**

The preparation of our consolidated financial statements requires us to make estimates and assumptions that affect reported amounts. Those estimates include revenue recognized over time, estimated costs at completion, loss provisions, variable consideration, inventory valuation, environmental and legal liabilities, the valuation of goodwill and intangible assets, income taxes, and stock-based compensation. Actual results may differ materially from our estimates. Changes in accounting standards issued by the Financial Accounting Standards Board or the SEC, or changes in their interpretation, could also require changes in our accounting policies, systems, and processes and could adversely affect our reported results.

**If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, we may be unable to report our financial results accurately or timely, and investor confidence could be harmed.**

We are required to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting and to report on its effectiveness annually. Our independent registered public accounting firm is required to attest to the effectiveness of our internal control over financial reporting. Our business involves complex contract accounting, multiple segments and facilities, acquired businesses with varying control environments, and enterprise system changes. These factors increase the risk of control deficiencies.

If we identify a material weakness, or if our auditors are unable to attest to the effectiveness of our internal controls, we could be required to restate our financial statements. Investors could lose confidence in our financial reporting, and the market price of our common stock could decline. We could also become subject to regulatory investigations or litigation.

## Risks Related to Our Common Stock

**The market price of our common stock may be volatile, and our quarterly results may fluctuate significantly.**

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors, some of which are beyond our control. These include:

* actual or anticipated variations in our quarterly or annual results;
* changes in estimates at completion, loss provisions, or other adjustments on significant programs;
* contract awards, losses, terminations, or protests;
* U.S. Government budget developments, continuing resolutions, and shutdowns;
* changes in financial guidance or in securities analysts' estimates and recommendations;
* announcements by us, our customers, or our competitors of significant contracts, acquisitions, or technological developments;
* product failures, safety incidents, or cybersecurity incidents;
* legal or regulatory developments;
* changes in market valuations of aerospace and defense companies; and
* general economic, political, and market conditions.

Our quarterly results can fluctuate because of the timing of contract awards, funding actions, deliveries, program milestones, and cumulative catch-up adjustments. Award and funding activity is sometimes concentrated near the end of the U.S. Government's fiscal year on September 30. As a result, period-to-period comparisons of our results may not be meaningful and should not be relied on as indicators of future performance.

Our corporate name includes the words "Energy Group," reflecting our origins and our technical focus on energy systems for aerospace and defense platforms. Some market participants, data providers, or index methodologies could associate our name with the energy sector rather than with the aerospace and defense industry in which we operate. Any resulting confusion could affect the investor base for our common stock and its trading characteristics.

**We may not return capital to shareholders, and any capital returns may be reduced or discontinued.**

Our capital allocation priorities emphasize investments in organic growth, including research and development, capacity, and capability enhancements. We also consider targeted acquisitions and returning capital to shareholders when appropriate. Any decision to pay dividends or repurchase shares will depend on our results of operations, cash flows, financial condition, capital requirements, contractual restrictions, and other factors our Board of Directors considers relevant. We cannot assure that we will return capital to shareholders in any particular amount or at all. Any capital return programs we adopt may be suspended, reduced, or terminated at any time.

**Provisions in our governing documents and applicable law, together with security-related restrictions, could discourage or prevent a change in control.**

Provisions of our certificate of incorporation and bylaws, and of applicable state corporate law, could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us, even if a change in control would benefit our shareholders. These provisions may include advance notice requirements for shareholder proposals and director nominations, the authority of our Board of Directors to issue preferred stock without shareholder approval, and restrictions on certain business combinations with interested shareholders.

In addition, as described above, the foreign ownership, control, or influence requirements applicable to holders of facility security clearances, and potential review by the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, could limit the universe of potential acquirers. Antitrust review of combinations in the aerospace and defense industry, and the U.S. Government's interest in preserving competition within the defense industrial base, could further limit potential transactions. These factors could depress the market price of our common stock.

**Future issuances of our common stock or securities convertible into common stock could dilute existing shareholders.**

We may issue additional shares of common stock or securities convertible into common stock to raise capital, to fund acquisitions, or under our equity incentive plans. Such issuances could dilute the ownership interests of existing shareholders and could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

## General Risks

**Geopolitical events, armed conflicts, and international tensions could disrupt our operations and supply chain, even as they affect demand for our products.**

Recent geopolitical events, including armed conflicts and heightened tensions among major powers, have increased attention to munitions stockpiles, missile defense, and industrial base capacity. They have contributed to demand for certain of our products. However, such demand may not be sustained. Conflicts may end, priorities may shift, or allied governments may direct funds toward domestic suppliers.

At the same time, geopolitical events can disrupt our operations. They can constrain the availability of critical minerals, rare earth materials, and electronic components, increase energy and commodity prices, and increase cybersecurity threats. They can also result in sanctions, export restrictions, and trade measures that affect our suppliers and customers. Rapid increases in demand driven by geopolitical events may strain our capacity and that of our suppliers. That strain could lead to delivery delays, cost growth, and customer dissatisfaction.

**Public health crises, pandemics, and similar events could disrupt our operations, supply chain, and customer demand.**

Public health crises could cause workforce absences, facility closures, and supply chain disruptions. They could also lead to government-mandated restrictions, reduced commercial air travel, and delays in government contracting activities. Past public health crises have significantly affected commercial aerospace demand and global supply chains. A future crisis could adversely affect our APS segment's commercial business and our operations generally. It could also increase our costs in ways that may not be recoverable under fixed-price contracts.

**Adverse economic conditions could adversely affect our business.**

Economic downturns, recessions, inflation, high interest rates, and financial market volatility could reduce demand for our commercial aerospace and commercial space products. They could also impair the financial condition of our suppliers and customers and increase competition for government business. In addition, they could limit our access to capital. Weak economic conditions may also increase pressure on government budgets, including defense budgets.

**Damage to our reputation could adversely affect our business.**

Our reputation for technical excellence, reliability, safety, integrity, and compliance is important to our relationships with customers, employees, suppliers, regulators, and investors. Our reputation could be harmed by any of the risks described in this section. These include product failures, safety incidents, cybersecurity breaches, compliance violations, program execution problems, litigation, and negative publicity, including publicity on social media regarding defense industry activities. Damage to our reputation could impair our ability to win contracts, attract and retain employees, and access capital. Restoring a damaged reputation can take substantial time and resources.

**Our business could be adversely affected by risks associated with the development and use of artificial intelligence and other emerging technologies.**

We are evaluating and, in certain cases, using artificial intelligence and machine learning tools in engineering, manufacturing, and business processes. The use of these technologies presents risks. Tools may produce inaccurate or biased outputs, may expose proprietary or export-controlled information if not properly controlled, and may be subject to evolving legal and regulatory requirements. Competitors that adopt these technologies more effectively may gain advantages in design speed, cost, or product performance. Customers may also impose new requirements regarding the use of artificial intelligence in defense programs. If we fail to manage these risks or to adopt emerging technologies effectively, our competitive position could be adversely affected.
