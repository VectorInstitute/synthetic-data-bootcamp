## Overview

### Our Business

Riverton Energy Group, Inc. is an aerospace and defense technology company headquartered in Arizona. We design, develop, manufacture, integrate, and sustain energy-centric systems for military, civil government, space, and commercial aerospace customers. Our products generate, store, condition, distribute, and manage electrical, thermal, and propulsive energy aboard aircraft, missiles, munitions, ground vehicles, naval vessels, and spacecraft. We also provide the engineering, test, aftermarket, and lifecycle support services that keep those systems ready for operation.

We operate through three reportable segments:

* **Aerospace Power Systems ("APS")** supplies electrical power generation, conversion, distribution, and energy storage equipment for military and commercial aircraft, together with related aftermarket spares, repair, and overhaul services.
* **Defense Energy Systems ("DES")** supplies power, energy storage, thermal management, and pulsed power subsystems for tactical missiles, munitions, interceptors, ground and naval platforms, and directed energy weapons. It also produces thermal batteries, gas generators, and other energetic devices.
* **Space & Advanced Technologies ("SAT")** develops and produces spacecraft and launch vehicle power systems and conducts advanced research and development in hypersonics, compact energy storage, and next-generation power architectures.

A substantial portion of our revenues derives, directly or indirectly, from programs funded by the U.S. Government, principally the Department of Defense. In most cases we participate in those programs as a subcontractor or supplier to large prime contractors. We also serve civil space agencies, commercial aerospace original equipment manufacturers and operators, commercial satellite manufacturers and operators, and, subject to U.S. export controls, allied foreign governments and international prime contractors.

Our business has several characteristics that shape our financial results:

* **Long program lifecycles.** Many of our products are designed into platforms during development and are then produced and supported for many years. We often invest in development-phase pursuits, sometimes at modest or negative margins, in anticipation of production and sustainment revenues that follow.
* **Significant contract accounting judgment.** We recognize a significant portion of our revenues over time using estimates of total contract costs. Changes in those estimates can materially affect our results in any period.
* **Substantial investment in technology.** We fund a significant level of independent research and development ("IR&D"). We also perform customer-funded research and development, the revenues and costs of which are included in revenues and cost of revenue.
* **A mix of contract types.** Most of our production and aftermarket work, and many of our development contracts with prime contractors, are fixed-price. Research and early-stage development work is more often cost-reimbursable. This mix affects our exposure to cost growth and our margin profile.
* **Dependence on government budgets.** Our results are influenced by U.S. Government appropriations, the timing of funding actions, and the procurement decisions of the prime contractors we supply.

### Management's Priorities

Our strategy, described in Item 1, is to be the leading independent provider of energy generation, storage, conditioning, and thermal management solutions for aerospace and defense platforms. Management's operating priorities for the coming year are consistent with that strategy and with the conditions we experienced in fiscal year 2023:

* **Execute on our programs.** We are focused on meeting cost, schedule, and technical commitments on our fixed-price development and production contracts. We also continue to strengthen our estimating, earned value management, and program review disciplines. Consistent execution protects our margins and is essential to winning follow-on work.
* **Convert demand into capacity and deliveries.** Demand for thermal batteries, energetic devices, and space power assemblies has increased with munitions replenishment and the proliferation of satellite constellations. We intend to continue investing in capacity and automation where customer commitments and the demand outlook justify the investment.
* **Transition emerging technologies toward production.** Our directed energy, thermal management, and hypersonic activities are currently weighted toward development, prototyping, and flight test. We are working to position our PulseBank™ and ThermAxis™ product families and our hypersonic power and thermal solutions for the programs of record we expect to emerge.
* **Strengthen supply chain resilience.** We will continue to qualify alternative sources, increase domestic sourcing of critical materials, maintain targeted safety stock, and support key suppliers. Our objective is to reduce delivery risk while managing the working capital these measures require.
* **Maintain financial strength and flexibility.** We ended fiscal year 2023 with $740,770,190 of cash and cash equivalents and no short-term debt. We intend to preserve a strong balance sheet that allows us to fund organic investment, pursue disciplined acquisitions, and respond to changes in our markets.

These priorities are forward-looking and subject to the risks described in Item 1A. We cannot assure that we will achieve them.

## Business Environment

Our financial performance is influenced by conditions in the U.S. defense and civil government budget environment, the end markets we serve, the global supply chain, the labor market, and the regulatory framework applicable to government contractors. This section summarizes the conditions that affected fiscal year 2023 and that we expect to remain relevant. Additional information is included in Item 1 and Item 1A.

### U.S. Government Budget and Appropriations

U.S. Government spending on defense, space, and homeland security is the most significant external driver of our business. Defense priorities continue to emphasize capabilities that align with our portfolio, including:

* munitions and missile production;
* integrated air and missile defense;
* directed energy;
* hypersonic offensive and defensive systems;
* resilient and proliferated space architectures; and
* the modernization of aircraft, ground vehicle, and naval fleets.

At the same time, the appropriations process remains a source of uncertainty. Congress frequently funds the government through continuing resolutions at the start of the government's fiscal year. Under a continuing resolution, agencies generally may not initiate new programs or increase production rates above prior-year levels. Extended continuing resolutions can delay new program starts and production rate increases, cause prime contractors to defer orders, and compress award activity into shorter periods. These effects can shift the timing of our revenues and cash flows between quarters and, in some cases, between fiscal years.

The potential for government shutdowns, debt limit impasses, and statutory spending caps also creates uncertainty about the timing and level of future funding. Even when overall defense funding is maintained or increased, allocations may not favor the specific platforms and capability areas we serve. We monitor budget developments closely and incorporate them into our planning. However, we cannot predict with certainty the timing or level of future appropriations.

### Demand Trends in Our Markets

**Munitions and missile power.** Recent geopolitical events have heightened the focus of the U.S. Government and allied governments on munitions inventories and the industrial base's capacity to replenish them. The products of our DES segment include thermal batteries, gas generators, initiators, and missile power electronics. These are specialized components that must be qualified for each application and are often produced on a sole-source or limited-source basis. Demand for these products has been supported by efforts to expand munitions production. That demand has also placed pressure on our production capacity and that of our suppliers, and it has informed our capacity investment decisions.

**Directed energy.** The Department of Defense continues to fund the development and prototyping of high-energy laser and high-power microwave systems. Power and thermal subsystems are frequently among the most significant technical challenges in fielding these systems. Our directed energy work remains weighted toward customer-funded development and prototyping, much of it under cost-reimbursable contracts, together with company-funded IR&D. The timing and scale of transitions to production programs of record remain uncertain.

**Aircraft electrification and modernization.** Military aircraft are carrying more power-intensive mission systems. New aircraft and major upgrades are increasingly specifying higher electrical power and more sophisticated power management. These trends support demand for the generation, conversion, distribution, and storage products of our APS segment. Military aftermarket demand reflects fleet sizes, flight hours, and depot maintenance funding. Commercial aerospace demand reflects business jet, regional aircraft, and rotorcraft production rates, together with aircraft utilization.

**Space.** Civil, military, and commercial operators continue to deploy satellites, particularly in low Earth orbit. The national security space community is fielding proliferated constellations for missile warning, missile tracking, and data transport. These markets require higher production rates and lower unit costs than traditional, bespoke spacecraft. Government customers also continue to procure high-reliability power systems for exquisite national security and science missions. Our SAT segment is investing to serve both ends of this market. The commercial portion of this market depends in part on our customers' access to capital, and constellation deployment schedules can shift.

**Hypersonics.** Government investment in hypersonic offensive and defensive capabilities supports demand for specialized power, thermal, and test capabilities. Our hypersonic activities are concentrated in flight test campaigns and developmental programs.

**Ground vehicle and naval power.** Ground forces increasingly depend on electrical power for sensors, communications, protection systems, and, in some cases, hybrid-electric propulsion. Naval surface vessels are adopting high-power mission systems that require energy storage and power conversion. These trends support demand for DES vehicle, expeditionary, and naval power products.

### Supply Chain, Inflation, and Labor

The aerospace and defense supply chain remained challenged in fiscal year 2023. As described in Item 1, we experienced:

* extended lead times for electronic components, castings, and forgings;
* supplier capacity constraints and labor shortages at suppliers;
* inflationary pressures on materials, components, and freight; and
* quality escapes at certain suppliers.

These conditions affected our operations through delayed deliveries of certain components and increased expediting costs. They also required us to hold higher inventory levels for certain programs. At the end of fiscal year 2023, inventory was $274,888,695, equivalent to approximately 41.7% of our annual cost of revenue.

We responded by increasing supplier monitoring and on-site supplier development support, placing longer-term purchase commitments for constrained materials, qualifying alternative sources, establishing safety stock for selected critical items, and redesigning certain products to accommodate more readily available components. These measures reduce delivery risk. They also increase our costs and working capital requirements.

Inflation in labor, materials, and energy costs affects our margins, particularly on long-term fixed-price contracts priced before recent cost increases that lack economic price adjustment provisions. We seek to mitigate this exposure through:

* pricing discipline on new contracts;
* escalation clauses where available;
* long-term supply agreements; and
* productivity and cost-reduction initiatives under our enterprise operating system.

Competition for engineers, technicians, and security-cleared professionals remains intense, particularly in the Phoenix metropolitan area. Wage pressure has increased our labor costs. Those costs are not always recoverable under fixed-price contracts.

### Tax Legislation

U.S. tax law requires the capitalization and amortization of research and experimental expenditures for tax purposes rather than their immediate deduction. Given the significance of company-funded and customer-funded research and development to our business, this requirement affects the timing of our cash tax payments. We continue to monitor proposed legislative changes that could alter this treatment. We also monitor the availability of research and development tax credits and other provisions that affect our effective tax rate.

### Geopolitical Conditions

Armed conflicts and heightened international tensions have increased attention to munitions stockpiles, missile defense, and industrial base capacity. These developments have contributed to demand for certain of our products. They have also contributed to constraints on critical minerals, rare earth materials, and electronic components, to elevated cybersecurity threats, and to new sanctions and export restrictions. Demand driven by geopolitical events may not be sustained. Rapid increases in demand can also strain our capacity and that of our suppliers.

## How We Evaluate Our Performance

Management uses a combination of financial and operational measures to evaluate our performance, allocate resources, and make decisions. The principal measures are described below.

**Revenues.** Revenues reflect our progress on contracts accounted for over time and our deliveries of products and services accounted for at a point in time. We evaluate revenues by segment, customer type, contract type, and program phase. We also evaluate the conversion of backlog into revenues and the mix between development, production, and aftermarket work.

**Gross profit and gross margin.** Gross profit reflects revenues less cost of revenue. Gross margin is affected by contract mix, program maturity, changes in estimated costs at completion, the absorption of manufacturing overhead, and our cost classification practices.

**Operating income and operating margin.** Operating income is our primary measure of consolidated profitability. Segment operating income is the measure our chief operating decision maker uses to evaluate segment performance and allocate resources.

**Research and development investment.** We evaluate company-funded research and development expense in absolute terms and as a percentage of revenues. We also assess the progress of our IR&D portfolio against technical milestones and customer opportunities.

**Program performance.** We conduct regular reviews of program cost, schedule, and technical performance at the program, segment, and enterprise levels. These reviews include analyses of estimates at completion, earned value metrics, risks and opportunities, and changes in contract estimates.

**Cash flow.** We evaluate net cash provided by operating activities and free cash flow, a non-GAAP measure, as indicators of our ability to fund investment, meet obligations, and return capital to shareholders. We also monitor working capital, including inventory, contract assets, contract liabilities, receivables, and payables.

**Backlog and orders.** Backlog and order activity, including the ratio of orders to revenues, indicate the near- and medium-term demand for our products and services.

**Return on investment.** We evaluate returns on capital deployed, including capital expenditures, IR&D, and acquisitions, relative to our expectations and cost of capital.

### Revenues

Revenues for fiscal year 2023 were $1,982,571,143. We generate revenues from several sources:

* production of power, energy storage, thermal management, and energetic products for military and commercial platforms;
* customer-funded development and research contracts;
* aftermarket spares, repair, overhaul, and sustainment services; and
* engineering, test, and technical support services.

**Revenue recognition pattern.** A significant portion of our revenues is recognized over time as we perform. We generally measure progress using costs incurred relative to total estimated costs at completion. This approach applies to most of our U.S. Government-related contracts and subcontracts, including fixed-price and cost-reimbursable development and production contracts. The customer typically controls the work in process as it is performed, or our products have no alternative use and we have an enforceable right to payment for performance completed. Revenues on certain commercial products, spare parts, and other standard products are recognized at a point in time, generally upon shipment or delivery.

Because we recognize much of our revenue over time, revenues in any period reflect the volume of work performed on our contracts and the costs we incur, including materials, subcontracted components, labor, and allocable overhead. Revenues are therefore influenced by the timing of material receipts from suppliers, labor availability, and program phase. They are also influenced by changes in estimated revenues and costs at completion, which we recognize on a cumulative catch-up basis.

**Factors affecting fiscal year 2023 revenues.** Our fiscal year 2023 revenues reflected the following factors:

* **Munitions and missile demand.** Demand for DES thermal batteries, energetic devices, and missile power electronics supported production activity. Our ability to convert that demand into revenue was influenced by our production capacity, supplier deliveries of constrained materials and components, and the timing of customer funding and orders.
* **Development and prototyping activity.** Customer-funded development work in directed energy, thermal management, hypersonics, advanced energy storage, and next-generation aircraft power architectures contributed to revenues across our segments. Much of this work is performed under cost-reimbursable contracts. The associated revenues therefore tend to track the level of program effort and funding.
* **Space power programs.** SAT revenues reflected work on high-reliability national security and civil space programs and on standardized products for proliferated low Earth orbit constellations. Revenues from constellation programs depend on customer deployment schedules and our production ramp.
* **Aircraft power and aftermarket.** APS revenues reflected production for military and commercial aircraft programs, development work on higher-power architectures, and aftermarket demand driven by fleet utilization and depot maintenance activity.
* **Supply chain conditions.** Delayed deliveries of certain components affected the timing of work performed and products delivered on some programs. Because we generally measure progress based on costs incurred, delays in material receipts can shift revenue recognition into later periods.

**Customer and contract mix.** A substantial portion of our revenues derives, directly or indirectly, from the U.S. Government, primarily through subcontracts with prime contractors. We also derive revenues from commercial aerospace and commercial space customers and from international customers. Our revenues by customer type, contract type, and segment, and information about customers that individually accounted for a significant portion of our revenues, are presented in the revenue and segment notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

Our contract mix affects both our revenue pattern and our risk profile. Fixed-price contracts expose us to the risk of cost overruns and give us the opportunity to benefit from cost underruns. Cost-reimbursable contracts generally carry lower margins but limit our exposure to cost growth. Time-and-materials contracts, which we use principally for certain engineering support, repair, and technical services, fall between these two in risk. Development-phase work tends to involve a higher proportion of cost-reimbursable contracts. However, prime contractors increasingly seek fixed-price terms for development work. Production and aftermarket work is predominantly fixed-price.

### Cost of Revenue and Gross Profit

Cost of revenue for fiscal year 2023 was $659,643,059, or 33.3% of revenues. Gross profit was $1,322,928,084, a gross margin of 66.7%.

Cost of revenue includes:

* direct materials and purchased components;
* subcontracted work;
* direct labor;
* manufacturing and engineering overhead allocated to contracts;
* the costs of customer-funded research and development; and
* provisions for anticipated losses on contracts and changes in estimated costs at completion.

Our gross margin reflects our cost classification practices. Under those practices, a significant portion of our indirect costs, including certain general, administrative, and other operating costs, is reported in operating expense line items rather than in cost of revenue. Company-funded research and development is also reported as a separate line item. Comparisons of our gross margin with those of other aerospace and defense companies may therefore not be meaningful. Management evaluates profitability primarily at the operating income level.

Factors that influenced our cost of revenue and gross margin in fiscal year 2023 included:

* **Contract and program mix.** Mature production programs and aftermarket work generally carry higher margins than development programs. Development programs may be priced at modest or negative margins when we pursue early design-in positions. Our mix of these activities affects consolidated gross margin.
* **Changes in contract estimates.** We review estimates of revenues and costs at completion regularly. We recognize changes on a cumulative catch-up basis. Favorable changes may result from improved productivity, retirement of technical or schedule risks, and lower-than-expected costs. Unfavorable changes may result from technical challenges, supplier issues, labor cost growth, and schedule delays.
* **Supply chain and inflation.** Expediting costs, premium freight, higher material and component prices, and the cost of qualifying alternative sources increased our costs on certain programs. Under fixed-price contracts without economic price adjustment provisions, we generally absorbed these increases.
* **Labor costs.** Wage inflation and competition for skilled technicians and engineers increased our labor costs.
* **Manufacturing efficiency.** Our enterprise operating system emphasizes standard work, root cause problem solving, and continuous improvement. Together with targeted automation investments, these practices helped offset some of the cost pressures described above.

### Research and Development Expenses

Company-funded research and development expense for fiscal year 2023 was $403,165,603, or 20.3% of revenues. This amount was approximately 2.0 times our operating income for the year. It reflects our view that technology leadership is central to our competitive position and that sustained investment is required to win positions early in the platform lifecycle.

Our fiscal year 2023 IR&D investments focused on the areas described in Item 1, including:

* high-energy-density and high-power-density energy storage, including advanced cell chemistries and battery packaging;
* wide-bandgap semiconductor power electronics;
* pulsed power architectures and energy magazines for directed energy weapons;
* two-phase cooling, thermal energy storage, and other advanced thermal management technologies;
* high-voltage electrical architectures for aircraft and vehicles;
* radiation-tolerant electronics and standardized space power products suitable for higher-rate production;
* energetic materials and thermal battery designs that improve performance, safety, and manufacturability; and
* digital engineering, simulation, and automated test capabilities.

Research and development expense excludes customer-funded research and development. The revenues and costs of customer-funded work are included in revenues and cost of revenue, respectively. Customer-funded research complements our IR&D by allowing us to mature technologies with customer support. In combination, these two channels represent our total research and development effort.

A portion of our IR&D costs is allowable and may be recovered through indirect cost rates on our U.S. Government contracts. As a result, the economic cost of our IR&D investment to the Company may be lower than the amount reported as research and development expense.

We evaluate our IR&D portfolio regularly. We direct resources toward opportunities with the greatest expected strategic and financial return and away from efforts that are not progressing or no longer align with customer priorities. We expect to continue investing in research and development at levels we believe are appropriate to support our strategy. The amount and timing of those investments will depend on customer opportunities, technical progress, and our financial performance.

### Income Taxes

We recorded an income tax benefit of $6,637,247 for fiscal year 2023 on income before income taxes of $203,907,274. This resulted in an effective tax rate of negative 3.3%.

Applying the U.S. federal statutory rate of 21% to our pretax income would have produced tax expense of approximately $42.8 million. Our recorded tax benefit was therefore approximately $49.5 million more favorable than tax computed at the federal statutory rate. The difference reflects items that more than offset tax at statutory rates, including research and development tax credits and other favorable items, partially offset by state income taxes and nondeductible items. The principal items reconciling our effective tax rate to the statutory rate are presented in the income tax note to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

Our effective tax rate can vary significantly from period to period. Factors that can cause variation include:

* the level of pretax income;
* the amount of research and development tax credits we generate and can utilize;
* changes in the valuation of deferred tax assets, including changes in valuation allowances;
* the tax effects of stock-based compensation;
* the resolution of tax examinations and changes in uncertain tax positions;
* changes in tax laws, regulations, and interpretations; and
* discrete items in a particular period.

We do not expect our fiscal year 2023 effective tax rate to be indicative of future periods. Our effective tax rate may increase in future periods if the favorable items that affected fiscal year 2023 do not recur at similar levels. As an illustration, each one percentage point change in our effective tax rate, applied to fiscal year 2023 pretax income, would change income tax expense by approximately $2.0 million.

The capitalization and amortization of research and experimental expenditures for tax purposes affects the timing of our cash tax payments. It does not affect our total income tax expense, because the resulting deferred tax assets reverse over the amortization period. Our cash tax payments may therefore differ from our income tax expense.

### Changes in Contract Estimates

As described under "Critical Accounting Estimates" below, we recognize changes in estimated revenues and costs at completion on contracts accounted for over time on a cumulative catch-up basis. Changes in these estimates can favorably or unfavorably affect our results. Such changes arise from many factors, including:

* improvements or deterioration in labor productivity and manufacturing yields;
* the resolution or emergence of technical and schedule risks;
* changes in material, component, and labor costs;
* supplier performance;
* contract modifications, claims, and changes in variable consideration such as award and incentive fees; and
* changes in estimated quantities and delivery schedules.

When the estimated total costs of a contract exceed its estimated total revenues, we recognize the entire anticipated loss in the period in which it becomes evident. The aggregate effect of net cumulative catch-up adjustments on our fiscal year 2023 operating income, and information about significant individual adjustments and loss provisions, is presented in the revenue recognition note to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

We continue to pay particular attention to fixed-price development contracts. Those contracts involve technologies that may not previously have been produced at the required performance levels or in the required configurations, including high-power generation architectures, pulsed power subsystems, advanced thermal management systems, hypersonic power solutions, and space-qualified electronics. Technical problems that emerge during design, integration, or qualification testing could require redesign, additional testing, and schedule extensions. They could also result in unfavorable estimate changes or loss provisions in future periods.

## Segment Discussion

We manage our business through three reportable segments: Aerospace Power Systems, Defense Energy Systems, and Space & Advanced Technologies. Our chief operating decision maker evaluates segment performance and allocates resources based principally on segment revenues and segment operating income. Segment operating income excludes certain corporate costs and other items that management does not consider in evaluating segment performance.

Our segments share common engineering disciplines, manufacturing processes, quality systems, and supply chains. We have consolidated common functions, including supply chain management, quality assurance, information technology, security, and finance, into shared service organizations. The costs of those organizations are allocated to the segments using methodologies we believe are reasonable.

Revenues and operating income for each segment, and a reconciliation of segment operating income to consolidated operating income, are presented in the segment reporting note to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8. The following discussion describes the principal activities, demand drivers, and performance factors for each segment in fiscal year 2023.

### Aerospace Power Systems

**Business activities.** APS designs, manufactures, and supports electrical power systems and components for military and commercial aircraft. Its product lines include:

* engine-driven and auxiliary-power-unit-driven generators and starter-generators;
* power conversion and conditioning equipment;
* our VoltSpan™ family of primary and secondary power distribution units and solid-state power controllers;
* our CoreCell™ family of aircraft batteries and battery management systems; and
* aftermarket spares, repair, overhaul, and technical support.

**Revenue drivers.** APS revenues are generated under long-term production agreements with airframe and engine prime contractors, development contracts for new platforms and upgrades, and aftermarket purchase orders and service agreements. Production revenues depend on aircraft build rates and the timing of deliveries to prime contractors. Military aftermarket revenues depend on fleet sizes, flight hours, and depot maintenance funding. Commercial aftermarket revenues depend on aircraft utilization and fleet age. Development revenues reflect the level of engineering effort on new aircraft and upgrade programs, which are increasingly specifying higher electrical power and more sophisticated power management.

**Performance factors in fiscal year 2023.** APS results in fiscal year 2023 were influenced by:

* production volumes on military and commercial aircraft programs, which depended in part on the timing of supplier deliveries of castings, forgings, rare earth magnet materials, and electronic components;
* aftermarket demand for spares and repair services, which generally carry higher margins than original equipment production;
* development activity on higher-power generation and high-voltage distribution architectures, which supports future production positions but generally carries lower margins than mature production;
* inflation in material and labor costs, particularly on long-term commercial supply agreements with multi-year pricing commitments; and
* continued investment in wide-bandgap power conversion, solid-state power distribution, and battery safety technologies.

**Outlook considerations.** We expect APS performance to depend on aircraft production rates, the timing of next-generation military aircraft and upgrade programs, our ability to expand solid-state power distribution products into retrofit markets, and the growth of our aftermarket business through expanded repair capabilities and long-term sustainment agreements. Commercial aerospace demand remains subject to economic conditions and air travel demand.

### Defense Energy Systems

**Business activities.** DES supplies power, energy storage, thermal management, and pulsed power subsystems for tactical missiles, munitions, interceptors, ground vehicles, naval platforms, and directed energy weapons. Its product lines include:

* thermal batteries;
* gas generators, squibs, initiators, and pyrotechnic actuators;
* missile and munition power electronics;
* our PulseBank™ family of pulsed power and energy storage systems;
* our ThermAxis™ family of thermal management systems; and
* vehicle, expeditionary, and naval power and energy storage products.

**Revenue drivers.** The DES production business is characterized by multi-year programs with relatively stable demand once a product is qualified. Demand has increased with U.S. and allied efforts to replenish and expand munitions inventories. DES production revenues depend on munitions and missile production rates, our production capacity, and the availability of specialized materials. The directed energy and thermal management business is weighted toward development and prototyping, frequently under cost-reimbursable contracts, and its revenues track the level of customer funding and program effort.

**Performance factors in fiscal year 2023.** DES results in fiscal year 2023 were influenced by:

* production volumes of thermal batteries and energetic devices supporting munitions, missile, and interceptor programs;
* capacity constraints at our specialized energetic materials production facility in Arizona, and our investments in capacity and automation to address them;
* the availability of specialized thermal battery and energetic materials, some of which are available from a single or limited number of qualified suppliers;
* the level of customer-funded development and prototyping activity in directed energy, thermal management, and vehicle power, which generally carries lower margins than production work; and
* the costs of maintaining our process safety management systems and regulatory licenses for energetic materials operations.

**Outlook considerations.** We expect DES performance to depend on sustained munitions and missile production demand, our ability to bring additional thermal battery and energetic device capacity online and qualify it with customers, and the pace at which directed energy systems transition from prototypes to programs of record. Capacity expansion projects involving energetic materials require regulatory approvals, specialized construction, long-lead equipment, and customer qualification. These projects may encounter delays or cost overruns.

### Space & Advanced Technologies

**Business activities.** SAT develops, manufactures, and integrates power systems for spacecraft and launch vehicles. It also conducts advanced research and development. Its product lines include:

* spacecraft power management and distribution electronics and solar array regulators;
* space-qualified battery assemblies;
* launch vehicle and upper stage power products;
* hypersonic power and thermal solutions; and
* customer-funded advanced research in energy storage, compact power generation, wide-bandgap and radiation-hardened electronics, and power beaming concepts.

**Revenue drivers.** SAT national security and civil space revenues are generated primarily through low-volume, high-reliability products, often under fixed-price contracts with significant engineering content. Commercial and proliferated constellation revenues involve higher volumes and greater emphasis on unit cost and delivery speed. Advanced research revenues are generally earned under cost-reimbursable contracts and cooperative agreements with government research organizations.

**Performance factors in fiscal year 2023.** SAT results in fiscal year 2023 were influenced by:

* progress on high-reliability national security and civil space programs, where performance depends on meeting exacting quality and mission assurance standards;
* the ramp of standardized power products for proliferated low Earth orbit constellations, including investments in design standardization, automated test, and production lines;
* the availability of radiation-tolerant electronics and space-qualified cells, which are available from a limited number of qualified suppliers;
* the level of hypersonic flight test support and customer-funded research activity; and
* the inherent estimation risk in fixed-price development of space hardware.

**Outlook considerations.** We expect SAT performance to depend on constellation deployment schedules, our ability to achieve cost and production-rate targets for standardized products, continued positions on high-reliability missions, and the transition of hypersonic technologies from flight test into programs of record. Commercial constellation demand depends in part on our customers' access to capital. National security constellation programs may be restructured as the government refines its space architecture.

## Backlog

Backlog represents the estimated value of firm orders for which work has not yet been performed. It includes:

* **funded backlog**, meaning firm orders for which funding has been appropriated and authorized; and
* **unfunded backlog**, meaning firm orders for which funding has not yet been appropriated.

Backlog excludes unexercised contract options, potential orders under indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contracts, and programs for which we have not received a firm order.

Backlog is an important indicator of future revenues, but it is subject to change. Orders may be canceled or terminated, delivery schedules may change, funding may not be appropriated, and contracts may be modified. Backlog also includes long-term development and production programs that will be performed over multiple years. Backlog under commercial agreements may be subject to cancellation or deferral with limited penalties. For these reasons, backlog may not be indicative of revenues in any particular period.

Our order activity in fiscal year 2023 reflected the demand trends described under "Business Environment," including:

* munitions and missile production demand;
* development and prototyping awards in directed energy, thermal management, and hypersonics;
* national security and commercial space programs; and
* aircraft production, development, and aftermarket demand.

The timing of orders can vary significantly from quarter to quarter. Award and funding activity is sometimes concentrated near the end of the U.S. Government's fiscal year on September 30 and can be delayed by continuing resolutions.

Our remaining performance obligations, which represent the transaction price of firm orders for which work has not been performed, and the periods over which we expect to recognize them as revenue, are presented in the revenue recognition note to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8. Remaining performance obligations differ from backlog in certain respects, principally in the treatment of unfunded amounts and certain contract options.

### Revenue Recognition and Contract Estimates

**Nature of the estimate.** We recognize a significant portion of our revenues over time as we perform on our contracts. We generally measure progress using costs incurred relative to total estimated costs at completion. Under this method, revenues, including estimated fees or profits, are recorded proportionally as costs are incurred. Accounting for these contracts requires us to estimate:

* the total transaction price, including variable consideration;
* the total costs to complete each contract; and
* in some cases, the allocation of the transaction price among multiple performance obligations.

**Judgments and assumptions.** Estimating total costs at completion is complex and involves significant judgment. Our estimates consider:

* the technical complexity and maturity of the design, particularly for development programs;
* labor productivity, availability, and rates, including the effects of wage inflation;
* the cost and availability of materials and components, including supply chain conditions;
* supplier and subcontractor performance;
* manufacturing yields and learning curves;
* qualification and test outcomes;
* schedule risks, including the timing of customer approvals and government-furnished equipment; and
* overhead and indirect cost rates.

Our estimates of the transaction price consider variable consideration, such as award fees, incentive fees, and performance incentives tied to cost, schedule, or technical performance, including on-orbit performance for certain space products. We include variable consideration in the transaction price to the extent it is probable that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue recognized will not occur. We also consider the effects of contract modifications, claims, and requests for equitable adjustment. We include those items when they are approved, or when it is probable they will result in additional enforceable rights.

Our program teams update estimates at completion regularly, and our segment and enterprise leadership reviews them. Those reviews assess performance to date, remaining risks and opportunities, and the reasonableness of key assumptions.

**Effect of changes in estimates.** We recognize changes in estimated contract revenues and costs on a cumulative catch-up basis in the period in which they are identified. Under this method, the cumulative effect of a change on current and prior periods is recognized in the current period. Because of the size, duration, and complexity of some of our programs, a change in estimated costs at completion on a single program, or on a small number of programs, can significantly affect our results in a particular period.

Fixed-price development contracts present the greatest estimation risk. Technical problems that emerge during design, integration, or qualification testing may require redesign, additional testing, and schedule extensions. We may not be able to recover the cost of those efforts. Long-term fixed-price production contracts priced before recent inflationary increases also present risk if they lack economic price adjustment provisions.

As an illustration of sensitivity, a change in aggregate estimated costs equal to 1% of our fiscal year 2023 revenues would affect operating income by approximately $19.8 million. The actual effect of a change in estimate depends on the stage of completion of the affected contracts and other factors. This illustration is hypothetical.

The aggregate effect of net cumulative catch-up adjustments on our results, and information about significant individual adjustments, is presented in the revenue recognition note to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

### Provisions for Anticipated Losses on Contracts

**Nature of the estimate.** When the estimated total costs of a contract exceed its estimated total revenues, we recognize the entire anticipated loss in the period in which it becomes evident. Loss provisions are generally recorded as a reduction of contract assets or as a liability, and are included in cost of revenue.

**Judgments and assumptions.** Determining whether a contract is in a loss position, and the amount of the loss, requires the same estimates of total revenues and costs described above. We may accept development contracts with modest or negative margins in pursuit of production and sustainment opportunities. Accordingly, some of our development contracts are priced with little or no margin cushion against cost growth, and changes in estimates on those contracts can result in loss provisions. Under GAAP, we evaluate each contract on its own. We do not offset anticipated losses with expected profits on future production contracts that have not been awarded.

**Effect of changes in estimates.** Increases in estimated costs on contracts in a loss position, or on contracts that move into a loss position, result in additional charges in the period identified. Conversely, improvements in performance on loss contracts may result in reductions of previously recorded provisions.

### Goodwill and Intangible Assets

**Nature of the estimate.** At the end of fiscal year 2023, we had goodwill of $63,118,771 and intangible assets, net, of $24,857,570, which arose from our acquisitions of technology-focused businesses. We test goodwill for impairment annually and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. We review intangible assets with finite lives for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable.

**Judgments and assumptions.** Our impairment testing may begin with a qualitative assessment of whether it is more likely than not that a reporting unit's fair value is less than its carrying amount. When we perform a quantitative test, we estimate the fair value of each reporting unit using income-based approaches, market-based approaches, or both. These approaches require significant assumptions, including:

* projected revenues, operating margins, and cash flows, which depend on program awards, production rates, and government budgets;
* discount rates that reflect the risks inherent in the reporting unit's cash flows;
* terminal growth rates; and
* the selection of comparable companies and transactions and the related valuation multiples.

**Effect of changes in estimates.** Declines in projected revenues or profitability, the loss of significant programs or customers, adverse changes in the business climate, increases in discount rates, or sustained declines in our market capitalization could result in impairment charges. Goodwill and intangible assets together represented 2.2% of total assets and 5.3% of total stockholders' equity at year-end. Their relatively modest scale limits the potential magnitude of impairment charges relative to our overall financial position. An impairment charge could nonetheless be material to our results of operations in the period recognized.

### Inventory Valuation

**Nature of the estimate.** Inventory was $274,888,695 at the end of fiscal year 2023. We state inventory at the lower of cost or net realizable value. We record reserves for excess, obsolete, and slow-moving inventory.

**Judgments and assumptions.** Estimating inventory reserves requires judgment about future demand, product lifecycles, program changes, the usability of materials across programs, and the net realizable value of inventory. Supply chain conditions have led us to hold higher inventory levels for certain programs, including safety stock and materials purchased under longer-term commitments. If program quantities are reduced, designs change, programs are canceled, or demand is lower than anticipated, a portion of that inventory could become excess or obsolete. Certain materials, including energetic materials and battery cells, have limited shelf lives or specialized storage requirements, which also bears on our evaluation.

**Effect of changes in estimates.** Changes in demand assumptions or program decisions could require additional reserves, which would increase cost of revenue. Where inventory relates to U.S. Government contracts that are terminated for convenience, we may recover certain costs through termination settlements.

### Income Taxes

**Nature of the estimate.** We recorded an income tax benefit of $6,637,247 for fiscal year 2023. Accounting for income taxes requires us to estimate our current tax obligations and our deferred tax assets and liabilities. Deferred tax balances arise from temporary differences between the financial reporting and tax bases of assets and liabilities, and from tax credit and loss carryforwards. They include the deferred tax assets resulting from the capitalization of research and experimental expenditures for tax purposes.

**Judgments and assumptions.** Significant judgments include:

* **Realizability of deferred tax assets.** We evaluate whether it is more likely than not that our deferred tax assets will be realized. We consider projected future taxable income, the reversal of existing taxable temporary differences, tax planning strategies, and our recent earnings history. We record a valuation allowance when realization is not more likely than not.
* **Research and development tax credits.** Determining the amount of credits to recognize requires analysis of qualifying activities and expenditures, and the credits are subject to examination.
* **Uncertain tax positions.** We recognize the tax benefit of an uncertain position only if it is more likely than not to be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. We measure the benefit at the largest amount that is more than 50% likely to be realized upon ultimate settlement.

**Effect of changes in estimates.** Changes in our assessment of the realizability of deferred tax assets, the outcome of tax examinations, changes in tax laws, and changes in our mix of earnings could materially affect our effective tax rate and results of operations. Our fiscal year 2023 effective tax rate of negative 3.3% reflected favorable items that may not recur at similar levels in future periods.

### Environmental, Legal, and Other Contingencies

**Nature of the estimate.** We are subject to environmental remediation obligations, legal proceedings, government investigations and audits, and other contingencies. We record a liability when a loss is probable and the amount can be reasonably estimated. When a range of possible loss exists and no amount within the range is a better estimate than any other, we record the low end of the range.

**Judgments and assumptions.** Estimating environmental remediation liabilities requires judgments about:

* the extent of contamination;
* remediation methods and their costs;
* the timing of remediation;
* the number and financial condition of other potentially responsible parties; and
* the evolving regulatory treatment of substances such as per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances.

Some environmental costs related to our government contracts may be allowable and recoverable through contract pricing, subject to applicable regulations. Estimating legal and government audit contingencies requires judgments about the likely outcome of proceedings, the interpretation of laws and regulations, including the Cost Accounting Standards and cost allowability principles, and the amounts of potential settlements or judgments.

**Effect of changes in estimates.** New information, changes in laws or regulations, the discovery of additional contamination, or developments in legal proceedings or audits could require us to adjust our recorded liabilities. Such adjustments could be material to our results in the period recognized.

## Other Matters

### Government Contracting Considerations

Because a substantial portion of our revenues derives from U.S. Government programs, several aspects of government contracting affect our financial statements.

**Cost allowability and recovery.** Under our U.S. Government contracts and many of our subcontracts, the costs we may charge are governed by the Federal Acquisition Regulation, the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement, and the Cost Accounting Standards. Allowable indirect costs, including portions of SG&A, IR&D, and bid and proposal costs, are recovered through indirect cost rates applied to our contracts. Unallowable costs are not recoverable and are excluded from amounts charged to government contracts.

**Audits and rate settlements.** The Defense Contract Audit Agency and the Defense Contract Management Agency routinely audit our incurred costs, indirect rates, and business systems. Final indirect rates for a given year may not be settled until several years later. We record revenues based on the rates we expect to be finally approved. We believe that adjustments resulting from audits of prior years will not have a material effect on our financial position, results of operations, or cash flows. However, we cannot assure that this will be the case.

**Business systems.** DFARS business system rules require us to maintain acceptable accounting, estimating, purchasing, earned value management, material management and accounting, and property management systems. If government reviewers identify significant deficiencies, contracting officers may withhold a portion of payments until the deficiencies are corrected. That could affect the timing of our cash flows.

**Contract financing.** Many of our fixed-price contracts provide for progress payments or performance-based payments, which reduce the working capital we must fund during contract performance. Changes in government contract financing policies, or in the terms prime contractors offer us, could affect our working capital requirements.

**Terminations.** If a contract is terminated for convenience, we are generally entitled to recover allowable costs incurred, settlement expenses, and a reasonable profit on work performed. Termination settlements can take substantial time to negotiate. Amounts recoverable under termination settlements are recorded when probable and estimable.

### Effects of Inflation

Inflation in labor, materials, components, energy, and freight costs affected our operations in fiscal year 2023. The effects of inflation on our results depend on our contract mix.

* **Cost-reimbursable contracts.** Increased allowable costs are generally recoverable, subject to contract funding limits. Inflation may cause us to reach funding limits sooner and require additional customer funding.
* **Fixed-price contracts.** Inflation reduces our margins unless the contract includes economic price adjustment provisions or we can offset cost increases through productivity improvements. Many of our long-term fixed-price contracts were priced before recent cost increases.
* **New contracts and options.** We seek to reflect current and expected cost levels in our pricing of new contracts and contract options. However, competitive pressures and customer price expectations may limit our ability to do so.

We seek to mitigate the effects of inflation through pricing discipline, escalation clauses where available, long-term supply agreements, and productivity initiatives. In addition, U.S. Government budgets are generally established in nominal terms, so sustained inflation may reduce the real purchasing power of appropriations and the quantities of products our customers can procure.
