#### Revenue Recognized Over Time — Estimates of Total Costs at Completion on Fixed-Price Contracts

*Description of the matter.* As described in Notes 1 and 2 to the consolidated financial statements, the Company recognizes a significant portion of its revenues over time. Revenues for fiscal year 2023 totaled $1,982,571,143. The Company generally measures progress toward satisfaction of performance obligations using costs incurred relative to total estimated costs at completion. Under this method, management must estimate the total costs expected to be incurred to satisfy each performance obligation.

For fixed-price contracts, and particularly fixed-price development contracts involving immature technologies, these estimates require significant judgment. Management's estimates incorporate assumptions about:

* labor hours, productivity, and rates;
* the cost and availability of materials and purchased components, including the effects of supply chain disruptions and inflation;
* subcontractor and supplier performance;
* the technical risk associated with design, integration, and qualification testing; and
* the timing of program milestones and customer approvals.

Changes in these estimates are recognized on a cumulative catch-up basis and, where estimated costs exceed estimated revenues, through recognition of the full anticipated loss in the period in which it becomes evident.

Auditing management's estimates of total costs at completion was especially challenging and subjective. Contract performance can extend over multiple years. The estimates depend on assumptions about future events. Relatively small changes in estimated costs on a single program can have a significant effect on revenues and operating income in the period of change.

*How we addressed the matter in our audit.* We obtained an understanding of the Company's processes for developing, reviewing, and approving estimates of total costs at completion, and we evaluated the design and tested the operating effectiveness of controls over those processes. These included controls over management's periodic program reviews, the review and approval of estimate changes by program, segment, and enterprise leadership, and the identification and measurement of loss contracts.

For a selection of contracts, based on quantitative and qualitative factors, our audit procedures included, among others:

* reading the contract and related modifications to evaluate whether management identified the relevant terms, including performance obligations, pricing, and variable consideration;
* evaluating the reasonableness of significant assumptions underlying the estimates of remaining costs by comparing them to historical performance on similar programs, current program status, earned value data, supplier quotes and purchase commitments, and labor rate projections;
* inquiring of program management, engineering, and finance personnel, outside of the accounting function, regarding the technical status of the programs, identified risks and opportunities, and the basis for estimate changes;
* testing the accuracy and completeness of costs incurred to date;
* evaluating management's ability to estimate costs accurately by comparing prior estimates to actual results and to subsequent revisions; and
* evaluating the timing and measurement of cumulative catch-up adjustments and loss provisions recorded during the year.

#### Income Taxes — Research and Development Tax Credits and Realizability of Deferred Tax Assets

*Description of the matter.* As described in Notes 1 and 11 to the consolidated financial statements, the Company recorded an income tax benefit of $6,637,247 for fiscal year 2023 on income before income taxes of $203,907,274. This resulted in an effective tax rate that was negative and substantially below the U.S. federal statutory rate. The difference is attributable principally to federal and state research and development tax credits and other favorable items.

Determining the amount of research and development tax credits to recognize requires management to:

* identify qualifying research activities and qualified research expenditures across a large population of company-funded and customer-funded projects; and
* evaluate whether the resulting credits, together with related uncertain tax positions, meet the more-likely-than-not recognition threshold.

The Company also maintains significant deferred tax assets, including those arising from the capitalization of research and experimental expenditures for tax purposes and from credit carryforwards. Management evaluates whether those deferred tax assets are more likely than not to be realized, considering projected future taxable income, the reversal of existing temporary differences, and other factors.

Auditing these matters was especially challenging because of the complexity of the applicable tax law, the volume of projects evaluated, and the degree of judgment required to measure uncertain tax positions and to assess future taxable income.

*How we addressed the matter in our audit.* We obtained an understanding of, evaluated the design of, and tested the operating effectiveness of controls over the Company's process for computing research and development tax credits, measuring uncertain tax positions, and assessing the realizability of deferred tax assets. With the assistance of our income tax professionals, our audit procedures included, among others:

* evaluating the methodology management used to identify qualifying activities and expenditures, and testing, on a sample basis, the underlying project documentation and cost data;
* evaluating the technical merits of positions taken and management's measurement of related unrecognized tax benefits, considering applicable tax law, regulations, and the Company's examination history;
* evaluating the reasonableness of management's projections of future taxable income by comparing them with historical results, backlog, and the forecasts used elsewhere in the Company's planning and impairment assessments; and
* assessing the adequacy of the Company's income tax disclosures.

### Definition and Limitations of Internal Control over Financial Reporting

A company's internal control over financial reporting is a process designed to provide reasonable assurance regarding the reliability of financial reporting and the preparation of financial statements for external purposes in accordance with generally accepted accounting principles. A company's internal control over financial reporting includes those policies and procedures that:

1. pertain to the maintenance of records that, in reasonable detail, accurately and fairly reflect the transactions and dispositions of the assets of the company;
2. provide reasonable assurance that transactions are recorded as necessary to permit preparation of financial statements in accordance with generally accepted accounting principles, and that receipts and expenditures of the company are being made only in accordance with authorizations of management and directors of the company; and
3. provide reasonable assurance regarding prevention or timely detection of unauthorized acquisition, use, or disposition of the company's assets that could have a material effect on the financial statements.

Because of its inherent limitations, internal control over financial reporting may not prevent or detect misstatements. Also, projections of any evaluation of effectiveness to future periods are subject to the risk that controls may become inadequate because of changes in conditions, or that the degree of compliance with the policies or procedures may deteriorate.

## Note 1 — Nature of Operations and Summary of Significant Accounting Policies

### Nature of Operations

Riverton Energy Group, Inc. (together with its consolidated subsidiaries, "Riverton," the "Company," "we," "us," or "our") is an aerospace and defense technology company headquartered in Arizona. We design, develop, manufacture, integrate, and sustain energy-centric systems for military, civil government, space, and commercial aerospace customers. Our products generate, store, condition, distribute, and manage electrical, thermal, and propulsive energy aboard aircraft, missiles, munitions, ground vehicles, naval vessels, and spacecraft. We also provide engineering, test, aftermarket, and lifecycle support services for those systems.

We operate through three reportable segments:

* **Aerospace Power Systems ("APS")** designs and manufactures electrical power generation, conversion, distribution, and energy storage equipment for military and commercial fixed-wing aircraft, rotorcraft, and unmanned aircraft systems. It also provides related aftermarket spares, repair, and overhaul services.
* **Defense Energy Systems ("DES")** supplies power, energy storage, thermal management, and pulsed power subsystems for tactical missiles, munitions, interceptors, ground combat and tactical vehicles, naval platforms, and directed energy weapons. It also produces thermal batteries, gas generators, initiators, and other energetic devices.
* **Space & Advanced Technologies ("SAT")** develops and produces spacecraft and launch vehicle power systems. It also conducts advanced research and development in hypersonics, compact energy storage, and next-generation power architectures, frequently under government-funded research contracts.

A substantial portion of our revenues derives, directly or indirectly, from programs funded by the U.S. Government, principally the Department of Defense. In most cases we participate in those programs as a subcontractor or supplier to prime contractors. We also serve civil space agencies, commercial aerospace original equipment manufacturers and operators, commercial satellite manufacturers and operators, launch service providers, and, subject to U.S. export controls, allied foreign governments and international prime contractors.

Our corporate headquarters, principal engineering center, and largest manufacturing operations are located in the Phoenix metropolitan area of Arizona. We also operate a specialized production facility for thermal batteries and energetic devices at a separate Arizona location, together with engineering, manufacturing, repair, and customer support sites in several other U.S. states.

### Revenue Recognition

We account for revenue in accordance with Accounting Standards Codification ("ASC") Topic 606, *Revenue from Contracts with Customers*. We recognize revenue when, or as, we transfer control of promised goods or services to a customer, in an amount that reflects the consideration to which we expect to be entitled.

**Identifying the contract.** We account for a contract when:

* it has approval and commitment from both parties;
* the rights of the parties are identified;
* payment terms are identified;
* the contract has commercial substance; and
* collectibility of consideration is probable.

For U.S. Government contracts and subcontracts, we generally consider a contract to exist when it has been funded, because funding establishes the enforceable rights and obligations of the parties. Unfunded portions of contracts are included in the transaction price only to the extent we have determined that enforceable rights exist.

We evaluate whether multiple contracts with the same customer should be combined and accounted for as a single contract. We also evaluate whether a contract modification should be accounted for as a separate contract or as part of the existing contract. Most of our contract modifications relate to goods or services that are not distinct from the existing contract, because of the significant integration service provided in the context of the contract. Those modifications are accounted for as if they were part of the existing contract, and their effect on the transaction price and our measure of progress is recognized as a cumulative catch-up adjustment. Modifications that add distinct goods or services at their standalone selling prices are accounted for as separate contracts.

**Identifying performance obligations.** A performance obligation is a promise in a contract to transfer a distinct good or service to the customer. Many of our contracts, particularly development contracts and contracts for the design and production of complex systems, involve a significant service of integrating components into a combined output. Those contracts are typically accounted for as a single performance obligation. Production contracts for multiple units of a recurring product may also be accounted for as a single performance obligation when the units are substantially the same and have the same pattern of transfer to the customer. Contracts for products and services that are distinct, such as certain aftermarket spares, repairs, and engineering services, may include multiple performance obligations.

Options for additional goods or services are accounted for when exercised, unless they provide the customer with a material right.

**Determining the transaction price.** The transaction price is the amount of consideration we expect to be entitled to in exchange for transferring promised goods or services. Our contracts may include variable consideration, including:

* award fees and incentive fees on cost-reimbursable contracts;
* incentive provisions on fixed-price incentive contracts, under which cost overruns and underruns are shared with the customer up to a ceiling price;
* performance incentives tied to cost, schedule, or technical performance, including, for certain space products, incentives tied to on-orbit performance;
* liquidated damages and penalties for late delivery or nonperformance; and
* claims and unpriced change orders.

We estimate variable consideration using the expected value method or the most likely amount method, whichever we expect to better predict the amount to which we will be entitled. We include variable consideration in the transaction price to the extent it is probable that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue recognized will not occur when the uncertainty is resolved. Our estimates consider all information reasonably available to us, including historical, current, and forecasted information. We include claims and requests for equitable adjustment in the transaction price only when we have an enforceable right and it is probable that a significant reversal will not occur.

Our contracts generally do not include significant financing components. Payment terms on U.S. Government contracts and subcontracts, including progress payments and performance-based payments, are designed to provide customers with assurance of performance rather than to provide financing. Commercial contracts may include advance payments for similar reasons, or to secure capacity.

**Allocating the transaction price.** For contracts with multiple performance obligations, we allocate the transaction price to each performance obligation based on its relative standalone selling price. For most of our products and services, standalone selling prices are not directly observable because the products and services are customized to the customer's requirements. We therefore generally estimate standalone selling prices using an expected cost plus a reasonable margin approach.

**Recognizing revenue over time.** We recognize revenue over time for a significant portion of our contracts. Over-time recognition applies to most of our U.S. Government-related contracts and subcontracts, including fixed-price and cost-reimbursable development and production contracts. It applies when either:

* the customer obtains control of the work in process as we perform, as evidenced by clauses in U.S. Government contracts and flowed-down subcontracts that allow the customer to unilaterally terminate the contract for convenience, pay us for costs incurred plus a reasonable profit, and take control of any work in process; or
* our performance creates an asset with no alternative use to us, and we have an enforceable right to payment for performance completed to date, including a reasonable margin.

Many of our products are designed and built to customer specifications, are subject to export control restrictions, or require customer qualification. These products typically have no alternative use to us.

For performance obligations satisfied over time, we generally measure progress using the ratio of costs incurred to date to total estimated costs at completion (the "cost-to-cost method"). Costs incurred include direct labor, direct materials, subcontractor costs, and allocable indirect costs. We believe the cost-to-cost method best depicts the transfer of control to the customer, because it measures the value of work performed and the effort expended to satisfy the performance obligation. Revenues, including estimated fees or profits, are recorded proportionally as costs are incurred.

For certain service contracts, such as time-and-materials engineering support and certain repair and sustainment services, we recognize revenue as services are performed, using the right-to-invoice practical expedient where the amount invoiced corresponds directly to the value transferred to the customer.

**Recognizing revenue at a point in time.** Revenue on contracts that do not meet the criteria for over-time recognition is recognized at the point in time when control transfers to the customer. This generally occurs upon shipment or delivery, depending on contract terms. Point-in-time recognition generally applies to certain commercial aerospace products, standard catalog products, and aftermarket spare parts. In evaluating when control transfers, we consider indicators such as legal title, physical possession, the transfer of the risks and rewards of ownership, customer acceptance, and our present right to payment.

**Contract estimates.** Accounting for contracts recognized over time requires judgment about the total transaction price and the total costs at completion. Our estimates consider, among other factors:

* the technical complexity and maturity of the design, particularly on development programs;
* labor productivity, availability, and rates, including wage inflation;
* the cost and availability of materials and components, including the effects of supply chain disruptions;
* the performance of subcontractors and suppliers;
* manufacturing yields and learning curves;
* qualification and test outcomes;
* schedule risks, including the timing of customer approvals and government-furnished equipment;
* overhead and indirect cost rates; and
* the expected outcome of variable consideration.

Program teams update their estimates at completion regularly, and program, segment, and enterprise leadership reviews them. We recognize changes in estimated revenues and costs on a cumulative catch-up basis in the period in which they are identified. Under this method, the cumulative effect of a change on current and prior periods is recognized in the current period. Changes in estimates can result from, among other things, the retirement or emergence of technical and schedule risks, changes in labor and material costs, supplier performance, contract modifications, and changes in variable consideration.

**Contract losses.** When estimated total costs to satisfy a performance obligation exceed estimated total revenues, we recognize the entire anticipated loss in the period in which the loss becomes evident. We recognize loss provisions first as a reduction of any related contract asset balance, with any remaining amount recorded as a liability within other liabilities. Loss provisions are included in cost of revenue. We evaluate each contract separately. We do not offset anticipated losses on a contract with expected profits on future contracts that have not yet been awarded, even when we pursued the loss contract in anticipation of follow-on production or sustainment work.

**Contract costs.** We expense costs to obtain a contract, such as bid and proposal costs, as incurred, because the amortization period would generally be one year or less or because such costs are not incremental. Costs to fulfill a contract incurred before contract award ("precontract costs") are deferred only when they relate directly to a specific anticipated contract, generate or enhance resources to be used in satisfying performance obligations, and are expected to be recovered. Deferred precontract costs are not significant.

**Warranties.** Many of our contracts include assurance-type warranties that our products will conform to agreed specifications and be free of defects for a specified period. We do not account for assurance-type warranties as separate performance obligations. We accrue estimated warranty costs when revenue is recognized, based on historical experience, contract terms, and known product issues, and we adjust those accruals as experience develops. Warranty reserves are included in accrued liabilities. Certain commercial contracts include extended warranties or service-type warranties that are accounted for as separate performance obligations.

**Shipping and handling.** Shipping and handling activities performed after the customer obtains control of the goods are accounted for as fulfillment costs and are included in cost of revenue.

**Taxes collected from customers.** Sales and similar taxes collected from customers and remitted to governmental authorities are excluded from revenues.

### Cost of Revenue

Cost of revenue includes:

* direct materials and purchased components;
* subcontracted work;
* direct labor;
* manufacturing and engineering overhead allocated to contracts;
* the costs of customer-funded research and development;
* warranty costs;
* provisions for excess and obsolete inventory; and
* provisions for anticipated losses on contracts and the cost effects of changes in estimated costs at completion.

Under our cost classification practices, a significant portion of our indirect costs, including certain general, administrative, and other operating costs, is reported within selling, general and administrative expenses and other operating expenses rather than within cost of revenue. Company-funded research and development is reported as a separate line item. For U.S. Government contracts, allowable general and administrative costs, independent research and development ("IR&D") costs, and bid and proposal costs are recovered through indirect cost rates charged to contracts, even though they are presented outside cost of revenue in our consolidated statement of operations.

### Research and Development

Company-funded research and development costs are expensed as incurred and are presented as research and development expense. These costs include IR&D projects and the costs of internally funded development of new products and product improvements. Company-funded research and development expense was $403,165,603 in fiscal year 2023, or 20.3% of revenues.

Costs of research and development performed under contracts with customers are accounted for as contract costs. The related revenues and costs are included in revenues and cost of revenue, respectively, and are not included in research and development expense.

A portion of our IR&D costs is allowable and may be recovered through indirect cost rates on our U.S. Government contracts, consistent with the Federal Acquisition Regulation ("FAR") and the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement ("DFARS").

### Selling, General and Administrative Expenses

Selling, general and administrative expenses include the costs of:

* business development and bid and proposal activities;
* program management and contract administration not charged directly to contracts;
* finance, legal, human resources, information technology, and other corporate and shared service functions;
* security, trade compliance, ethics and compliance, and environmental, health, and safety programs;
* cybersecurity programs; and
* stock-based and incentive compensation for personnel in these functions.

Advertising costs are expensed as incurred and are not significant.

### Other Operating Expenses

Other operating expenses include operating costs and credits that are not classified within cost of revenue, selling, general and administrative expenses, or research and development expense. The components of other operating expenses are described in Note 7 and Note 17 and include amortization of acquired intangible assets, certain facility and occupancy costs, restructuring and integration costs, environmental remediation costs, and other operating charges and credits.

### Cash and Cash Equivalents

Cash and cash equivalents consist of cash on deposit and highly liquid investments with original maturities of three months or less when purchased, including money market funds, U.S. Treasury and agency securities, commercial paper, and bank time deposits. Cash equivalents are carried at cost, which approximates fair value, or at fair value for money market funds. Substantially all of our cash and cash equivalents is held in the United States.

We maintain cash balances at financial institutions that may exceed federally insured limits. We monitor the credit quality of these institutions and of the issuers of instruments in which we invest, and we seek to diversify our exposure.

### Investments

Investments in debt securities with original maturities greater than three months are classified as available-for-sale and are carried at fair value. These investments are included in other assets. Unrealized gains and losses, net of tax, are reported in accumulated other comprehensive loss until realized. Realized gains and losses are determined using the specific identification method.

For available-for-sale debt securities in an unrealized loss position, we evaluate whether we intend to sell the security, or whether it is more likely than not that we will be required to sell the security before recovery of its amortized cost basis. If either condition is met, we recognize the entire difference between amortized cost and fair value in earnings. Otherwise, we evaluate whether any portion of the decline in fair value results from credit losses. Credit losses are recognized through an allowance, limited to the amount by which fair value is below amortized cost. Any remaining decline is recognized in other comprehensive income (loss).

Investment income earned on cash equivalents and investments, including interest, accretion of discounts, amortization of premiums, and realized gains and losses, is presented as a credit within other operating expenses.

### Receivables and Allowance for Credit Losses

Receivables represent our unconditional rights to consideration from customers. They consist principally of amounts billed and currently due from customers. Receivables are included in other assets. A substantial portion of our receivables is due, directly or indirectly, from the U.S. Government and from large prime contractors.

We maintain an allowance for credit losses on receivables and contract assets in accordance with ASC Topic 326, *Financial Instruments — Credit Losses*. We estimate expected credit losses based on historical experience, the age of balances, the credit quality of customers, current economic conditions, and reasonable and supportable forecasts. Because a substantial portion of our receivables and contract assets is due from the U.S. Government or from prime contractors performing on U.S. Government programs, our historical credit losses have not been significant. Our allowance for credit losses was not material at the end of fiscal year 2023.

### Inventories

Inventories consist of raw materials, purchased parts, work in process, and finished goods. They relate principally to contracts and products accounted for at a point in time, and to materials and components that have not yet been issued to contracts accounted for over time. Inventories are stated at the lower of cost or net realizable value. Cost is determined using the first-in, first-out method or average cost methods, and includes direct materials, direct labor, and applicable manufacturing overhead.

We record reserves for excess, obsolete, and slow-moving inventory based on estimates of future demand, product lifecycles, program changes, the usability of materials across programs, shelf-life limitations, and net realizable value. Costs incurred on contracts accounted for over time are generally recognized as cost of revenue as incurred, and are not included in inventories, because control of the work in process transfers to the customer as we perform.

Certain of our contracts provide for progress payments or performance-based payments. Where the U.S. Government holds title to inventory under progress payment clauses, the related inventory remains on our consolidated balance sheet because we retain the risks and rewards of the inventory until delivery.

### Property, Plant and Equipment

Property, plant and equipment is stated at cost, less accumulated depreciation. Cost includes expenditures for improvements that substantially extend the capacity or useful life of an asset. Expenditures for maintenance and repairs are expensed as incurred. Depreciation is computed using the straight-line method over the estimated useful lives of the assets. Useful lives range from the shortest lives, applied to computer equipment and software, to the longest lives, applied to buildings. Leasehold improvements are depreciated over the shorter of their estimated useful lives or the remaining lease term. Land is not depreciated. Construction in progress is not depreciated until the related assets are placed in service.

Costs incurred to develop or obtain internal-use software are capitalized during the application development stage and amortized over the software's estimated useful life. Costs incurred in the preliminary project stage and post-implementation stage are expensed as incurred. Implementation costs for cloud computing arrangements that are service contracts are capitalized under the same criteria and amortized over the term of the hosting arrangement. These capitalized implementation costs are included in other assets.

Upon the retirement or disposition of an asset, the cost and related accumulated depreciation are removed from the accounts, and any resulting gain or loss is recognized in operating income.

Certain of our facilities, including test infrastructure and production capacity for energetic devices and space power assemblies, have been funded in part by customers or government agencies. Where we receive such funding, we evaluate the terms of the arrangement to determine whether the funding represents consideration for goods or services under a contract with a customer, a reduction of the cost of the related asset, or deferred income. We account for it accordingly.

### Leases

We determine whether an arrangement is or contains a lease at inception. We lease certain manufacturing, engineering, office, and warehouse facilities and equipment. Operating lease right-of-use assets are included in other assets, and operating lease liabilities are included in other liabilities. Finance leases, if any, are not significant.

Lease liabilities are measured at the present value of the remaining lease payments over the lease term. Because the rates implicit in our leases are generally not readily determinable, we use our incremental borrowing rate, based on information available at lease commencement, to determine the present value of lease payments. Right-of-use assets are measured at the amount of the lease liability, adjusted for prepaid lease payments, lease incentives received, and initial direct costs. Lease terms include options to extend or terminate the lease when it is reasonably certain we will exercise those options.

We recognize operating lease cost on a straight-line basis over the lease term. We have elected not to recognize right-of-use assets and lease liabilities for leases with terms of twelve months or less. We have also elected to account for lease and non-lease components together for our real estate leases. Additional information about our leases is presented in Note 10.

### Business Combinations

We account for business combinations using the acquisition method. We allocate the purchase price to the tangible and identifiable intangible assets acquired and liabilities assumed, based on their estimated fair values at the acquisition date. The excess of the purchase price over the fair value of the net assets acquired is recorded as goodwill.

Determining the fair values of acquired intangible assets, such as customer relationships, program positions, developed technology, and trade names, requires significant judgment. Those valuations depend on estimates of future revenues, margins, customer attrition, technology obsolescence, and discount rates. Acquisition-related costs are expensed as incurred. The results of acquired businesses are included in our consolidated financial statements from their respective acquisition dates.

### Goodwill

Goodwill represents the excess of the purchase price of acquired businesses over the fair value of the net assets acquired. Goodwill is not amortized. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level annually, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that it is more likely than not that the fair value of a reporting unit is less than its carrying amount.

We may first assess qualitative factors to determine whether it is more likely than not that the fair value of a reporting unit is less than its carrying amount. If we conclude that it is, or if we elect to bypass the qualitative assessment, we perform a quantitative test. In the quantitative test, we compare the fair value of the reporting unit with its carrying amount. If the carrying amount exceeds fair value, we recognize an impairment charge equal to the excess, limited to the carrying amount of goodwill allocated to the reporting unit. We estimate the fair value of reporting units using income-based approaches, market-based approaches, or a combination of both. Additional information is presented in Note 6.

### Intangible Assets and Other Long-Lived Assets

Intangible assets with finite lives, which consist principally of customer relationships and program positions, developed technology, and trade names acquired in business combinations, are amortized over their estimated useful lives. Amortization follows the straight-line method or, where it better reflects the pattern in which the economic benefits are consumed, an accelerated method. Amortization of acquired intangible assets is included in other operating expenses.

We review property, plant and equipment, finite-lived intangible assets, and right-of-use assets for impairment whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying amount of an asset group may not be recoverable. If the sum of the undiscounted cash flows expected to result from the use and eventual disposition of an asset group is less than its carrying amount, we recognize an impairment loss equal to the excess of the carrying amount over fair value.

### Debt and Debt Issuance Costs

Debt is carried at amortized cost. Debt issuance costs and discounts, if significant, are presented as a direct deduction from the carrying amount of the related debt and amortized to interest expense over the term of the debt using the effective interest method.

Our long-term debt consists of convertible senior notes that do not bear regular interest and whose principal amount does not accrete. We account for the notes as a single liability in accordance with Accounting Standards Update ("ASU") 2020-06, *Debt — Debt with Conversion and Other Options and Derivatives and Hedging — Contracts in Entity's Own Equity*. The embedded conversion feature is not separately accounted for, because it is indexed to our own stock, qualifies for classification in stockholders' equity, and does not require bifurcation as a derivative. Costs incurred in connection with the issuance of the notes were not significant and were expensed in the period of issuance. Accordingly, no interest expense related to the notes was recognized in fiscal year 2023. Additional information is presented in Note 9.

### Derivative Financial Instruments

From time to time, we may use derivative instruments, principally foreign currency forward contracts, to manage our exposure to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates on certain supplier purchases and customer contracts denominated in currencies other than the U.S. dollar. We do not use derivative instruments for trading or speculative purposes. Derivatives are recognized at fair value. The accounting for changes in fair value depends on whether the derivative is designated and qualifies as a hedge. Our derivative activity was not significant in fiscal year 2023.

### Fair Value Measurements

Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date. We apply a fair value hierarchy that prioritizes the inputs used to measure fair value:

* **Level 1** — quoted prices in active markets for identical assets or liabilities;
* **Level 2** — observable inputs other than Level 1 prices, such as quoted prices for similar assets or liabilities, quoted prices in markets that are not active, or other inputs that are observable or can be corroborated by observable market data; and
* **Level 3** — unobservable inputs that are supported by little or no market activity and that are significant to the fair value of the assets or liabilities.

The carrying amounts of receivables, accounts payable, and accrued liabilities approximate fair value because of their short-term nature. Additional information is presented in Note 16.

### Income Taxes

We account for income taxes using the asset and liability method. Deferred tax assets and liabilities are recognized for the future tax consequences of temporary differences between the financial reporting and tax bases of assets and liabilities, and for tax credit and loss carryforwards. Deferred tax balances are measured using enacted tax rates expected to apply in the years in which the temporary differences are expected to reverse. The effect of a change in tax rates is recognized in income in the period that includes the enactment date.

We record a valuation allowance to reduce deferred tax assets to the amount that is more likely than not to be realized. In making this assessment, we consider all available positive and negative evidence, including projected future taxable income, the reversal of existing taxable temporary differences, tax planning strategies, and our recent earnings history.

We recognize the tax benefit of an uncertain tax position only if it is more likely than not that the position will be sustained on examination by the taxing authorities, based on its technical merits. We measure the benefit as the largest amount that is more than 50% likely to be realized upon ultimate settlement. We recognize interest and penalties related to unrecognized tax benefits within income tax expense.

Research and development tax credits are recognized as a reduction of income tax expense in the period in which the qualifying expenditures are incurred, subject to the recognition and measurement principles for uncertain tax positions. We account for the tax effects of global intangible low-taxed income, if any, as a period cost. Additional information is presented in Note 11.

### Stock-Based Compensation

We measure stock-based compensation at the grant-date fair value of the awards. We recognize the expense on a straight-line basis over the requisite service period, which is generally the vesting period. For awards with performance conditions, we recognize expense based on the probable outcome of the performance condition and adjust cumulative expense when that probable outcome changes. For awards with market conditions, the effect of the market condition is reflected in the grant-date fair value, and expense is recognized regardless of whether the market condition is ultimately satisfied, provided the requisite service is rendered. We account for forfeitures as they occur. Additional information is presented in Note 13.

### Environmental Costs

We record liabilities for environmental remediation when it is probable that a liability has been incurred and the amount can be reasonably estimated. When a range of possible loss exists and no amount within the range is a better estimate than any other, we record the low end of the range. Environmental liabilities are not discounted unless the amount and timing of the cash payments are fixed or reliably determinable. We record a receivable for expected recoveries from insurers or other third parties when recovery is probable.

Some environmental costs related to our U.S. Government contracts are allowable and recoverable through contract pricing, subject to applicable regulations. We record an asset for the portion of environmental costs we expect to recover through future contract pricing when recovery is probable. Environmental remediation costs that are not recoverable through contract pricing are included in other operating expenses. Additional information is presented in Note 17.

### Legal and Other Contingencies

We record a liability for a loss contingency when it is probable that a liability has been incurred and the amount of the loss can be reasonably estimated. When a range of possible loss exists and no amount within the range is a better estimate than any other, we record the low end of the range. We disclose loss contingencies that are reasonably possible, and loss contingencies that are probable but not reasonably estimable, where material. Legal costs associated with loss contingencies are expensed as incurred.

### Government Contract Matters

Our U.S. Government contracts and subcontracts are subject to audit by the Defense Contract Audit Agency ("DCAA"), the Defense Contract Management Agency ("DCMA"), and other agencies. Final indirect cost rates for a given year may not be settled until several years after the costs are incurred. We record revenues on cost-reimbursable contracts based on the indirect rates we expect to be approved. We recognize adjustments when they become probable and reasonably estimable.

Amounts recoverable under contract terminations for convenience are recorded when probable and reasonably estimable. Costs that are unallowable under the FAR, the DFARS, or the Cost Accounting Standards are excluded from amounts charged to U.S. Government contracts.

### Foreign Currency

Substantially all of our operations are conducted in the United States, and the U.S. dollar is the functional currency of substantially all of our subsidiaries. Transactions denominated in foreign currencies are remeasured into U.S. dollars at the exchange rates in effect at the transaction date. Monetary assets and liabilities denominated in foreign currencies are remeasured at the exchange rates in effect at the balance sheet date. Transaction gains and losses are included in other operating expenses and were not significant in fiscal year 2023.

### Self-Insurance

We are self-insured for certain employee medical and workers' compensation benefits, up to specified stop-loss limits, and we maintain insurance coverage above those limits. We record liabilities for claims incurred, including estimates of claims incurred but not reported, based on historical claims experience and actuarial assumptions. Those liabilities are included in accrued liabilities and other liabilities.

## Note 2 — Revenue

### Overview

We generated revenues of $1,982,571,143 in fiscal year 2023. Our revenues are derived principally from:

* production of power, energy storage, thermal management, and energetic products for military and commercial platforms;
* customer-funded development and research contracts;
* aftermarket spares, repair, overhaul, and sustainment services; and
* engineering, test, and technical support services.

### Contract Types

We perform work under fixed-price, cost-reimbursable, and time-and-materials contracts and subcontracts, and under commercial agreements negotiated on commercial terms.

**Fixed-price contracts.** Under firm fixed-price contracts, we agree to deliver products or services for a predetermined price, regardless of our actual costs. We therefore bear the risk of cost overruns and benefit from cost underruns. Under fixed-price incentive contracts, cost overruns and underruns relative to a negotiated target cost are shared with the customer according to a sharing formula, up to a ceiling price above which we bear the full cost. Most of our production contracts, our aftermarket work, and many of our development contracts with prime contractors are fixed-price.

Fixed-price development contracts involve heightened risk. Their technical and schedule challenges can be difficult to estimate accurately, particularly where the underlying technologies have not previously been produced at the required performance levels or in the required configurations. Examples include high-power aircraft generation architectures, pulsed power subsystems, advanced thermal management systems, hypersonic power solutions, and space-qualified electronics.

**Cost-reimbursable contracts.** Under cost-reimbursable contracts, we are reimbursed for allowable costs incurred, subject to applicable cost principles and contract funding limitations. We also receive a fee, which may be fixed, based on award criteria, or based on incentive formulas tied to cost, schedule, or technical performance. Cost-reimbursable contracts generally carry lower margins than fixed-price contracts, but they limit our exposure to cost growth. We use them principally for research, early-stage development, and prototyping work. That includes a meaningful portion of our directed energy, thermal management, and SAT advanced research activities, and work under cooperative agreements with government research organizations.

**Time-and-materials contracts.** Under time-and-materials contracts, we are paid fixed hourly rates for direct labor, which cover wages, overhead, general and administrative expenses, and profit, and are reimbursed for materials at cost. We use these contracts principally for certain engineering support, repair, and technical services.

**Commercial agreements.** Our commercial aerospace and commercial space sales are generally conducted under long-term supply agreements, purchase orders, and service agreements negotiated on commercial terms. Some of these agreements include pricing commitments that extend over multiple years.

Our mix of contract types varies by segment and program phase. Development-phase work tends to involve a higher proportion of cost-reimbursable contracts, although prime contractors increasingly seek fixed-price terms for development work. Production and aftermarket work is predominantly fixed-price.

### Disaggregation of Revenue

We disaggregate revenue by reportable segment, by customer type, by contract type, and by the timing of revenue recognition. We believe these categories best depict how the nature, amount, timing, and uncertainty of our revenues and cash flows are affected by economic factors.

**By segment.** Revenues are reported for our three reportable segments: Aerospace Power Systems, Defense Energy Systems, and Space & Advanced Technologies. The segments collectively generated the consolidated revenues of $1,982,571,143 reported in fiscal year 2023. Segment information is presented in Note 18.

**By customer type.** We categorize revenues by end customer type, as follows:

* *U.S. Government.* This category includes revenues from contracts with U.S. Government agencies and from subcontracts with prime contractors and higher-tier subcontractors where the ultimate customer is the U.S. Government. Revenues in this category derive principally from the Department of Defense and its military services and agencies, and also from the National Aeronautics and Space Administration and other civil agencies. A substantial portion of our revenues falls in this category.
* *International.* This category includes revenues from sales to allied foreign governments and international prime contractors, both direct sales and sales through the U.S. Government's Foreign Military Sales program. Sales through the Foreign Military Sales program are contracted through the U.S. Government but are classified as international revenues because the ultimate customer is a foreign government.
* *Commercial.* This category includes revenues from business jet, regional aircraft, and rotorcraft manufacturers; airlines and aircraft operators; maintenance, repair, and overhaul providers; commercial satellite manufacturers and operators; and launch service providers.

**By contract type.** We categorize revenues as derived from fixed-price contracts, which include firm fixed-price and fixed-price incentive contracts and commercial agreements, or from cost-reimbursable and time-and-materials contracts. Fixed-price contracts represent the majority of our production and aftermarket revenues. Cost-reimbursable contracts represent a larger share of our development, prototyping, and research revenues.

**By timing of recognition.** A significant portion of our revenues is recognized over time, principally on U.S. Government-related contracts and subcontracts. Revenues recognized at a point in time derive principally from certain commercial aerospace products, standard catalog products, and aftermarket spare parts.

### Revenue Characteristics by Segment

**Aerospace Power Systems.** APS revenues are generated under long-term production agreements with airframe and engine prime contractors and tier-one system integrators, under development contracts for new platforms and upgrades, and under aftermarket purchase orders and service agreements. APS sells aftermarket products and services directly to the military services and their depots, to the Defense Logistics Agency, to commercial airlines and operators, and to maintenance, repair, and overhaul providers. Production revenues under military programs are generally recognized over time. Commercial production and aftermarket spare parts revenues are generally recognized at a point in time, upon shipment or delivery. Repair and overhaul services are generally recognized over time, as the services are performed.

**Defense Energy Systems.** DES revenues are generated principally under multi-year production contracts with missile, munition, interceptor, vehicle, and weapon system prime contractors, and under development, prototyping, and technology maturation contracts with prime contractors, the military services, the Missile Defense Agency, and defense research laboratories. Thermal battery, energetic device, and missile power electronics production revenues are generally recognized over time, because these products are built to customer specifications, have no alternative use to us, and are subject to termination-for-convenience clauses. Directed energy, thermal management, and vehicle power development revenues are generally recognized over time and are frequently earned under cost-reimbursable contracts.

**Space & Advanced Technologies.** SAT revenues are generated under contracts with spacecraft prime contractors and bus manufacturers, launch vehicle providers, commercial satellite operators, and government customers, including the U.S. Space Force, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, and defense research organizations. National security and civil space contracts typically involve low-volume, high-reliability products produced under fixed-price contracts with significant engineering content. These revenues are generally recognized over time. Certain of these contracts include performance incentives tied to on-orbit performance, which we account for as variable consideration. Commercial and proliferated constellation revenues involve higher volumes and are recognized over time or at a point in time, depending on the contract terms. Advanced research revenues are generally earned under cost-reimbursable contracts and cooperative agreements and are recognized over time.

### Changes in Contract Estimates

We recognize changes in estimated revenues and costs at completion on contracts accounted for over time on a cumulative catch-up basis. Changes in estimates can be favorable or unfavorable. Favorable changes may result from:

* improved labor productivity and manufacturing yields;
* the retirement of technical or schedule risks;
* lower-than-expected material, component, or subcontract costs; and
* the favorable resolution of variable consideration, contract modifications, or claims.

Unfavorable changes may result from:

* technical challenges emerging during design, integration, or qualification testing;
* supplier delays, quality escapes, and expediting costs;
* increases in labor and material costs, particularly on fixed-price contracts without economic price adjustment provisions; and
* schedule delays.

Net cumulative catch-up adjustments, together with loss provisions recognized during fiscal year 2023, were reflected in revenues and cost of revenue and are included in the operating income of $203,907,274 reported for the year. In fiscal year 2023, the net effect of these adjustments reflected favorable adjustments on mature production programs, partially offset by unfavorable adjustments on certain fixed-price development programs. These unfavorable adjustments were primarily attributable to supply chain cost growth and to additional engineering and test effort. No individual adjustment had a material effect on our consolidated results of operations for fiscal year 2023.

Because of the size, duration, and complexity of certain programs, changes in estimated costs at completion on a single program, or on a small number of programs, could have a significant effect on our results in future periods. For illustrative purposes, a change in aggregate estimated costs equal to 1% of our fiscal year 2023 revenues would affect operating income by approximately $19.8 million. The actual effect of a change in estimate depends on the stage of completion of the affected contracts and other factors.

### Remaining Performance Obligations

Remaining performance obligations represent the transaction price of firm orders for which work has not been performed. They exclude:

* unexercised contract options;
* potential orders under indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contracts; and
* unfunded amounts on U.S. Government contracts, except to the extent we have determined that enforceable rights exist.

Remaining performance obligations differ from backlog, as discussed in Item 7, principally in the treatment of unfunded amounts and certain contract options.

Our remaining performance obligations relate to long-term production, development, and sustainment programs across our three segments. A portion is expected to be recognized as revenue over the next twelve months, and the remainder over subsequent periods as we perform on multi-year production and development programs. The timing of recognition depends on program schedules, supplier deliveries, customer funding, and contract modifications. Remaining performance obligations are subject to change because of contract terminations, scope reductions, delivery schedule changes, and modifications.

We have applied the practical expedients that permit us to exclude from remaining performance obligations:

* the value of unsatisfied performance obligations for contracts with an original expected duration of one year or less; and
* the value of unsatisfied performance obligations for which we recognize revenue at the amount to which we have the right to invoice for services performed.

### Customer Concentration

A substantial portion of our revenues derives, directly or indirectly, from the U.S. Government. In most cases we serve the U.S. Government as a subcontractor or supplier to prime contractors. We sell a significant portion of our products to a relatively concentrated group of large aerospace and defense prime contractors. Our relationships with these prime contractors are long-standing and span multiple programs, business units, and segments. Certain prime contractor customers each accounted for a significant portion of our revenues in fiscal year 2023, with revenues from those customers recognized in each of our three segments. The loss of, or a significant reduction in purchases by, one or more of these customers could have a material adverse effect on our results of operations. Additional information about concentrations of risk is presented in Note 19.

## Note 3 — Contract Assets and Contract Liabilities

### Contract Assets

Contract assets represent revenue recognized in excess of amounts billed on contracts accounted for over time, where our right to consideration is conditioned on something other than the passage of time. Under the cost-to-cost method, revenue is recognized as costs are incurred. Billing, however, frequently occurs on a different schedule, depending on contract terms, including:

* progress payment provisions, under which we bill a percentage of incurred costs and the balance upon delivery or completion;
* performance-based payment provisions, under which we bill upon the achievement of defined events or milestones;
* milestone billing provisions under commercial agreements; and
* retention provisions, under which a portion of the contract price is withheld until contract completion or customer acceptance.

Contract assets are reclassified to receivables when our right to consideration becomes unconditional, which generally occurs upon billing.

Contract assets are a significant component of our working capital and are characteristic of our over-time revenue recognition. They are included in other assets, which totaled $2,724,081,595 at the end of fiscal year 2023, or 67.3% of total assets. Contract assets are classified as current, because we expect to bill and collect them within our normal operating cycle, even though the operating cycle on some programs exceeds one year.

Contract assets accumulate particularly on development programs and long-lead production efforts, where significant costs are incurred before contractual billing events occur. The supply chain conditions we experienced in fiscal year 2023, including extended lead times for electronic components, castings, and forgings, affected the timing of billing events on certain programs.

### Contract Liabilities

Contract liabilities represent advance payments and billings in excess of revenue recognized. They arise when we receive consideration, or have an unconditional right to consideration, before we transfer goods or services to the customer. Contract liabilities include:

* customer advances, including advances received under commercial agreements to secure production capacity or to fund long-lead materials;
* performance-based payments and milestone billings that exceed revenue recognized to date; and
* deferred revenue on aftermarket service agreements and extended warranties.

Contract liabilities are reduced as we perform under the related contracts and recognize revenue. They will generally be satisfied through performance rather than through cash payments. Contract liabilities are included in other liabilities, which totaled $1,256,055,965 at the end of fiscal year 2023, or 52.8% of total liabilities. Contract liabilities are classified as current, because we expect to satisfy them within our normal operating cycle.

### Changes in Contract Balances

Changes in contract assets and contract liabilities during fiscal year 2023 resulted from:

* revenue recognized on contracts accounted for over time in advance of billing;
* the reclassification of contract assets to receivables as billing events occurred;
* the receipt of customer advances and performance-based payments;
* the recognition of revenue from previously deferred amounts;
* cumulative catch-up adjustments arising from changes in estimates; and
* the recognition of loss provisions, which reduce related contract asset balances before any remaining amount is recorded as a liability.

A significant portion of the contract liability balance at the beginning of fiscal year 2023 was recognized as revenue during the year as we performed under the related contracts. The net effect of changes in contract assets and contract liabilities is included in changes in working capital and other, net, in our consolidated statement of cash flows. Those changes, together with all other working capital and non-cash items, provided net cash of $11,449,721 in fiscal year 2023.

We did not recognize impairment losses on contract assets in fiscal year 2023, other than reductions attributable to loss provisions.

### Receivables

Receivables are included in other assets and consist principally of amounts billed and currently due from customers. A substantial portion of our receivables is due from the U.S. Government, or from prime contractors performing on U.S. Government programs. Billed receivables are generally collected within customary payment terms. Payments on U.S. Government contracts may be affected by government shutdowns, payment withholds arising from business system reviews, and contract administration delays.

## Note 4 — Inventories

Inventories were $274,888,695 at the end of fiscal year 2023. They represented 6.8% of total assets and 13.9% of fiscal year 2023 revenues, and were equivalent to approximately 41.7% of fiscal year 2023 cost of revenue.

Inventories consist of:

* **Raw materials and purchased parts.** Metals and alloys, rare earth magnet materials, lithium-ion cells and cell materials, thermal battery and energetic materials, electronic components (including power semiconductors and radiation-tolerant microelectronics), and engineered components such as castings, forgings, and precision machined parts. This category includes safety stock established for selected critical items and materials acquired under longer-term purchase commitments.
* **Work in process.** Partially completed products on contracts and orders accounted for at a point in time, principally commercial aerospace products and aftermarket spares.
* **Finished goods.** Completed products awaiting shipment, principally commercial aerospace products, standard catalog products, and aftermarket spare parts.

Inventories are presented net of reserves for excess, obsolete, and slow-moving items.

Our inventory levels reflect measures we adopted in response to supply chain conditions, which affected our operations in fiscal year 2023. Those conditions included extended lead times for electronic components, castings, and forgings; supplier capacity constraints; labor shortages at suppliers; inflationary pressures; and quality escapes at certain suppliers. In response, we placed longer-term purchase commitments for constrained materials and established safety stock for selected critical items. These measures reduce delivery risk, but they also increase our inventory and working capital requirements.

Certain materials in inventory, including energetic materials and battery cells, have limited shelf lives or specialized storage requirements. We consider these characteristics in evaluating the need for inventory reserves. If program quantities are reduced, designs change, programs are canceled, or demand is lower than anticipated, a portion of our inventory could become excess or obsolete, and we could be required to record additional reserves. Where inventory relates to U.S. Government contracts that are terminated for convenience, we may recover certain costs through termination settlements.

Where the U.S. Government holds title to inventory under progress payment clauses, the related inventory remains on our consolidated balance sheet, and progress payments received are recorded as contract liabilities or as a reduction of the related contract balances, as appropriate.

### Convertible Senior Notes

Our long-term debt consists of convertible senior notes (the "Notes"). Their principal terms are as follows:

* **Ranking.** The Notes are senior unsecured obligations of Riverton Energy Group, Inc. They rank equal in right of payment with any of our existing and future senior unsecured indebtedness, and senior in right of payment to any indebtedness that is expressly subordinated to them. They are effectively subordinated to any secured indebtedness to the extent of the value of the collateral. They are structurally subordinated to all indebtedness and other liabilities of our subsidiaries.
* **Interest.** The Notes do not bear regular interest, and the principal amount of the Notes does not accrete. The Notes may bear special interest under limited circumstances relating to our failure to comply with certain reporting obligations under the indenture governing the Notes. No special interest was incurred in fiscal year 2023.
* **Maturity.** The Notes mature in a future fiscal year more than twelve months after the end of fiscal year 2023, unless earlier converted, redeemed, or repurchased.
* **Conversion.** Holders may convert their Notes at their option, under specified circumstances before a specified date, and at any time thereafter until shortly before maturity. Those circumstances relate principally to the trading price of our common stock relative to the conversion price, the trading price of the Notes, the occurrence of specified corporate events, and our call of the Notes for redemption. The conversion rate is subject to adjustment upon the occurrence of certain events, but it will not be adjusted for any accrued and unpaid special interest.
* **Settlement upon conversion.** Upon conversion, we will pay cash up to the aggregate principal amount of the Notes being converted. We will pay or deliver, at our election, cash, shares of our common stock, or a combination of cash and shares for any conversion obligation in excess of that principal amount.
* **Optional redemption.** We may redeem the Notes, in whole or in part, at our option on or after a specified date and before maturity, subject to conditions relating to the trading price of our common stock. The redemption price is equal to the principal amount of the Notes to be redeemed, plus any accrued and unpaid special interest.
* **Fundamental change.** If we undergo a fundamental change, as defined in the indenture, holders may require us to repurchase their Notes for cash at a price equal to the principal amount, plus any accrued and unpaid special interest. In addition, if specified corporate events occur, or if we deliver a notice of redemption, we will in certain circumstances increase the conversion rate for holders who convert their Notes in connection with those events.

The conversion conditions were not satisfied during fiscal year 2023, and the Notes were not convertible at the option of the holders at the end of fiscal year 2023. Accordingly, the Notes are classified as long-term debt. We will reassess the classification of the Notes each reporting period, based on whether the conversion conditions are satisfied and whether we have the intent and ability to settle the principal amount of any converted Notes with long-term financing.

### Accounting for the Notes

We account for the Notes as a single liability measured at amortized cost, in accordance with ASU 2020-06. The embedded conversion feature is not separately accounted for. It is indexed to our own stock, qualifies for equity classification, and does not require bifurcation as a derivative.

The carrying amount of the Notes was $785,521,524 at the end of fiscal year 2023, which equals the outstanding principal amount of the Notes. The Notes do not bear regular interest, and costs incurred in connection with their issuance were not significant and were expensed in the period of issuance. As a result, we recognized no interest expense related to the Notes in fiscal year 2023, and our consolidated statement of operations reflects no interest expense for the year. We made no cash payments of interest in fiscal year 2023.

The Notes are included in the computation of diluted earnings per share using the if-converted method, as described in Note 14. Because the principal amount of the Notes must be settled in cash upon conversion, only the conversion spread, if any, has a dilutive effect.

### Covenants

The indenture governing the Notes contains customary covenants, including requirements to file certain reports with the SEC and restrictions on our ability to consolidate, merge, or sell all or substantially all of our assets. It does not contain financial maintenance covenants, and it does not restrict our ability to pay dividends, incur additional indebtedness, or repurchase our securities. The indenture contains customary events of default. If an event of default occurs and is continuing, the trustee or the holders of a specified percentage of the outstanding Notes may declare the principal amount of the Notes, together with any accrued and unpaid special interest, to be immediately due and payable. We were in compliance with the covenants under the indenture at the end of fiscal year 2023.

### Deferred Tax Assets and Liabilities

Deferred tax assets and liabilities reflect the tax effects of temporary differences between the financial reporting and tax bases of assets and liabilities, and of tax credit and loss carryforwards. Our principal deferred tax assets arise from:

* **Tax credit carryforwards.** State research and development tax credit carryforwards, and federal credits where applicable.
* **Contract-related temporary differences.** These include accrued contract loss provisions, as well as differences between financial reporting and tax recognition of revenue and costs on long-term contracts.

* **Stock-based compensation.** Compensation cost recognized for awards that have not yet vested or been exercised.
* **Inventory reserves and capitalization.** Inventory reserves and costs capitalized into inventory for tax purposes.
* **Environmental and legal accruals and warranty reserves.**
* **Operating lease liabilities.**

Our principal deferred tax liabilities arise from:

* differences between financial reporting and tax depreciation of property, plant and equipment;
* the amortization of goodwill and intangible assets for tax purposes;
* operating lease right-of-use assets; and
* prepaid expenses and other items.

Net deferred tax assets are included in other assets. Deferred tax liabilities in jurisdictions where they are not offset by deferred tax assets are included in other liabilities.

### Valuation Allowance

We evaluate the realizability of our deferred tax assets each reporting period. In making this assessment, we consider projected future taxable income, the reversal of existing taxable temporary differences, tax planning strategies, and our recent earnings history. Our history of pretax income, including income before income taxes of $203,907,274 in fiscal year 2023, together with our projections of future taxable income, constitutes significant positive evidence regarding the realizability of our federal deferred tax assets.

We maintain a valuation allowance against certain state research and development tax credit carryforwards that we do not expect to use before they expire, because our credit generation in those states exceeds our expected tax liability there. We believe it is more likely than not that our remaining deferred tax assets will be realized.

### Unrecognized Tax Benefits

We recognize the tax benefit of an uncertain tax position only if it is more likely than not to be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. Our unrecognized tax benefits relate principally to research and development tax credits, which require analysis of qualifying activities and expenditures across a large population of projects and are subject to examination. If recognized, a substantial portion of our unrecognized tax benefits would affect our effective tax rate.

Changes in unrecognized tax benefits during fiscal year 2023 reflected:

* additions for tax positions related to the current year, principally research and development tax credits;
* additions and reductions for tax positions of prior years; and
* reductions attributable to the lapse of statutes of limitations and settlements.

We recognize interest and penalties related to unrecognized tax benefits within income tax expense. Interest and penalties were not significant in fiscal year 2023.

It is reasonably possible that our unrecognized tax benefits will change within the next twelve months because of the resolution of examinations or the expiration of statutes of limitations. We do not expect any such change to have a material effect on our consolidated financial statements.

### Tax Examinations

We file income tax returns in the U.S. federal jurisdiction, in Arizona, in other states in which we operate, and in certain foreign jurisdictions. Our tax returns are subject to examination by the Internal Revenue Service and state and local tax authorities for various periods. We believe we have adequately provided for any adjustments that may result from examinations of open tax years. However, the outcome of examinations is uncertain. Adverse outcomes could result in additional taxes, interest, and penalties.

### Cash Taxes

Our cash tax payments may differ from our income tax expense. The capitalization and amortization of research and experimental expenditures for tax purposes increases our cash tax payments relative to our income tax expense in the near term. It does not affect our total income tax expense, because the resulting deferred tax assets reverse over the amortization period. We continue to monitor proposed legislative changes that could alter this treatment.
