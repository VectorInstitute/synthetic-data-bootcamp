# ITEM 1A. RISK FACTORS

Our business, financial condition, results of operations, liquidity, and cash flows are subject to many risks. You should consider carefully the risks described below, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations" in Item 7, "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk" in Item 7A, and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8.

The risks described below are not the only ones we face. Additional risks that we do not currently know about, or that we currently consider immaterial, may also affect us. Any of these risks could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, liquidity, financial strength ratings, or the trading price of our common stock. Many of these risks are interrelated, and the occurrence of one may increase the likelihood or severity of others. The order in which the risks appear is not an indication of their relative likelihood or significance.

This Item 1A contains forward-looking statements. Our actual results could differ materially from those anticipated in these statements because of the factors described below and elsewhere in this Annual Report. See "Forward-Looking Statements" in Item 1.

## Summary of Risk Factors

The following is a summary of the principal risks that could adversely affect our business. It is qualified in its entirety by the more complete discussion that follows.

- Mortality, morbidity, longevity, and policyholder behavior experience that differs from our pricing and reserving assumptions could reduce our profitability and require us to strengthen reserves.
- Pandemics, other public health events, and catastrophes could cause claims to rise sharply across our individual and group businesses at the same time.
- Our run-off long-term care block is exposed to significant long-term uncertainty, and our ability to obtain premium rate increases is subject to regulatory approval.
- Our reserves are estimates, and changes in assumptions, discount rates, or regulatory reserve requirements could materially affect our GAAP and statutory results.
- Changes in interest rates, whether sustained low rates or sharp increases, could compress our spreads, increase surrenders, reduce the value of our investments, and increase our capital requirements.
- Credit deterioration, defaults, and declines in the value of our investments, including commercial mortgage loans, private placements, structured securities, and alternative investments, could reduce our earnings and capital.
- Our hedging programs for indexed products and other market exposures may not be effective.
- As a holding company, we depend on dividends from our insurance subsidiaries, which are subject to regulatory limits.
- Increases in regulatory capital requirements, adverse changes to our reserve financing arrangements, or limited access to capital markets could constrain our growth and our ability to return capital to shareholders.
- A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could harm our sales, increase surrenders and collateral requirements, and raise our cost of capital.
- We depend on reinsurance, and reinsurer credit risk, rate increases, or reduced availability could adversely affect us.
- We face intense competition, including from insurers affiliated with alternative asset managers and from technology-oriented entrants.
- We depend on independent and affiliated distribution, and the loss of key relationships or the inability to recruit and retain career agents could reduce our sales.
- Cyberattacks, data breaches, technology failures, and failures of third-party service providers could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.
- Our models, including predictive and artificial intelligence tools used in underwriting and claims, may not perform as intended and are subject to evolving regulation.
- We are subject to extensive and changing regulation, including standards of conduct for sales of annuities and life insurance, rules governing supplemental health products, privacy and data security requirements, and tax laws that affect our products.
- We are subject to litigation, regulatory examinations, and investigations that could result in significant liabilities and reputational harm.
- Climate change and related regulation could affect our investments, our insured populations, and our operations.
- Insurance laws, Georgia corporate law, and our governing documents could delay or prevent a change of control.

## Risks Related to Our Insurance and Annuity Businesses

***Mortality experience that differs significantly from our pricing and reserving assumptions could adversely affect our results of operations and financial condition.***

We price our individual life, group life, and accidental death and dismemberment products, and establish reserves for them, using assumptions about the rates at which insured persons will die. These assumptions draw on our own experience, industry studies, reinsurer input, and actuarial judgment. Actual mortality may differ from our assumptions for many reasons, including:

- changes in the prevalence of diseases and medical conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, obesity, substance use disorders, and respiratory illness;
- changes in medical treatment, access to care, and public health conditions;
- the effects of pandemics and other events that cause excess deaths;
- societal trends, including rates of accidental death, overdose, and suicide;
- anti-selection by applicants or policyholders, including in products underwritten without medical examinations or fluid testing; and
- random statistical fluctuation, particularly in blocks with large face amounts or concentrations of risk.

Our assumptions also incorporate expectations about future mortality improvement. If mortality improvement is slower than we have assumed, or if mortality deteriorates, our claims could exceed our expectations for extended periods. Because many of our individual life policies, including our in-force block of universal life with secondary guarantees, have guaranteed premiums or charges, our ability to recover adverse experience through pricing adjustments on in-force business is limited. Although we use reinsurance to limit our retention on any one life, reinsurance does not protect us against broad-based adverse mortality across our retained business. Adverse mortality experience could reduce our earnings, require us to increase our liabilities for future policy benefits, and reduce the statutory capital of our insurance subsidiaries.

***Morbidity, disability incidence, and claim recovery experience that differs from our assumptions could reduce the profitability of our Group Benefits and Supplemental Health segments and our run-off businesses.***

The profitability of our group disability, supplemental health, dental, and vision products, and of our run-off long-term care block, depends heavily on the frequency, severity, and duration of claims. Our long-term disability business is particularly sensitive to claim incidence and to the rate at which claimants recover, return to work, or otherwise cease receiving benefits. These rates can be influenced by:

- economic conditions, including unemployment and layoffs, which have historically been associated with higher disability incidence and lower recovery rates;
- changes in medical diagnosis and treatment practices, including the growing incidence of mental health and musculoskeletal claims;
- the long-term health effects of infectious diseases;
- changes in the availability and administration of Social Security disability benefits and other offsets that reduce our benefit payments;
- changes in the legal environment, including court decisions affecting ERISA claims standards and the interpretation of policy definitions; and
- the effectiveness of our claims management, nurse case management, and vocational rehabilitation programs.

In our Supplemental Health segment, benefits are triggered by specified events such as accidents, hospital admissions, and diagnoses of covered illnesses. Changes in health care utilization patterns, including deferred care that later returns at elevated levels, changes in the setting in which care is delivered (for example, shifts from inpatient to outpatient or observation settings), advances in diagnostic screening that increase the detection of covered conditions, and increases in claims submission resulting from our own wellness and engagement campaigns, could cause claims to rise above our pricing assumptions. In our dental and vision businesses, utilization and the cost of provider services may increase faster than we can reprice. If morbidity experience is worse than we expect, our margins could decline, and we may need to strengthen reserves.

***Pandemics and other public health events could materially increase our claims and disrupt our operations.***

A pandemic or other widespread public health event could adversely affect our business in multiple ways at the same time. Such an event could:

- increase mortality claims in our Individual Life segment and in our group life and accidental death coverages;
- increase short-term and long-term disability claims, including claims arising from long-term health effects of infection;
- change utilization of supplemental health, dental, and vision benefits in ways that are difficult to predict, including temporary declines followed by increases;
- reduce employment and covered payrolls at our employer clients, lowering premiums and persistency in our workplace businesses;
- disrupt financial markets, reduce the value of our investments, and increase credit losses;
- interfere with our ability to underwrite, enroll, and service business, particularly through face-to-face worksite channels; and
- impair the operations of our distribution partners, vendors, and reinsurers.

Because our Group Benefits segment is our largest segment by premium, and because group life and disability claims tend to be correlated during broad public health events, a severe pandemic could produce losses in multiple segments simultaneously. Although longevity exposures in our income annuity and group annuity businesses could partially offset higher mortality losses, any offset would likely be modest and would not occur in the same periods. Our catastrophe reinsurance coverage is subject to deductibles, limits, and exclusions, and it may not respond to, or may be insufficient for, a pandemic event.

***Catastrophic events and concentrations of insured lives could result in losses that exceed our expectations and our reinsurance protection.***

Natural disasters, terrorist attacks, industrial accidents, mass-casualty incidents, and other catastrophic events could cause a large number of deaths, injuries, or disabilities among the people we insure. Our workplace businesses expose us to concentrations of insured lives at individual employer locations, and our worksite marketing efforts emphasize small and mid-sized employers in particular regions. A single event affecting a densely populated area or a large employer facility could produce claims across our group life, accidental death and dismemberment, disability, supplemental health, and individual life products at the same time.

We maintain catastrophe reinsurance covering accumulations of life and accidental death losses from single events, but this protection is subject to deductibles, limits, and exclusions, including exclusions that may apply to certain acts of terrorism, nuclear, biological, chemical, or radiological events, and pandemics. The cost of catastrophe reinsurance may increase, and its availability may decline, particularly after significant industry losses. Catastrophic events could also disrupt our operations, which are concentrated in Georgia, and adversely affect the value of our investments, including commercial mortgage loans secured by properties in affected regions.

***Longevity experience that differs from our assumptions could adversely affect our income annuity, group annuity, and long-term care businesses.***

Our single premium immediate annuities, deferred income annuities, structured settlement annuities, and closed blocks of pension risk transfer business obligate us to make payments for the lives of the annuitants. Our fixed indexed annuities with guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit riders similarly expose us to the risk that contract holders live longer than expected while receiving guaranteed income after their account values are exhausted. If annuitants live longer than our assumptions, whether because of medical advances, improved access to care, lifestyle changes, or other factors, our payments will exceed our expectations, and we may need to increase our reserves.

Longevity risk also affects our run-off long-term care block. Longer lives may increase the period during which policyholders are at risk of needing care and the duration of claims in payment. Unlike mortality risk, longevity risk generally emerges gradually over long periods, and adverse trends may not become apparent until they have been embedded in our in-force business for years. Our ability to mitigate longevity risk through reinsurance or other risk transfer transactions depends on market capacity and pricing, which may be unfavorable when we wish to transact.

***Policyholder and contract holder behavior that differs from our assumptions could adversely affect our results, reserves, and capital.***

Our pricing and reserving depend on assumptions about how policyholders and contract holders will exercise the options available to them. These include decisions to:

- lapse, surrender, or partially withdraw from their policies and contracts;
- continue paying premiums, and in what amounts, on flexible premium universal life policies;
- fund universal life policies with secondary guarantees at levels that keep the guarantees in force;
- convert term policies to permanent coverage without new evidence of insurability;
- take policy loans;
- elect and begin taking withdrawals under guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit riders; and
- annuitize or reallocate among crediting strategies.

Policyholder behavior can be difficult to predict and may change with economic conditions, interest rates, market performance, the actions of competitors and distributors, tax law, and the emergence of secondary markets for life insurance policies. For example, lower-than-expected lapse rates on lapse-supported products such as term life and universal life with secondary guarantees would increase the number of policies that remain in force to pay death benefits, reducing our profitability. Conversely, policyholders who are healthier may be more likely to lapse at the end of a level premium term period, leaving us with a less healthy remaining population. Higher-than-expected surrenders of annuity contracts during periods of rising interest rates could require us to sell assets at a loss to fund withdrawals. Greater-than-expected utilization of lifetime income riders would increase our obligations. If actual behavior differs materially from our assumptions, we may need to increase reserves, record losses, or hold additional capital.

***Our run-off long-term care block is subject to substantial uncertainty, and adverse developments could materially affect our results and capital.***

Although we no longer sell long-term care insurance, we retain an in-force block of individual long-term care policies issued in prior years, which we report in Corporate and Other. Long-term care insurance involves projections of morbidity, mortality, persistency, claim incidence, claim duration, the cost of care, and investment returns over very long periods, often extending decades into the future. Industry experience has repeatedly demonstrated that original pricing assumptions for these products were inadequate, particularly with respect to lapse rates, mortality improvement, and interest rates.

Our management of this block depends substantially on our ability to obtain premium rate increases from state insurance regulators where actuarially justified. Rate increase approvals are discretionary, may be granted in amounts lower than requested or phased in over time, and vary significantly by state. Regulators may refuse or delay approval, and policyholders may respond to rate increases by electing reduced benefit options or lapsing in ways that differ from our assumptions. We are also subject to litigation and regulatory scrutiny regarding the adequacy of disclosures concerning rate increases and benefit reduction options.

The partial reinsurance of this block exposes us to reinsurer credit risk and to the risk of disputes over reinsurance terms. Because long-term care claims may persist for many years, changes in the cost of care, care delivery models, and family caregiving patterns could increase claim severity. Adverse developments in any of these areas could require us to strengthen reserves, which could materially reduce our earnings and the statutory capital of Ironwood Life.

***Our accelerated underwriting programs and use of predictive models in risk selection may not produce the mortality experience we expect.***

We have expanded accelerated underwriting so that a growing share of qualifying applicants for individual life coverage can obtain coverage without traditional medical examinations or fluid testing. These programs rely on predictive models, automated rules engines, and electronic data sources, including prescription drug history, motor vehicle records, medical information bureau data, and, where permitted, credit-based and other third-party data. Our final expense and simplified-issue products rely on abbreviated health questionnaires and electronic data checks.

The mortality experience of business underwritten through these programs has a limited history, both for us and for the industry, and may prove worse than our pricing assumptions. Applicants may misrepresent their health or habits, and the absence of fluid testing and examinations may make misrepresentation harder to detect. The data sources on which our models depend may be incomplete, inaccurate, or unavailable, and regulatory restrictions on the use of external consumer data could limit the information available to us. Our post-issue audits and monitoring may not identify adverse trends quickly enough to allow timely corrective action. If our accelerated programs produce worse-than-expected mortality, we may need to tighten eligibility, reprice products, or strengthen reserves, any of which could reduce sales and profitability.

***Competitive pricing pressure, multi-year rate guarantees, and renewal dynamics could reduce the profitability of our Group Benefits segment.***

Group insurance contracts are generally renewable annually, but multi-year rate guarantees are common, particularly for group life and long-term disability coverage. When experience deteriorates during a rate guarantee period, we generally cannot adjust premiums until the guarantee expires. At renewal, our ability to raise rates may be constrained by competitive conditions, by employer and consultant resistance, and in some cases by regulatory requirements. Employers and benefits consultants frequently market their benefit programs, and large-case pricing is particularly competitive. If we raise rates to restore profitability, we may lose cases to competitors, and if we hold rates to retain business, our margins may decline.

Our pricing for larger cases relies partly on the group's own claims experience, blended with manual rates according to its credibility. If the experience on which we rely is not predictive of future claims, or if our manual rates do not adequately reflect industry, geographic, or demographic risk, our pricing could prove inadequate. Changes in the composition of our book, including shifts in industry mix or employer size, could also affect our aggregate results in ways that our pricing does not anticipate.

***The profitability of our workplace businesses depends on employment levels, payroll growth, and employee participation.***

Premiums in our Group Benefits and Supplemental Health segments depend on the number of employees covered at our employer clients, their salary levels for salary-based coverages, and employee participation in voluntary and employee-paid products. Economic downturns, layoffs, reductions in hours, business failures, and slower wage growth could reduce covered lives and premiums. Higher employee turnover reduces persistency in our voluntary supplemental health and supplemental life products, which are generally paid through payroll deduction and may lapse when an employee leaves the employer. Our worksite products also depend on employers' willingness to permit enrollment events and payroll deduction, and on the effectiveness of our enrollment and education efforts.

Small and mid-sized employers, which are a focus of our strategy, may be more vulnerable to economic stress than larger employers. A deterioration in conditions affecting these employers could reduce sales and persistency at the same time that disability incidence rises, compounding the adverse effect on our results.

***Our closed block of participating whole life insurance and other run-off blocks may perform worse than expected.***

We maintain a closed block of participating whole life policies acquired in a prior transaction, which is reported in Corporate and Other. The assets and cash flows of the closed block are generally used to pay benefits and policyholder dividends on the policies in the block. If the performance of the closed block is worse than anticipated, because of adverse mortality, investment results, or persistency, we may be required to reduce policyholder dividends to the extent permitted, or to support the block with assets outside the closed block, which would reduce our earnings and capital. Policyholder dividend actions may also be subject to regulatory review and may expose us to litigation or reputational harm. Other run-off blocks, including our long-term care block, similarly consume management attention and capital without contributing to future growth.

***Our absence management and leave administration services expose us to operational, compliance, and legal risks.***

Through Ironwood Benefits Administrators, LLC, we administer leave under the federal Family and Medical Leave Act, numerous state and municipal leave laws, and statutory disability and paid family leave programs, and we support workplace accommodation under the Americans with Disabilities Act. The number, complexity, and variability of these laws has grown significantly and continues to change. New state paid leave programs may require us to build new capabilities quickly, to obtain additional licenses, or to issue insured coverage under new regulatory regimes.

Errors in administering leave or accommodation processes could expose our employer clients to claims by employees, and could expose us to contractual liability, indemnification obligations, regulatory penalties, and reputational harm. Because our absence management services are integrated with our insured disability products, operational failures in one area could affect client relationships across our Group Benefits segment. If we cannot keep pace with changes in leave laws or maintain the quality of our administration, our competitive differentiation in this area could erode.

## Risks Related to Reserves, Assumptions, and Accounting

***Our reserves are estimates and may prove inadequate, which could require us to increase our liabilities and reduce our earnings and capital.***

We establish liabilities for future policy benefits, policyholder account balances, unpaid claims, and claim adjustment expenses based on assumptions about mortality, morbidity, longevity, persistency, policyholder behavior, expenses, interest rates, and other factors. These liabilities are estimates of amounts that will be paid over many years, sometimes over decades, and cannot be determined with precision. Our liabilities for long-term disability claims in course of payment and for our run-off long-term care block are particularly sensitive to assumptions about claim duration and recovery.

We review our actuarial assumptions at least annually, generally in the third quarter, and update them when experience or other information indicates that a change is warranted. Changes in assumptions can significantly affect our results in the period in which they are made. Our reserves may need to increase because of:

- emerging experience that differs from our assumptions;
- changes in regulatory requirements or actuarial standards;
- changes in our methodologies or models;
- court decisions or regulatory actions that expand our obligations; and
- macroeconomic changes that affect the cost of care, claimant behavior, or investment returns.

If our reserves prove inadequate, we would be required to increase them, which would reduce our net income and stockholders' equity and could reduce the statutory capital and dividend capacity of our insurance subsidiaries. Any significant reserve increase could also lead rating agencies to review our ratings.

***Our adoption of the long-duration targeted improvements accounting guidance has increased the volatility of our reported results and stockholders' equity.***

Effective January 1, 2023, we adopted accounting guidance that significantly changed the measurement of our long-duration insurance contracts. Under this guidance, liabilities for future policy benefits on traditional long-duration contracts are measured using current assumptions that we review at least annually, and they are discounted at an upper-medium grade fixed income instrument yield that is updated at each reporting date, with changes in the discount rate effect recognized in accumulated other comprehensive income. Market risk benefits, including certain guaranteed benefits associated with our annuity products, are measured at fair value, with most changes recognized in net income.

As a result, our reported stockholders' equity is more sensitive to changes in market interest rates, and our net income is more sensitive to changes in interest rates, equity markets, and our own credit standing as it affects the fair value of market risk benefits. The effect of discount rate changes on our liabilities may not be offset by changes in the reported value of the assets supporting those liabilities, because those assets are measured on different bases. This accounting volatility may not reflect the underlying economics of our business, may make period-to-period comparisons more difficult, and may make our results harder for investors and analysts to understand. Our non-GAAP measures, including adjusted operating income, exclude certain of these effects, but investors may not find those measures useful, and they are not substitutes for GAAP measures.

***Statutory reserve requirements, including principle-based reserving and actuarial guidelines for products with secondary guarantees and guaranteed living benefits, could increase our capital needs.***

Our insurance subsidiaries establish reserves under statutory accounting principles, which form the basis for regulatory capital, risk-based capital, and dividend capacity. For certain life insurance products issued in recent years, statutory reserves are determined using a principle-based reserving framework that relies on company-specific experience and stochastic modeling of economic scenarios. This framework introduces sensitivity to our own experience and to the economic scenario generators prescribed by regulators, which the NAIC has been updating. Changes in prescribed scenarios, assumptions, or methodologies could increase our statutory reserves.

Actuarial guidelines governing reserves for universal life with secondary guarantees and for annuity products with guaranteed living benefits can require statutory reserves well above the levels we consider economically necessary. We finance some of these requirements through Ironwood Re and through reinsurance with unaffiliated reinsurers. If regulators change these guidelines, if our appointed actuaries' asset adequacy analyses require additional reserves under moderately adverse conditions, or if our reserve financing arrangements become unavailable or more expensive, our insurance subsidiaries could be required to hold additional statutory reserves and capital, which could reduce their ability to pay dividends to the holding company and constrain our ability to write new business.

***Changes in accounting standards, and the critical estimates and judgments we make in preparing our financial statements, could materially affect our reported results.***

Our financial statements are prepared in accordance with GAAP, and our insurance subsidiaries' statutory financial statements are prepared in accordance with statutory accounting principles. Both sets of principles are subject to change by standard setters and regulators, and changes can be difficult to predict and costly to implement. New standards may require us to change our systems, processes, and controls, and may change the timing or amount of the earnings we report.

Preparing our financial statements requires us to make estimates and judgments that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses. Our most significant estimates involve:

- liabilities for future policy benefits and unpaid claims;
- market risk benefits and embedded derivatives;
- the fair value of investments, particularly those without observable market prices;
- allowances for credit losses on investments, mortgage loans, and reinsurance recoverables;
- the recoverability of deferred tax assets; and
- the carrying value of goodwill and other intangible assets arising from acquisitions, including the value of business acquired.

If actual results differ from our estimates, or if we change our estimates, we could be required to record charges, including impairments of goodwill or intangible assets or valuation allowances against deferred tax assets. Such charges could materially reduce our net income and stockholders' equity in the period in which they occur.

***If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, we may not be able to report our financial results accurately or on time.***

We are required under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting and to have our independent registered public accounting firm attest to its effectiveness. Our financial reporting processes are complex. They depend on actuarial models, investment accounting systems, multiple policy administration platforms (including legacy systems acquired in prior transactions), and data from third parties. Our ongoing modernization of policy administration systems, the implementation of new accounting standards, and the integration of any future acquisitions could introduce control deficiencies. If we identify a material weakness, or if our controls fail to detect or prevent errors, we could be required to restate prior-period financial statements, our access to capital markets could be impaired, we could become subject to regulatory sanctions, and investors could lose confidence in our reported financial information, which could cause the price of our common stock to decline.

## Risks Related to Economic Conditions, Interest Rates, and Investments

***Adverse economic and financial market conditions could reduce demand for our products, increase claims, and reduce the value of our investments.***

Our business is sensitive to general economic conditions in the United States. A recession, a period of slow growth, elevated inflation, financial market disruption, or a deterioration in conditions affecting our customers and employer clients could adversely affect us in many ways, including:

- reducing demand for life insurance, supplemental health, and annuity products as households face financial strain;
- reducing covered lives and payrolls in our workplace businesses;
- increasing lapses, surrenders, and policy loans;
- increasing disability incidence and slowing recoveries;
- increasing defaults and credit losses in our investment portfolio and reducing the value of our investments;
- reducing fee income tied to account values;
- increasing the cost and reducing the availability of capital and credit; and
- increasing collateral requirements under derivative and reinsurance arrangements.

Elevated inflation could increase our operating expenses, including compensation and technology costs, and the cost of benefits that are linked to the cost of care or services, including in our dental and run-off long-term care businesses. Inflation could also increase benefit amounts on salary-based group coverages faster than we can reprice. Governmental responses to economic conditions, including changes in monetary and fiscal policy, may have unpredictable effects on interest rates, credit markets, and our business.

***Changes in interest rates could adversely affect our profitability, the value of our investments, our reserves, and our capital.***

Interest rates significantly affect our business, and both declining and rising rates present risks.

During periods of low or declining interest rates, we may be unable to reinvest the cash flows from our investment portfolio at yields sufficient to support our guaranteed minimum crediting rates on universal life and annuity products, which could compress our spreads. We may also experience increased prepayment of mortgage-backed securities and callable bonds, requiring reinvestment at lower yields. Products with long-duration guarantees, including universal life with secondary guarantees and our run-off long-term care block, become more expensive to support. Low rates can also increase our GAAP liabilities for future policy benefits and long-term disability claims through lower discount rates, increase our statutory reserves, and increase the cost of hedging guaranteed benefits.

During periods of rapidly rising interest rates, contract holders may surrender fixed and fixed indexed annuities or reallocate funds to seek higher returns elsewhere, a phenomenon known as disintermediation. Surrender charges and market value adjustments may not fully offset the effect of these surrenders. To fund surrenders, we may need to sell investments at prices below their carrying value, realizing losses. Rising rates also reduce the fair value of our fixed maturity securities, creating unrealized losses that reduce reported stockholders' equity and may affect our liquidity and flexibility. Competition from bank deposits, money market funds, and other products may intensify, and competitors with different investment strategies may offer crediting rates we cannot match profitably.

Changes in the shape of the yield curve, the spread between corporate and Treasury yields, and the volatility of rates can also affect our results in ways that are difficult to hedge. Our asset-liability management programs may not fully protect us against these risks, particularly if interest rate movements are sudden or large or if policyholder behavior differs from our assumptions.

***Defaults, downgrades, and other credit events in our investment portfolio could reduce our net investment income, increase our credit losses, and reduce our capital.***

Our general account investment portfolio consists predominantly of fixed maturity securities, including public and private corporate bonds, U.S. Treasury and agency securities, state and municipal obligations, residential and commercial mortgage-backed securities, collateralized loan obligations, and other asset-backed securities, as well as commercial mortgage loans and other invested assets. We are exposed to the risk that issuers, borrowers, and counterparties fail to make timely payments of principal and interest, or that their credit quality deteriorates.

Credit risk may increase during economic downturns, in particular industries or sectors, or because of issuer-specific events. Privately placed corporate debt, which is a significant component of our portfolio, is less liquid than publicly traded securities and may be more difficult to value or sell. Structured securities, including collateralized loan obligations, may experience losses that are amplified by their structure and by deterioration in the underlying collateral. Downgrades of our holdings, even without default, can increase the regulatory capital charges our insurance subsidiaries must hold, reduce the value of our investments, and limit our flexibility.

We evaluate our investments for credit losses and record allowances under our accounting policies. These evaluations involve significant judgment, and our allowances may prove insufficient. Credit losses and impairments would reduce our net income, stockholders' equity, and the statutory capital of our insurance subsidiaries.

***Our commercial mortgage loan portfolio and other real estate–related investments are exposed to risks in the commercial real estate market, including office properties.***

We originate commercial mortgage loans secured by income-producing properties diversified by property type and geography. The value of these loans depends on the ability of borrowers to make payments, which depends on property income, and on the value of the underlying properties. Changes in work patterns, including the persistence of remote and hybrid work, have reduced demand for office space in many markets, and conditions in the office sector could continue to weaken. Higher interest rates have increased borrowers' refinancing costs and may make it difficult for some borrowers to refinance maturing loans. Retail, hospitality, and other property types are also exposed to changes in consumer behavior and economic conditions.

Although we underwrite our loans to conservative loan-to-value and debt service coverage standards, property values could decline significantly, and borrowers may default, seek loan modifications, or extend maturities. Foreclosure and workout processes can be lengthy and costly, and we may be required to own and operate properties for a period of time. Deterioration in commercial real estate could increase our credit loss allowances, reduce our net investment income, and increase regulatory capital charges. Our residential mortgage loans, mortgage-backed securities, and real estate fund investments are exposed to similar risks.

***Our alternative and illiquid investments may produce volatile returns and may be difficult to value or sell.***

Our other invested assets include limited partnership interests in private equity, private credit, and real estate funds. Although we limit our allocation to these investments, their returns can be volatile and are generally reported on a lag, so declines in value may affect our results in periods after the underlying market changes. These investments are generally illiquid, may require us to fund capital commitments when called, and may be subject to restrictions on transfer. They also attract higher regulatory capital charges.

More generally, a portion of our investments do not have readily observable market prices, and we must estimate their fair values using models and assumptions that involve significant judgment. During periods of market disruption, more of our investments may become illiquid or difficult to value, and the prices we could realize upon a sale might be significantly lower than our estimates. If we were required to sell illiquid investments to meet liquidity needs, we might realize substantial losses. Changes in the valuation of our investments could reduce our reported stockholders' equity and the capital of our insurance subsidiaries.

***Equity market volatility and the limitations of our hedging programs could adversely affect our indexed products and our results.***

Our indexed universal life and fixed indexed annuity products credit interest linked to the performance of external market indices, subject to caps, participation rates, spreads, and guaranteed minimums. We hedge these index-linked crediting obligations primarily with purchased call options and similar instruments, which we generally rebalance each crediting period. Our fixed indexed annuities with guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit riders also expose us to the risk that poor market performance reduces account values and increases the value of the guarantee.

Our hedging programs may not be fully effective. Risks include:

- basis risk, where the performance of our hedging instruments does not match the performance of the liabilities;
- increases in the cost of options during periods of high market volatility, which may reduce our margins if we cannot adjust caps and participation rates promptly on in-force business;
- policyholder behavior that differs from our assumptions, causing our hedge positions to be mismatched;
- the inability to obtain hedges at acceptable prices, or at all, during periods of market stress; and
- model risk in determining hedge positions.

Equity market declines could also reduce the value of our limited equity and alternative investments and increase the fair value of market risk benefits, reducing our net income. Our hedging programs are designed to manage economic exposure and may not offset accounting volatility, which could cause our reported results to fluctuate.

***We are exposed to counterparty risk on our derivatives and other financial arrangements.***

We use derivatives, including equity index options, interest rate swaps and swaptions, foreign currency swaps, and, selectively, credit default swaps, to hedge our exposures. We are exposed to the risk that our derivative counterparties fail to perform. Although we manage counterparty risk through master netting agreements, collateral arrangements, and central clearing where applicable, collateral may be insufficient to cover our exposure, particularly during periods of rapid market change. Central clearing exposes us to the risk of default by clearinghouses and clearing members. In addition, our derivative arrangements require us to post collateral when market movements are adverse to us, which can create significant and sudden liquidity demands. The failure of a major financial institution could also disrupt the markets in which we transact and impair our ability to hedge.

## Risks Related to Liquidity, Capital, and Our Holding Company Structure

***As a holding company, we depend on the ability of our subsidiaries to pay dividends, which is subject to regulatory restrictions.***

Ironwood Logistics Group, Inc. is a holding company with no significant operations of its own. Its principal assets are its ownership interests in its subsidiaries, and its principal sources of liquidity are dividends and other permitted payments from those subsidiaries. We use these funds to pay shareholder dividends, service our corporate debt, repurchase shares, pay operating expenses, and contribute capital to subsidiaries when needed.

State insurance laws limit the amount of dividends our insurance subsidiaries may pay without prior regulatory approval, generally based on statutory surplus and statutory net gain from operations or net income. Dividends above these limits are considered extraordinary and require prior approval, and some states require notice of all dividends before payment. Insurers may generally pay dividends only from unassigned or earned surplus, and regulators may restrict or prohibit dividends if they determine that payment would be adverse to policyholders. Dividend capacity may decline because of adverse experience, reserve increases, investment losses, increased capital requirements, or regulatory action. Rating agency expectations regarding subsidiary capital may further limit the amounts our subsidiaries can prudently distribute.

Transactions between our insurance subsidiaries and their affiliates, including service, cost-sharing, tax allocation, and reinsurance agreements, are subject to regulatory review and approval requirements. If our subsidiaries cannot pay sufficient dividends or make other payments to the holding company, we may be unable to meet our obligations, pay dividends on our common stock, or repurchase shares, and we may be required to raise capital on unfavorable terms.

***Our balance sheet is significantly leveraged by policyholder and other obligations relative to stockholders' equity, which magnifies the effect of adverse developments on our capital.***

As of December 31, 2023, we reported total assets of $136,747,486,447, total liabilities of $113,004,344,000, and total stockholders' equity of $23,743,142,447. Our liabilities, which consist principally of obligations to policyholders and contract holders, together with corporate debt and other liabilities, represented approximately 82.6% of our total assets. Stockholders' equity represented approximately 17.4% of total assets, and our ratio of total liabilities to total stockholders' equity was approximately 4.76 to 1. Put differently, each dollar of stockholders' equity supported approximately $5.76 of total assets.

Because our assets substantially exceed our equity, a relatively small percentage decline in the value of our assets, or a relatively small percentage increase in our liabilities, can have a proportionally much larger effect on our equity. For illustration only, a decline equal to 1% of our total assets as of December 31, 2023 would equal approximately $1.37 billion, or approximately 5.8% of our total stockholders' equity at that date. This illustration does not reflect any offsetting change in liabilities, tax effects, or the accounting basis of particular assets, and it is not a prediction of future results. Investment losses, reserve increases, adverse changes in the fair value of market risk benefits, or other developments that affect a large balance sheet could therefore materially reduce our stockholders' equity, our statutory capital, and our financial flexibility.

***Increases in regulatory capital requirements or changes in solvency standards could require us to hold additional capital and constrain our business.***

Our insurance subsidiaries are subject to risk-based capital requirements, and our insurance holding company group is subject to the group capital calculation and, for larger life insurance groups that meet specified scope criteria, the NAIC's liquidity stress test framework. Although each of our insurance subsidiaries had total adjusted capital above all levels that would require regulatory or company action as of December 31, 2023, our RBC ratios could decline because of adverse experience, investment losses, downgrades of our holdings, changes in the mix of business we write, reserve increases, or changes in the RBC formula.

The NAIC continually evaluates and updates the RBC formula and other solvency standards. Recent and ongoing developments include revisions to the treatment of structured securities and other asset classes, changes to the framework for designating privately rated securities, updates to interest rate and economic scenario generators, and enhanced scrutiny of reinsurance transactions with offshore and affiliated counterparties and of investments in complex or private assets. These developments could increase capital charges on investments we hold, including private placements and collateralized loan obligations, limit the investment strategies available to us, or affect the regulatory treatment of our reinsurance arrangements. If our capital requirements increase, we may need to reduce new business, change our investment strategy, enter into additional reinsurance on unfavorable terms, raise capital, or reduce dividends and share repurchases.

***Our liquidity could be strained by surrenders, collateral demands, maturing obligations, or disruptions in funding markets.***

Our insurance subsidiaries must maintain sufficient liquidity to pay claims, surrenders, withdrawals, and other obligations as they come due. Liquidity demands could increase suddenly because of:

- elevated annuity surrenders or withdrawals, particularly during periods of rising interest rates or following a ratings downgrade;
- collateral calls under derivative, reinsurance, and reserve financing arrangements;
- maturities of funding agreements, including those issued in connection with funding agreement-backed note programs;
- repayment of secured advances from the Federal Home Loan Bank of which Ironwood Life is a member; and
- a catastrophic event or pandemic producing large claims in a short period.

If our liquid assets are insufficient to meet these demands, we may need to sell investments, possibly at a loss, or seek financing, which may not be available on acceptable terms. Our access to Federal Home Loan Bank advances depends on our continued membership, the availability of eligible collateral, and the bank's policies. Changes to the Federal Home Loan Bank system, including changes to insurer membership eligibility or collateral requirements, could reduce or eliminate our access to this funding source. Our funding agreement programs depend on institutional investor demand and on our ratings, and disruptions in these markets could limit our ability to roll over maturing obligations.

***Our reserve financing arrangements, including those supporting Ironwood Re, may become unavailable, more expensive, or subject to adverse regulatory treatment.***

Ironwood Re, a special purpose financial captive domiciled in Vermont, reinsures certain term life and universal life with secondary guarantee business ceded by Ironwood Life, and finances the associated statutory reserves through arrangements with unaffiliated financial institutions in accordance with applicable regulation. These arrangements depend on the continued willingness of financial institutions to provide financing on acceptable terms, on the performance of the reinsured business, and on continued regulatory acceptance of the structure.

Regulators and the NAIC have adopted, and may continue to adopt, requirements that limit or condition the use of captive reinsurance to finance reserves, including standards governing the types of assets that may support reserves. Rating agencies may also view these arrangements less favorably. If financing becomes unavailable or more expensive upon renewal, if counterparties fail to perform, if the reinsured business performs worse than expected and triggers additional funding obligations, or if regulatory changes reduce the credit we receive for these arrangements, Ironwood Life could be required to hold significantly higher statutory reserves and capital. This could reduce its dividend capacity, require us to contribute capital, or require us to seek alternative financing on less favorable terms.

***We may be unable to access capital and credit markets on favorable terms, or at all, when we need financing.***

We may need to raise capital or obtain financing to refinance corporate debt, support the capital of our insurance subsidiaries, fund acquisitions, or maintain liquidity. Our access to capital markets depends on market conditions, our financial strength and credit ratings, our results of operations, and investor perceptions of our business and the insurance industry. Disruptions in the capital and credit markets could make financing unavailable or significantly more expensive. If we raise equity capital, existing shareholders could be diluted. If we incur additional debt, our leverage and interest expense would increase, and the terms of new debt could restrict our operating flexibility. Our inability to obtain financing when needed could adversely affect our ratings, our growth, and our ability to meet our obligations.

***A downgrade in our financial strength or credit ratings could materially harm our business.***

Financial strength ratings assigned to our insurance subsidiaries are important to our competitive position. Many employers, benefits consultants, and financial institutions require minimum ratings from the carriers with which they do business, and independent agents and consumers consider ratings when choosing among products. Credit ratings assigned to our holding company affect our cost of borrowing and our access to capital.

Rating agencies periodically review our ratings and may change them at any time, based on factors including capital adequacy, operating performance, business profile, investment portfolio quality, risk management, financial flexibility, and the economic and industry environment. Rating agencies may also change their methodologies or criteria in ways that adversely affect our ratings, independent of any change in our condition.

A downgrade, or the possibility of a downgrade, could:

- reduce new sales of our products, particularly annuities sold through financial institutions and group business sold through consultants;
- increase lapses and surrenders of existing policies and contracts;
- cause distribution partners to terminate or reduce their relationships with us;
- increase our cost of capital and reduce our access to financing;
- reduce investor demand for our funding agreement programs;
- trigger collateral posting requirements or termination rights under reinsurance, derivative, reserve financing, and other agreements; and
- require us to reduce crediting rates or increase commissions to remain competitive, reducing our margins.

Any of these consequences could materially and adversely affect our results of operations, liquidity, and financial condition.

***We may be unable to continue paying dividends or repurchasing shares at current levels, or at all.***

Our capital management priorities include returning excess capital to shareholders through dividends and share repurchases, subject to approval by our Board of Directors and to applicable legal and regulatory restrictions. Our ability to pay dividends and repurchase shares depends on the dividend capacity of our insurance subsidiaries, our liquidity and capital position, rating agency considerations, restrictions in debt and other agreements, and our Board's assessment of other uses of capital. Adverse experience, investment losses, increased capital requirements, or regulatory action could cause us to reduce or suspend dividends or repurchases. Any reduction or suspension could cause the price of our common stock to decline.

## Risks Related to Reinsurance

***We may not be able to recover amounts due from reinsurers, and reinsurance may become more expensive or less available.***

We use reinsurance to limit exposure to large individual risks and concentrations of risk, manage volatility, support product development, finance statutory reserve requirements, and free up capital. We cede amounts above our retention on individual life business, quota shares of certain business below our retention, certain group life and accidental death exposures, portions of our annuity business under coinsurance and modified coinsurance arrangements, and portions of our run-off long-term care block.

Reinsurance does not relieve us of our direct obligations to policyholders. If a reinsurer fails to pay amounts due, whether because of insolvency, financial weakness, dispute, or unwillingness to pay, we remain liable for the ceded obligations. Although we seek to manage reinsurer credit risk by dealing with highly rated reinsurers, diversifying across counterparties, monitoring their financial condition, and obtaining collateral where appropriate or required, collateral may be insufficient and a reinsurer's condition may deteriorate quickly. The reinsurance industry is concentrated, and a limited number of reinsurers provide a large portion of the industry's life reinsurance capacity, so the failure of a significant reinsurer could have broad effects.

Reinsurers have in the past sought, and may in the future seek, to increase rates on in-force yearly renewable term reinsurance where contracts permit, and such increases could reduce the profitability of the underlying business. We may become involved in disputes or arbitration with reinsurers regarding the interpretation of agreements or rate actions. The cost and availability of new reinsurance depend on market conditions, and if reinsurance becomes more expensive or unavailable, we may need to retain more risk, raise prices, reduce sales, or hold additional capital.

## Risks Related to Competition, Distribution, and Our Strategy

***We face intense competition, which could reduce our sales, margins, and market share.***

The life and health insurance and annuity industry is highly competitive. We compete with large diversified multi-line insurers, specialty life, group, and supplemental health carriers, mutual insurance companies, fraternal benefit societies, insurers owned by or affiliated with alternative asset managers and private equity sponsors, and, in some product lines, banks, asset managers, and other financial services firms. Some competitors are larger than we are, have greater financial resources, broader product lines, larger distribution networks, higher financial strength ratings, or lower costs of capital, or are not subject to the same regulatory requirements.

Competition is particularly intense in term life, where pricing is transparent and easily compared through quoting platforms; in large-case group benefits, where employers frequently market their programs; and in fixed and fixed indexed annuities, where insurers affiliated with alternative asset managers have increased competitive pressure on pricing. Those competitors may pursue investment strategies that generate higher yields, allowing them to offer higher crediting rates, caps, or participation rates than we can offer while meeting our return thresholds. In supplemental health, we compete with carriers that have large career sales forces or that bundle supplemental products with core benefits.

To compete, we may need to reduce prices, increase commissions, enhance product features, or accept lower margins. If we maintain our pricing discipline and decline to write business that does not meet our standards, our sales may decline in some periods. Consolidation in the industry could create larger competitors with greater scale and distribution reach. Any of these factors could reduce our sales, profitability, and market share.

***We depend on third-party distribution, and the loss of key relationships or adverse changes in distribution economics could reduce our sales.***

We distribute our products through independent employee benefits brokers and consultants, independent brokerage general agencies, independent marketing organizations, banks, broker-dealers, registered investment advisers, professional employer organizations, benefits administration technology platforms, and other partners. Most of these intermediaries are not exclusive to us and also sell competitors' products. We compete for their attention based on product competitiveness, underwriting speed, service, technology, compensation, and our financial strength ratings.

Although no single distribution organization accounted for a material portion of our consolidated revenues in 2023, our sales in particular product lines may depend significantly on a limited number of relationships. Distribution partners may terminate or reduce their relationships with us, change the products they emphasize, demand higher compensation, consolidate with other distributors, or be acquired by competitors or by firms affiliated with competitors. Ownership of independent marketing organizations and brokerage firms by insurers or private capital sponsors could reduce our access to those channels. Financial institutions subject us to rigorous product due diligence, and changes in their product approval processes or regulatory obligations could reduce the shelf space available to our products.

We are also exposed to the risk that intermediaries engage in inappropriate sales practices, misrepresent our products, or fail to comply with suitability, best interest, licensing, or other requirements. In some circumstances, we may be held responsible for their conduct.

***Our success in the worksite and supplemental health markets depends on our ability to recruit, train, and retain productive career agents, and changes in the classification of these agents could increase our costs.***

Our affiliated career sales force, consisting of independent contractor agents under contract with our agency subsidiaries, is central to our Supplemental Health segment and contributes to sales of voluntary life, individual life, and annuity products. Recruitment and retention of career agents directly affect sales in this channel. Recruiting and retaining productive agents is challenging, and agent turnover in the industry is historically high. Competition for agents from other carriers and from other occupations, changes in labor market conditions, and changes in compensation could reduce our ability to maintain and grow this sales force. Our investments in recruiting, training, licensing assistance, enrollment technology, and lead generation may not produce the expected results.

Our career agents are independent contractors and not employees. Federal and state authorities, courts, and private litigants have increasingly challenged the classification of workers as independent contractors in various industries. If our career agents, or the agents of our independent distribution partners, were reclassified as employees under federal or state law, we could be subject to claims for employment taxes, wages, overtime, benefits, and penalties, and we could be required to change our distribution model, which could significantly increase our costs and disrupt our sales.

***We may not successfully execute our strategy, including our investments in digital capabilities and the modernization of our policy administration systems.***

Our strategy depends on growing our middle-market and workplace franchises, maintaining pricing and underwriting discipline, investing in digital capabilities and operational efficiency, managing our in-force blocks and capital actively, and maintaining a conservative investment portfolio. We may not achieve our strategic objectives, and our assumptions about market opportunities may prove incorrect.

We are making significant investments in digital enrollment and self-service tools, electronic application and policy delivery, straight-through claims processing, automated underwriting decision engines, and modernization of our legacy policy administration systems. These initiatives are complex and may take longer, cost more, or deliver fewer benefits than we expect. System conversions involve risks of data migration errors, processing disruptions, and service failures that could harm customers, distribution partners, and employer clients. Our historical identity as an administrator means that operational excellence in enrollment, billing, and claims is central to our reputation and competitive differentiation, and failures in these areas could be particularly damaging. If our competitors deliver superior digital experiences, or if our initiatives fail, we could lose business and fail to achieve expected efficiencies.

***Acquisitions, dispositions, and other strategic transactions involve risks that could adversely affect our results.***

We have historically supplemented organic growth with acquisitions of blocks of business, distribution organizations, and benefits administration capabilities, and we may pursue similar transactions in the future. We may also dispose of businesses, enter into reinsurance transactions to transfer in-force blocks, or pursue other strategic actions. These transactions involve risks, including:

- difficulties in valuing acquired businesses, including uncertainty in the reserves, investments, and policyholder behavior of acquired blocks;
- liabilities, including legal, regulatory, and tax liabilities, that were not identified in due diligence or that exceed our indemnification protection;
- difficulties in integrating administrative platforms, personnel, controls, and cultures;
- failure to achieve anticipated synergies, cost savings, or growth;
- diversion of management attention from existing operations;
- the need for regulatory approvals, which may be delayed or conditioned;
- reputational harm and disruption of customer or distributor relationships; and
- the risk that dispositions result in continuing exposure through indemnities, reinsurance counterparty risk, or transition services.

Acquisitions may also result in goodwill and other intangible assets that are subject to impairment. If we fail to manage these risks, strategic transactions could reduce our earnings and capital rather than enhance them.

***Technological change and new market entrants could disrupt our business model.***

Technology-oriented entrants, including digital distributors and insurance technology firms, are seeking to change how insurance is distributed, underwritten, and serviced. Advances in data analytics, artificial intelligence, digital distribution, and wearable and health monitoring technologies could change consumer expectations, alter the economics of distribution, and enable competitors to price risk more precisely or acquire customers more cheaply. Benefits administration technology platforms are increasingly central to how employers select and enroll benefits, and these platforms may favor carriers with deeper integration or more favorable economic arrangements. Large technology companies or other firms with significant customer relationships could enter our markets. Advances in medical diagnostics, including genetic testing, could increase anti-selection if consumers have access to information that we cannot use in underwriting. If we fail to adapt to these changes, our competitive position could deteriorate.

***Damage to our reputation could harm our business.***

Our reputation with customers, employer clients, distribution partners, regulators, rating agencies, investors, and employees is important to our success. Our reputation could be harmed by many factors, including claims handling practices, sales practices, regulatory actions, litigation, cybersecurity incidents, service failures, actions of our employees, agents, or vendors, rate increases on in-force business such as our run-off long-term care block, cost of insurance increases on universal life policies, and public perception of the insurance industry generally. Negative publicity can spread quickly through social media and other channels, regardless of its accuracy.

Our corporate name reflects our origins as a benefits administration and processing business. While we believe the name carries recognition among employer clients, distribution partners, and employees, it may not clearly communicate to consumers, investors, or other audiences that we operate solely as a life and health insurance holding company, which could limit our brand recognition in individual and direct-to-consumer markets or require additional marketing investment. Damage to our reputation or brand could reduce sales, increase lapses, impair distribution relationships, attract regulatory scrutiny, and make it harder to attract and retain talent.

## Operational Risks

***Cyberattacks, data breaches, and other information security incidents could disrupt our operations, expose sensitive information, and result in significant liability and reputational harm.***

Our business depends on the secure processing, storage, and transmission of large amounts of confidential and personal information, including health, financial, and other sensitive data about policyholders, insured employees, beneficiaries, claimants, and employer clients. Our dental and vision businesses and certain business we administer involve protected health information subject to federal health privacy and security requirements. We, our distribution partners, and our vendors are targets of cyberattacks, including ransomware, phishing, social engineering, credential theft, denial-of-service attacks, and attacks by sophisticated criminal and state-sponsored actors. The techniques used in such attacks change frequently, increasingly use artificial intelligence, and may not be detected until after significant harm has occurred.

Despite our information security program, which includes risk assessments, security controls and monitoring, vulnerability management, third-party risk management, employee training, incident response planning, and business continuity and disaster recovery testing, we cannot eliminate the risk of a successful attack. Remote and hybrid work arrangements, our reliance on third-party systems and cloud service providers, our ongoing systems modernization, and the integration of digital tools for customers and distributors increase the number of potential points of vulnerability.

A significant cybersecurity incident could:

- disrupt our ability to process premiums, pay claims, issue policies, and service customers;
- result in the theft, loss, corruption, or unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information;
- enable fraudulent transactions, including fraudulent surrenders, withdrawals, or claims payments;
- require costly notifications, remediation, credit monitoring, and other responses;
- expose us to litigation, including class actions, and to regulatory investigations, fines, and sanctions under federal and state law, including the NAIC model-based insurance data security laws and the cybersecurity regulation of the New York State Department of Financial Services; and
- damage our reputation and our relationships with customers, employer clients, and distribution partners.

Our cyber insurance may not cover all losses, and coverage may become more expensive or less available. For additional information, see Item 1C, "Cybersecurity."

***Failures or disruptions of our information technology systems, or of our business continuity plans, could interrupt our operations.***

Information technology is critical to our operations, including policy administration, billing and premium reconciliation, claims, investment management, hedging, actuarial modeling, financial reporting, and communications with customers and distribution partners. We administer most of our business on internal platforms, including legacy systems acquired in prior transactions, supplemented by third-party systems. Legacy systems may be difficult to maintain, may rely on outdated technology or scarce technical skills, and may be more vulnerable to failure or attack.

Our systems could fail or be disrupted because of hardware or software failures, programming errors, power or telecommunications outages, natural disasters, severe weather, pandemics, terrorism, cyberattacks, or errors during system upgrades and conversions. Our administrative operations are centered in Georgia, and an event affecting our facilities or personnel in that region, such as severe weather, a regional power or telecommunications failure, or a localized public health emergency, could disrupt a substantial portion of our operations. Our business continuity and disaster recovery plans may not be effective in all circumstances, and recovery may take longer than expected. Significant disruptions could prevent us from serving customers, processing transactions, meeting regulatory and financial reporting deadlines, or managing our investments and hedges, and could result in financial loss, regulatory action, and reputational harm.

***We depend on third-party service providers, and their failures could adversely affect our operations.***

We rely on third parties for many functions, including technology infrastructure and cloud services, certain policy administration and recordkeeping, dental and vision provider network access, data used in underwriting and claims, print and mail services, payment processing, external investment management for specialized asset classes, and actuarial software. Our ability to oversee these providers is limited, and they may experience operational failures, cybersecurity incidents, financial difficulties, regulatory problems, or disruptions in their own supply chains.

The failure of a significant vendor to perform, the loss of a vendor relationship, or a security incident at a vendor or a vendor's subcontractor could disrupt our operations, expose sensitive information, result in regulatory violations for which we remain responsible, and increase our costs. Replacing a vendor may be difficult, time-consuming, and expensive, particularly for specialized services or where there are few alternative providers. Regulators increasingly expect insurers to oversee third-party risk rigorously, and deficiencies in our oversight could result in regulatory criticism or sanctions.

***Our models, including predictive models and artificial intelligence tools, may be flawed, and their use is subject to evolving regulation.***

We use complex models throughout our business, including in pricing, underwriting, reserving, capital planning, asset-liability management, hedging, investment valuation, claims triage, fraud detection, and financial reporting. Models are simplified representations of reality and depend on assumptions, data, and methodologies that may prove incorrect. Models may contain errors, be applied incorrectly, or fail to anticipate changes in experience or market conditions. Reliance on flawed models could lead to inadequate pricing, inaccurate reserves, ineffective hedges, poor investment decisions, or misstatements in our financial reports.

Our use of predictive models, external consumer data, algorithms, and artificial intelligence in underwriting, pricing, marketing, and claims is subject to a growing body of state laws, regulations, and regulatory guidance, including NAIC model bulletins adopted by many states and specific state statutes and regulations. These rules require insurers to maintain governance and risk management frameworks for these tools and to guard against unfair discrimination. Compliance may require us to limit the data and techniques we use, to conduct testing for disparate outcomes, and to document our models extensively. If regulators determine that our models or data sources produce unfairly discriminatory results or are not adequately governed, we could face sanctions, restrictions on our practices, litigation, and reputational harm, and we may need to change underwriting or pricing approaches in ways that reduce their effectiveness.

***Fraud, errors, and misconduct by employees, agents, claimants, or others could result in losses and reputational harm.***

We are exposed to the risk of fraud and misconduct by employees, career agents, independent agents, brokers, vendors, applicants, policyholders, claimants, and others. Examples include application misrepresentations, fraudulent claims, identity theft and account takeover, misappropriation of premiums or policyholder funds, improper sales practices, unauthorized transactions, and violations of anti-money laundering and sanctions laws. Our controls, including our special investigations unit and data analytics, may not prevent or detect all such conduct. Employee errors in processing transactions, administering benefits, or applying policy terms could also result in losses. Fraud, errors, and misconduct could cause financial losses, regulatory sanctions, litigation, and damage to our reputation.

***Our success depends on our ability to attract and retain qualified personnel.***

We depend on the skills and experience of our senior management and of employees in specialized disciplines, including actuarial science, underwriting, claims, investment management, risk management, information technology, cybersecurity, data science, and compliance. Competition for talent in these areas is intense, including from employers outside the insurance industry, and the availability of flexible and remote work arrangements has expanded the pool of employers competing for our employees. The loss of key personnel, or our inability to attract and develop qualified successors, could impair our ability to execute our strategy, manage our risks, and operate effectively. Rising compensation costs to attract and retain talent could also increase our expenses.

***Our risk management policies and procedures may not be effective.***

Our enterprise risk management framework is designed to identify, measure, monitor, and manage the risks to which we are exposed, and our risk appetite framework sets qualitative and quantitative tolerances for key risks. However, our risk management methods may not anticipate every economic, financial, insurance, or operational outcome, and they rely in part on historical data, models, and judgments that may not be predictive of future events. Some risks are difficult to quantify or are interrelated in ways that may not be apparent until they materialize. Our stress testing and scenario analysis may not capture the severity or combination of events that actually occur. If our risk management framework fails to identify or adequately manage significant risks, we could suffer unexpected losses.

## Legal, Regulatory, and Tax Risks

***We are subject to extensive regulation, and changes in regulation or its interpretation could adversely affect our business.***

Our insurance operations are subject to extensive regulation and supervision in each jurisdiction where we do business, primarily for the protection of policyholders rather than shareholders. State insurance regulators have broad authority over licensing, policy forms and rates, reserves, capital, investments, affiliate transactions, dividends, market conduct, claims handling, privacy, and financial reporting. Ironwood Life and Ironwood Annuity are regulated principally by the Georgia Office of Insurance and Safety Fire Commissioner, Ironwood New York by the New York State Department of Financial Services, and Ironwood Re by the Vermont Department of Financial Regulation. We are also subject to federal and state laws governing securities, broker-dealers, investment advisers, employee benefits, privacy and data security, consumer protection, telemarketing, anti-money laundering, sanctions, and taxation.

Laws and regulations change frequently, and regulators have significant discretion in interpreting and enforcing them. Changes could require us to change our products, pricing, distribution, investments, reinsurance, or operations, increase our compliance costs, limit our ability to raise rates, or increase our capital requirements. Our insurance subsidiaries are subject to periodic financial and market conduct examinations, and regulators may conduct targeted reviews of specific practices or products. Examinations and reviews could result in findings that require corrective action, restitution, fines, or other sanctions. Although our insurance business is regulated primarily by the states, federal legislation and administrative action, including actions affecting the Federal Home Loan Bank system, financial stability oversight, and federal health and employee benefit laws, can also significantly affect our business. International regulatory developments may influence U.S. regulation even though we operate only in the United States.

***Standards of conduct for the sale of annuities and life insurance, including potential expansion of fiduciary obligations, could adversely affect our sales and increase our costs.***

Most states have adopted revisions to the NAIC's model regulation on suitability in annuity transactions that impose a best interest standard of conduct on producers and insurers recommending annuities, and New York's best interest regulation applies to sales of both life insurance and annuities. The SEC's Regulation Best Interest applies to recommendations by broker-dealers, including Ironwood Financial Securities, LLC, to retail customers. These standards require us to maintain supervisory systems and controls across our distribution channels and expose us to the risk of regulatory action and litigation if we or our distributors fail to comply.

The DOL proposed a new rule in 2023 that would expand the circumstances under which persons who provide investment recommendations to retirement plans, plan participants, and individual retirement account owners are treated as fiduciaries under ERISA and the Internal Revenue Code. If adopted in its proposed form or a similar form, such a rule could affect the sale of annuities, including rollovers from employer plans into individual annuity contracts, by imposing fiduciary obligations and conditions on compensation arrangements. Such requirements could cause distributors, particularly independent agents and marketing organizations that sell fixed indexed annuities, to change or reduce their sales of our products, could require us to change our compensation practices and product designs, and could increase our compliance costs and litigation exposure. The outcome of the rulemaking and any related legal challenges is uncertain.

***Changes in federal and state rules governing supplemental health and other excepted benefit products could adversely affect our Supplemental Health segment.***

Our supplemental health products are generally treated as excepted benefits under federal law and are therefore excepted from many of the requirements applicable to comprehensive major medical coverage. Excepted benefit status depends on meeting specific requirements about product design, independence from group health plans, and, in some cases, required notices to consumers. Federal agencies have proposed and adopted rules from time to time affecting the classification and marketing of fixed indemnity and other excepted benefit products, including rules on notice requirements and coordination with group health plans.

Changes in these rules or their interpretation could require us to redesign products, change benefit structures, revise enrollment materials and processes, or limit the ways we market products through the workplace. Such changes could reduce demand for our products, increase our administrative costs, or cause certain products to lose excepted benefit status, which would subject them to substantially more burdensome requirements. State laws governing accident and health insurance, including minimum loss ratio requirements and rate and form filing requirements, could also change in ways that reduce our flexibility. Our Medicare supplement and pediatric dental products are subject to additional federal and state requirements that may change.

***Privacy, data protection, and consumer protection laws could increase our costs and expose us to liability.***

We are subject to numerous federal and state laws governing the privacy and security of personal information, including the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and related regulations, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, state insurance data security laws based on the NAIC model law, the cybersecurity regulation of the New York State Department of Financial Services (which has recently been amended to strengthen its requirements), state comprehensive consumer privacy laws to the extent applicable, and state data breach notification laws. We are also subject to laws governing telemarketing, electronic communications, and the collection and use of consumer data in marketing.

These laws are complex, are evolving rapidly, and may be inconsistent across jurisdictions. Compliance requires significant investment in systems, processes, and personnel. New laws could restrict our ability to collect and use data in underwriting, marketing, and claims, increase our obligations to consumers, or expand private rights of action. Our direct-to-consumer and telephone sales activities expose us to risks under telemarketing and electronic communications laws, which have been the subject of significant class action litigation in the financial services industry. Violations could result in fines, penalties, litigation, and reputational harm.

***We are subject to litigation and regulatory proceedings that could result in significant liabilities and reputational harm.***

We are, and in the future may be, involved in litigation, arbitration, regulatory examinations, investigations, and inquiries arising in the ordinary course of business. Insurers have historically been subject to substantial litigation, including class actions, relating to:

- cost of insurance charge increases and the administration of universal life policies;
- sales practices, product suitability, and policy illustrations, including for indexed universal life and fixed indexed annuities;
- disability claim denials and terminations, which are frequently litigated under ERISA;
- premium rate increases and benefit reduction options in long-term care insurance;
- the payment of death benefits, the identification of deceased insureds, and unclaimed property practices;
- the classification of agents as independent contractors;
- privacy, data security, and telemarketing practices;
- the use of external data and algorithms in underwriting and pricing; and
- ERISA fiduciary obligations in connection with group benefits and retirement products.

Plaintiffs may seek large or indeterminate damages, including punitive or extra-contractual damages, and juries may award substantial amounts. Regulatory proceedings could result in fines, restitution, changes to our business practices, and restrictions on our licenses. The outcome of legal and regulatory matters is inherently uncertain, and we may incur significant costs defending them regardless of their merits. Changes in legal theories, judicial interpretations, or regulatory priorities could expose us to new claims relating to past practices. An adverse outcome in one or more matters could materially affect our results of operations, financial condition, and reputation.

***Unclaimed property laws and related audits could result in additional payments and penalties.***

State laws and regulations govern our handling of unclaimed property and our procedures for identifying deceased insureds and locating beneficiaries, including the use of death master file data. States periodically audit insurers' compliance with these laws, and audits may cover extended look-back periods. Changes in the interpretation of these laws, or in regulators' expectations regarding the frequency and methods of death master file comparisons, could require us to pay additional benefits, interest, and penalties, to escheat additional amounts to states, and to change our administrative practices, which could increase our costs and reduce our earnings. Policies administered on legacy systems acquired in prior transactions may present heightened risk because of incomplete or inconsistent records.

***Guaranty association assessments could adversely affect our results.***

Our insurance subsidiaries are required to be members of life and health insurance guaranty associations in every state, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. These associations protect policyholders, within statutory limits, when an insurer becomes insolvent, and they fund their obligations through assessments on member insurers, generally based on each member's share of premiums written in the relevant lines in that state. The amount and timing of future assessments cannot be predicted and depend on future insurer insolvencies. The insolvency of a large insurer, particularly one with significant long-term care or annuity exposures, could result in substantial assessments. Although many states allow insurers to offset assessments against premium tax liabilities over time, those offsets may be reduced or eliminated by legislative changes, and assessments could materially reduce our earnings in the periods in which they are recognized.

***Changes in tax laws could reduce demand for our products and increase our tax burden.***

Many of our products benefit from favorable tax treatment under current federal law, including the tax-deferred accumulation of inside buildup in life insurance and annuity contracts and the exclusion of life insurance death benefits from beneficiaries' gross income. Congress has periodically considered proposals that would reduce or eliminate these benefits or that would provide more favorable tax treatment to competing savings and investment products. Changes that reduce the relative tax advantages of our products could reduce demand for them and increase lapses and surrenders.

Our insurance subsidiaries are subject to federal income tax under provisions of the Internal Revenue Code applicable to life insurance companies, including provisions governing tax reserves, the treatment of policy acquisition expenses, and the tax treatment of reinsurance. Changes in these provisions, in corporate tax rates, or in the interpretation of tax laws by the Internal Revenue Service and courts could increase our tax liabilities. We are also subject to the corporate alternative minimum tax enacted under the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, if and to the extent its thresholds are met, and guidance under that regime continues to develop. Changes in state premium taxes, retaliatory taxes, and other state and local taxes could also increase our costs. Products must meet specific statutory requirements to qualify for favorable tax treatment, and failure to meet them could expose us to liability to policyholders and to remediation costs.

***Climate change, and regulatory and stakeholder responses to it, could adversely affect our business.***

Climate change could affect our business in several ways. Physical risks, including more frequent or severe hurricanes, floods, heat events, wildfires, and other weather events, could increase mortality and morbidity among our insured populations, disrupt our operations and those of our vendors and distribution partners, and damage properties securing our commercial mortgage loans and real estate investments. Changes in climate patterns could also affect the spread of infectious diseases and the health of the populations we insure over long periods.

Transition risks arising from changes in policy, technology, and market preferences could reduce the value of investments in carbon-intensive industries or in issuers that fail to adapt, increase credit risk in affected sectors, and affect the value of commercial real estate. Regulators, including the NAIC, state insurance departments, and the SEC, have increased their focus on climate-related risks, and many states, including New York, now require insurers to complete annual climate risk disclosure surveys. The New York State Department of Financial Services has issued guidance on managing the financial risks of climate change that applies to Ironwood New York. Additional climate-related disclosure and risk management requirements could increase our compliance costs.

We also face divergent expectations from stakeholders regarding environmental, social, and governance matters, including investment practices. Some stakeholders and jurisdictions favor greater consideration of these factors, while others have adopted or proposed restrictions on their consideration. Navigating these divergent expectations could expose us to reputational, legal, and regulatory risk regardless of the approach we take.

## Risks Related to Our Common Stock and Corporate Structure

***Insurance laws, Georgia corporate law, and provisions of our governing documents could delay, deter, or prevent a change of control.***

State insurance holding company laws generally require any person seeking to acquire control of a domestic insurer, directly or indirectly, to obtain the prior approval of the insurer's domiciliary regulator. Because our insurance subsidiaries are domiciled in Georgia, New York, and Vermont, an acquirer of control of Ironwood Logistics Group, Inc. would need approval from the regulators in each of those states. Control is generally presumed to exist when a person owns, controls, or holds with power to vote a specified percentage of the voting securities of the insurer or its parent, although that presumption may be rebutted. Some states also require pre-acquisition notification of changes of control that may affect competition. These requirements could delay, deter, or prevent transactions that some or all of our shareholders consider desirable, including transactions in which shareholders might receive a premium for their shares, and could discourage investors from acquiring significant positions in our common stock.

In addition, provisions of Georgia corporate law and of our articles of incorporation and bylaws could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us or to change our Board of Directors or management. These provisions could limit the price that investors are willing to pay for our common stock.

***The market price of our common stock may be volatile.***

The market price of our common stock could fluctuate significantly in response to many factors, including:

- variations in our quarterly and annual results, including volatility arising from the accounting for long-duration contracts and market risk benefits;
- changes in interest rates, credit spreads, and equity markets;
- the results of our annual actuarial assumption reviews and any resulting reserve changes;
- changes in our financial strength or credit ratings;
- changes in analysts' estimates or recommendations;
- announcements by us or our competitors of significant transactions, product changes, or strategic initiatives;
- regulatory, legislative, or tax developments affecting the insurance industry;
- changes in our dividend or share repurchase practices;
- litigation and regulatory proceedings; and
- general market and economic conditions, including conditions unrelated to our performance.

Stock price volatility could also make it more difficult for us to raise equity capital and could result in securities class action litigation, which could be costly and divert management's attention.

## General Risk Factors

***Geopolitical events, terrorism, and other macroeconomic shocks could adversely affect our business.***

Although we operate only in the United States, our business is affected by global events. Armed conflicts, geopolitical tensions, terrorism, trade disputes, sanctions, political instability, and governmental dysfunction, including disputes over the federal debt limit or government shutdowns, could disrupt financial markets, increase volatility, widen credit spreads, reduce the value of our investments, affect interest rates and inflation, and harm economic activity among our customers and employer clients. Such events could also increase the risk of cyberattacks, including by state-sponsored actors, and affect the operations of our vendors and counterparties. Compliance with economic sanctions programs that change rapidly in response to geopolitical events could increase our compliance costs and risks. The effects of these events are difficult to predict and could materially and adversely affect our results of operations and financial condition.

***Changes in public perception of the insurance industry, or in societal trends, could reduce demand for our products.***

Demand for our products depends on consumers' and employers' perceptions of the value of life insurance, supplemental health coverage, disability coverage, and annuities, and on their trust in insurers. Negative publicity about the industry, including in relation to claims handling, rate increases on long-term care and universal life products, sales practices for complex products, or the use of data and algorithms in underwriting, could reduce demand and increase regulatory scrutiny. Changes in household formation, workforce participation, employer benefit strategies, the prevalence of independent and gig work outside traditional employer relationships, and the expansion of government-provided benefits such as public paid leave programs could also reduce the need for, or change the form of, the products we offer. If we fail to anticipate and respond to these trends, our sales and profitability could decline.

***Our insurance and other coverage may not be adequate to protect us against all losses.***

We maintain insurance coverage, including directors' and officers' liability, errors and omissions, fidelity, cyber, and property coverage, to protect against certain risks. This coverage is subject to deductibles, limits, and exclusions, and it may not cover all losses we incur or all claims made against us. Insurers may dispute coverage, and the cost of coverage may increase or its availability may decline, particularly for cyber and professional liability risks. Losses that exceed or fall outside our coverage could adversely affect our results of operations and financial condition.
