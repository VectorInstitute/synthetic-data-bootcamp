PART I

Item 1. Business

OVERVIEW

Lonnov Software, Inc. ("Lonnov," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") is a global provider of enterprise software, cloud infrastructure and technology services. Organizations across industries use our offerings to modernize their information technology environments, run mission-critical workloads, protect their data and manage complex digital operations at scale. We were incorporated in the State of Delaware, and our corporate headquarters are located in Austin, Texas. Our common stock trades under the ticker symbol "LNON."

Our offerings span the technology lifecycle of our customers. We help them plan and design their technology architectures, deploy and integrate software and cloud platforms, migrate legacy systems, and operate and secure their environments on an ongoing basis. We combine proprietary software platforms with a large, globally distributed services organization. This allows us to serve customers who want packaged software, customers who want fully managed outcomes, and customers who want a combination of the two.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024 ("fiscal 2024"), our total revenue was $80,053 million, an increase of 3.9% compared with the fiscal year ended December 31, 2023 ("fiscal 2023"). We believe the breadth of our portfolio, the recurring nature of a substantial portion of our revenue, and the long-standing relationships we maintain with large enterprise and public sector customers provide a durable foundation for the business.

Enterprise technology environments have grown more complex. Organizations operate hybrid estates that combine on-premises data centers, multiple public cloud environments and a growing number of software-as-a-service applications. At the same time, they face higher expectations for cybersecurity and data protection, scarce specialized technical talent, and pressure to adopt artificial intelligence and advanced analytics in a responsible and cost-effective manner. We believe these trends increase demand for partners that can provide integrated platforms together with the engineering and operational capacity to implement and run them. Our strategy is to be that partner for the largest and most demanding organizations in the world.

REPORTABLE SEGMENTS

As of December 31, 2024, we managed our business through three operating and reportable segments, which are organized around the nature of the offerings we deliver and the way our chief operating decision maker evaluates performance and allocates resources:

  • Cloud and Data Platforms ("CDP"), which includes our hybrid cloud infrastructure software, data management and analytics platforms, and artificial intelligence development and deployment tools.
  • Enterprise Applications and Security ("EAS"), which includes our business applications, workflow automation software, identity and access management products, and threat detection and response solutions.
  • Managed and Professional Services ("MPS"), which includes consulting, systems integration, application development and maintenance, cloud migration, and outsourced infrastructure and application management services.

The CDP segment focuses on the foundational software layers on which customers build and run their workloads. Its offerings are typically sold as subscriptions or consumption-based arrangements and are designed to operate consistently across customer data centers and third-party public cloud environments.

The EAS segment focuses on software that supports specific business processes and on protecting the applications, users and data that underpin those processes. Its offerings are sold primarily as multi-year subscriptions, both as cloud-delivered services and as software deployed in customer environments.

The MPS segment provides the people, processes and operational tooling that customers rely on to design, implement and run technology environments. Engagements range from discrete advisory and implementation projects to multi-year managed services agreements under which we assume operational responsibility for significant portions of a customer's technology estate. The MPS segment employs the majority of our workforce and accounts for a substantial portion of our cost of revenue, reflecting the labor-intensive nature of services delivery.

Our segments work closely together. A significant portion of our largest customer relationships involves offerings from more than one segment, and we design our go-to-market approach to encourage customers to adopt integrated solutions. Additional information about our segments is included in Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," and in the notes to the consolidated financial statements in Item 8, "Financial Statements and Supplementary Data."

PRINCIPAL PRODUCTS AND SERVICES

Cloud and Data Platforms

Our hybrid cloud platform gives customers a common control plane for provisioning, orchestrating and governing compute, storage and networking resources across private and public cloud environments. The platform includes container orchestration, virtualization management, policy-based automation and cost governance capabilities. Customers use it to standardize operations, reduce dependence on any single infrastructure provider, and move workloads between environments as business needs change.

Our data management portfolio includes data integration, data cataloging, master data management and data quality tools, along with a cloud-native analytics engine that supports structured and unstructured data. These products help customers consolidate fragmented data sources, enforce governance and lineage requirements, and make trusted data available to analysts, applications and machine learning models.

Our artificial intelligence tools allow customers to build, fine-tune, evaluate and deploy machine learning and generative models on their own data, inside environments they control. The tools include model lifecycle management, monitoring for model drift and performance, and guardrail capabilities intended to support responsible use. We have embedded these capabilities into many products across our portfolio, and we continue to expand them in response to customer demand.

Enterprise Applications and Security

Our enterprise applications automate and digitize core operational processes, including IT service management, field service management, procurement workflows, and human resources service delivery. The applications are built on a common low-code platform, so customers can extend standard functionality and build custom workflows without extensive software development.

Our security portfolio includes identity governance, privileged access management, multi-factor authentication, endpoint and cloud workload protection, and a security analytics platform that correlates telemetry across customer environments to detect and prioritize threats. We also offer managed detection and response services in which our security operations personnel monitor customer environments and respond to incidents around the clock. These services are delivered in collaboration with our MPS segment.

Managed and Professional Services

Our consulting and systems integration practices advise customers on technology strategy, enterprise architecture, and operating model design. They also implement our own software as well as third-party platforms. Our application services teams develop, modernize, test and maintain custom and packaged applications. Our cloud migration services help customers assess application portfolios, plan migration waves, and move workloads to the target environments.

Our managed services offerings include infrastructure management, application management, end-user computing support, network operations, and service desk operations. These engagements are generally governed by multi-year contracts with defined service levels. We deliver them through a combination of onshore, nearshore and offshore delivery centers, supported by our proprietary automation and operational analytics platforms. We increasingly use artificial intelligence to automate routine operational tasks, predict and prevent incidents, and improve the productivity of our delivery personnel.

Revenue Models

We generate revenue through software subscriptions, consumption-based cloud services, perpetual software licenses with associated support, time-and-materials and fixed-price professional services engagements, and multi-year managed services arrangements. A substantial portion of our revenue is recurring in nature, which we believe gives us good visibility into future results. Our revenue recognition policies are described in the notes to the consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

STRATEGY

Our strategy is designed to deepen our role as a long-term technology partner to the world's largest organizations. Its principal elements are as follows.

Integrated Solutions. We intend to keep integrating our platforms with our services capabilities so that customers can buy outcomes rather than individual components. We believe customers increasingly want fewer, more strategic vendors that can take end-to-end accountability, and that our combination of proprietary software and delivery scale sets us apart.

Innovation in Artificial Intelligence and Automation. We are investing in artificial intelligence across our portfolio, both as product capabilities we offer customers and as tools that make our own services delivery more efficient. In fiscal 2024, research and development expense represented 13.8% of revenue, compared with 13.7% in fiscal 2023. This reflects our continued commitment to product innovation.

Hybrid and Multi-Cloud Neutrality. We design our platforms to run consistently across customer data centers and multiple public cloud providers. We believe this neutrality is valuable to customers seeking to avoid concentration risk, meet data residency requirements, and optimize costs.

Operational Excellence. We continuously work to improve delivery productivity, the utilization of our workforce, and the efficiency of our operations. Our efforts include standardizing delivery methodologies, expanding automation, rationalizing facilities, and optimizing the geographic mix of our delivery resources.

Disciplined Capital Allocation. We aim to balance investment in organic growth with selective acquisitions that add technology, talent or market access, while returning capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases.

CUSTOMERS

We serve a broad and diversified customer base that includes large multinational enterprises, mid-sized organizations, and government agencies at the national, state and local levels. Our customers operate in a wide range of industries, including financial services, insurance, healthcare and life sciences, manufacturing, retail and consumer goods, telecommunications, media, energy and utilities, transportation, and the public sector.

Our largest customers typically purchase offerings from more than one of our segments and are often party to long-term master agreements under which they procure software and services over multiple years. We focus on building enduring relationships and expanding them over time through additional products, services and geographies. Many of our largest relationships have lasted well over a decade.

No single customer accounted for a material portion of our total revenue in fiscal 2024. However, a meaningful portion of our revenue comes from a relatively small number of large enterprise and government customers, and the loss of, or a significant reduction in business from, one or more of these customers could adversely affect our results. Contracts with government customers are subject to particular risks, including termination for convenience, budget appropriation constraints and audit rights. SALES AND MARKETING

We sell our offerings primarily through a direct sales force organized by geography, industry and customer size. For our largest customers, we assign dedicated account teams that coordinate across all three segments and are supported by solution specialists, technical architects and delivery leaders. Industry-focused teams develop solutions and go-to-market programs tailored to the regulatory and operational requirements of particular sectors.

We complement our direct sales efforts with a network of indirect channels, including resellers, value-added distributors, systems integrators, managed service providers and public cloud marketplaces. Our software is also offered through the marketplaces of major public cloud providers, which enables customers to procure our products using committed cloud spending and simplifies purchasing. For smaller customers, we use inside sales teams and digital self-service channels.

Our marketing activities include customer conferences, industry events, digital marketing campaigns, thought leadership publications, analyst relations, and partner co-marketing programs. We also run customer success programs aimed at driving adoption of the products customers have purchased. We believe stronger adoption supports renewals and creates opportunities to expand existing relationships.

Our professional and managed services engagements are typically won through competitive proposal processes. These processes can be lengthy and often involve detailed solution design, pricing and contractual negotiation. Sales cycles for our largest software and managed services transactions can extend over many months and frequently require approval from senior executives within the customer organization.

COMPETITION

The markets in which we operate are highly competitive, fragmented in certain areas, and subject to rapid technological change, evolving customer requirements, and frequent new product introductions. We compete with a wide variety of companies, many of which compete with us in only some of our markets, and some of which are also our partners, suppliers or customers.

In our CDP segment, our competitors include large public cloud infrastructure providers that offer native platform and data services, established enterprise infrastructure software vendors, specialized data management and analytics companies, and a growing number of emerging companies focused on artificial intelligence tooling. Open-source projects and offerings built on them also compete with certain of our products.

In our EAS segment, our competitors include large diversified enterprise software vendors, cloud-native application platform companies, specialized cybersecurity vendors, and providers of managed security services.

In our MPS segment, we compete with global information technology services and consulting firms, offshore-based services providers, the services arms of hardware and software vendors, regional and boutique consulting firms, and the internal information technology departments of our customers.

We believe the principal competitive factors in our markets include:

  • the breadth, depth and integration of product and service offerings;
  • product functionality, performance, reliability, scalability and security;
  • the ability to operate across hybrid and multi-cloud environments;
  • quality, reliability and speed of services delivery;
  • industry and domain expertise;
  • the availability of skilled technical personnel;
  • global delivery scale and geographic reach;
  • pricing, total cost of ownership and contractual flexibility;
  • brand recognition, reputation and the strength of customer relationships; and
  • the pace of innovation, including in artificial intelligence and automation.

We believe we compete favorably on these factors, particularly for complex, large-scale engagements that call for both proprietary platforms and significant delivery capacity. Some of our competitors, however, have greater financial, technical or marketing resources in particular markets, more extensive offerings in specific product categories, or lower cost structures. Pricing pressure, particularly in commoditized portions of the services market, remains a persistent feature of our competitive environment.

SEASONALITY

Our business is subject to seasonal fluctuations. Software bookings and revenue from perpetual license and certain consumption-based arrangements have historically been stronger in the fourth quarter of our fiscal year. This reflects customer budgeting and procurement cycles and the timing of our sales incentive programs. Revenue from professional services can be lower in periods with significant holidays and vacation schedules in key geographies, which reduce billable working days. Government customer purchasing patterns are influenced by their fiscal year-end timing and budget appropriations. Because a substantial portion of our revenue is recurring and recognized ratably over contract terms, seasonal variations in bookings generally have a more muted effect on reported revenue. Quarterly results nonetheless should not be relied on as indicators of future performance.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

The markets in which we compete are intensely competitive, and failure to compete effectively could reduce our revenue, margins and market share.

Each of our segments competes in a crowded and rapidly changing market. In Cloud and Data Platforms, we compete with large public cloud providers that bundle infrastructure, data and artificial intelligence services, with independent software vendors, and with open source projects that customers can adopt at little or no licensing cost. In Enterprise Applications and Security, we compete with large application vendors, specialized software-as-a-service providers and a growing number of well-funded security companies. In Managed and Professional Services, we compete with global consulting and outsourcing firms, offshore-based service providers, regional specialists and the internal technology departments of our customers. Many competitors have greater resources in particular markets, more established brands in specific product categories, or lower cost delivery models.

Competitors may introduce products or services that are superior to ours, price their offerings more aggressively, bundle software with infrastructure or services in ways we cannot match, or consolidate to form larger and more capable rivals. Large customers frequently use competitive bidding when contracts come up for renewal, which can force us to accept lower prices or less favorable terms to retain business. If we cannot differentiate our offerings, maintain our pricing, or respond quickly to competitive developments, our revenue and operating margins could decline.

Our revenue growth has been moderate, and we may not be able to sustain or accelerate it.

Our revenue grew at a modest rate in fiscal 2024, reflecting the scale of our existing business, the mature character of certain services and software offerings, and the time required for newer cloud, data and artificial intelligence offerings to contribute meaningfully. Growth in our higher-margin software businesses may not be large enough to offset slower growth, or declines, in legacy services, perpetual license and support revenue. Customers may also delay or reduce discretionary projects, consolidate vendors, or bring work in-house.

Because our revenue base is large, even significant new business may not produce a meaningful increase in our overall growth rate. If we cannot grow revenue, we may be unable to keep expanding operating income through productivity and cost discipline alone, and investors may view our prospects less favorably. That view could adversely affect the trading price of our common stock.

Advances in artificial intelligence could disrupt demand for our services, compress pricing and require substantial investment with uncertain returns.

Artificial intelligence, including generative models and autonomous software agents, is rapidly changing how software is built and how technology environments are operated. Much of our Managed and Professional Services revenue comes from labor-based activities, such as application development and maintenance, testing, service desk operations and infrastructure management, which customers increasingly expect to be automated. Customers may demand that productivity gains from automation be passed through to them as lower prices. They may also shift from time-and-materials arrangements to outcome-based pricing, or perform more work themselves with artificial intelligence tools offered by third parties. Any of these developments could reduce our services revenue faster than we can replace it.

At the same time, we are investing significantly in artificial intelligence capabilities across our platforms and delivery operations. These investments may not produce products that achieve market acceptance. They may become obsolete as foundation models and competing tools evolve, or they may cost more than expected to develop and operate, including the cost of the computing capacity required to train and run models. If we fail to anticipate the direction of the technology, or if competitors with greater access to models, data or computing resources outpace us, our competitive position and results of operations could be harmed.

Our business depends on renewing and expanding relationships with large customers, and the loss or reduction of significant relationships could harm our results.

A substantial portion of our revenue comes from large enterprise and public sector customers under multi-year master agreements, subscriptions and managed services contracts. These customers generally have no obligation to renew when their contracts expire. Many contracts allow customers to reduce scope or terminate for convenience on relatively short notice, sometimes subject to termination fees that may not fully compensate us. Renewals frequently involve competitive procurements, price concessions and changes in scope.

Our largest relationships often span more than one segment. As a result, dissatisfaction in one area, such as a security incident, a missed service level or a failed implementation, could affect our revenue across several segments at once. Mergers, divestitures, financial distress or changes in leadership at a large customer could also lead to reduced spending or the loss of the relationship. The loss of, or a significant reduction in business from, one or more of our largest customers could adversely affect our revenue, profitability and cash flows.

Fixed-price, outcome-based and managed services contracts expose us to cost overruns, penalties and losses.

Many of our professional services engagements and most of our managed services arrangements are priced on a fixed-fee, unit-price or outcome basis rather than on time and materials. To price these contracts, we must estimate the effort, staffing, technology and time required to deliver over multi-year terms. Our estimates may prove inaccurate because of unforeseen technical complexity, customer-caused delays, wage inflation, changes in scope or failures by subcontractors. When our costs exceed our estimates, our margins on those contracts decline, and in some cases we must record losses.

Our managed services agreements also commonly include service level commitments with financial credits or penalties for underperformance. In addition, we often incur significant transition costs at the start of a contract that we recover only over its term. If a customer terminates early, if we fail to meet service levels, or if we cannot achieve the productivity improvements assumed in our pricing, our profitability could be materially reduced.

Our transition toward subscription and consumption-based models may cause our results to fluctuate and may not be successful.

We continue to shift our software business from perpetual licenses toward subscription and consumption-based arrangements. Under these models we recognize revenue over the contract term or as customers use our services, rather than largely at the outset. This can lower near-term revenue compared with perpetual license sales of similar value, and it makes our results more dependent on renewals and actual usage. Consumption-based revenue can be difficult to forecast because it depends on customer workloads, which may fall when customers optimize their cloud spending or face budget pressure.

Delivering cloud-based services also requires us to invest in infrastructure and operations, or to pay third-party cloud providers, which can lower our gross margins on those offerings. If customers do not adopt our subscription and cloud offerings at the rate we expect, or if the cost to deliver them is higher than anticipated, our revenue growth and margins could suffer.

Our research and development investments may not produce successful products or enhancements.

We invest a significant and growing share of our revenue in research and development, and we expect to continue doing so. Technology in our markets changes quickly, and customer preferences can shift unpredictably. Our investments may not result in new offerings or enhancements that customers value, products may be delayed, or competitors may introduce comparable or superior capabilities first. Because research and development costs are largely incurred before the related revenue, investments that fail to gain market acceptance would reduce our operating income without a corresponding benefit.

Acquisitions, investments and divestitures could disrupt our business and may not achieve their intended benefits.

As part of our capital allocation strategy, we pursue selective acquisitions to add technology, talent or market access, and we may divest businesses that no longer fit our strategy. Acquisitions involve risks, including difficulty integrating products, systems, personnel and cultures; loss of key employees or customers of the acquired business; assumption of unknown liabilities, including security vulnerabilities and compliance deficiencies; and diversion of management attention. We may pay more than an acquired business turns out to be worth, which could result in impairment charges. Divestitures may require us to provide transition services, retain certain liabilities, or accept stranded costs. Any of these outcomes could adversely affect our results of operations.

Our business with government customers is subject to specific risks.

We provide software and services to national, state and local government agencies. Government contracts are subject to budget and appropriation cycles, political priorities and legislative changes, any of which can delay, reduce or cancel programs. Government customers typically may terminate contracts for convenience, audit our costs and compliance, and impose specialized requirements relating to security clearances, data handling, pricing and accounting. Risks Related to Our Operations and Workforce

Our labor-intensive cost structure makes us vulnerable to wage inflation, attrition and talent shortages.

We employ approximately 163,900 people, the majority of whom work in our Managed and Professional Services segment. Personnel costs are the largest component of our cost of revenue, which is substantially greater than our gross profit. As a result, our margins are sensitive to changes in compensation levels, utilization and workforce productivity. Competition for skilled technical personnel, particularly in cloud engineering, cybersecurity, data science and artificial intelligence, is intense, and wage inflation in our principal delivery locations can rise faster than we are able to raise prices.

High attrition increases our recruiting and training costs, disrupts client engagements, and can weaken service quality. If we cannot attract, develop and retain qualified personnel at acceptable cost, or keep utilization at appropriate levels, our ability to deliver and grow our services business would be impaired and our operating margins could decline. The loss of key executives or senior technical leaders could also disrupt our operations and the execution of our strategy.

Our global delivery model exposes us to geopolitical, economic and operational risks in the countries where we operate.

We deliver services through onshore, nearshore and offshore delivery centers in many countries, and a significant portion of our workforce is located outside the United States. Our operations could be disrupted by political instability, armed conflict, civil unrest, terrorism, natural disasters, public health emergencies, or deterioration in infrastructure in those countries. Changes in immigration and work visa policies could limit our ability to deploy personnel where customers need them. In addition, some customers, legislators and regulators have opposed offshore outsourcing, and restrictions on cross-border delivery or data transfers could reduce demand for our offerings or increase the cost to deliver them.

A cybersecurity incident affecting our systems or the customer environments we manage could result in significant liability, loss of customers and reputational harm.

We develop security products, operate security monitoring services, and manage critical infrastructure and applications for many customers. As a result, we are an attractive target for nation-state actors, criminal organizations and other malicious parties. Our systems, our products, and the customer environments we operate could be compromised through software vulnerabilities, supply chain attacks, ransomware, social engineering, insider misconduct or errors by our personnel or third parties. Attackers increasingly use artificial intelligence to scale and refine their techniques. Because we often hold privileged access to customer systems, a single breach of our environment could spread to many customers.

A significant incident could expose us to claims for damages, regulatory investigations and penalties, contractual liability, remediation costs and the loss of current and prospective customers. Because security is central to the value of our offerings, a breach could damage our reputation more than it would damage a company outside the security market. Our insurance may not be sufficient to cover all losses, and contractual limitations of liability may not be enforceable in every case.

Interruptions or performance problems in our services or the infrastructure on which they depend could harm our business.

Our customers rely on our cloud platforms, applications and managed services to run mission-critical operations. These services depend on our data centers, our network and software systems, and capacity purchased from third-party public cloud and colocation providers. Outages, capacity constraints, software defects, failed upgrades, power failures or disruptions at third-party providers could make our services unavailable or degrade their performance. We do not control the operations of the third-party providers on which we depend, and changes in their pricing, terms or service quality could increase our costs or affect our ability to serve customers. Service disruptions could lead to service level credits, contract terminations, claims and damage to our reputation.

Defects, errors or vulnerabilities in our software or in the solutions we implement could result in claims and loss of market acceptance.

Complex software and the systems we build for customers may contain errors, defects or security vulnerabilities, particularly when first released or deployed into heterogeneous customer environments. Our artificial intelligence capabilities may produce inaccurate, biased or otherwise inappropriate outputs. Errors could cause customers to experience data loss, operational failures or regulatory violations, and could lead to warranty claims, litigation, delays in revenue recognition and the expense of remediation. Our contracts may not adequately limit our liability, and defending claims could be costly regardless of their merit.

In addition, vulnerabilities in third-party components, including open source software, could expose our products and customers to security risk.

Our internal systems and business processes may not keep pace with the scale and complexity of our operations.

We rely on enterprise systems for financial reporting, billing, resource planning and contract management across many jurisdictions. Upgrading or replacing these systems, or integrating the systems of acquired businesses, involves risks of implementation delays, cost overruns, and errors in financial or operational data. Failures in these systems could impair our ability to bill accurately, report results on time, or manage our workforce efficiently.

Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity Risk Management and Strategy

Cybersecurity is central to our business. We deliver software, cloud services and managed services that customers rely on to run mission-critical workloads and protect sensitive data. We also operate substantial portions of the technology environments of many large enterprise and public sector customers. As a result, a cybersecurity incident could affect both our own operations and the operations of the organizations we serve.

We maintain an information security program designed to identify, assess, prioritize and manage material risks from cybersecurity threats to our corporate systems, our products and cloud services, and the customer environments we manage. The program is aligned with widely recognized cybersecurity and secure software development frameworks published by governmental and industry standards bodies. We use these frameworks as reference points to organize and measure our program. Our use of them does not mean that we satisfy any particular technical standard or certification requirement.

Cybersecurity risk is part of our enterprise risk management process. Our enterprise risk function considers it alongside operational, financial, legal and strategic risks when it evaluates the principal risks facing the Company and reports them to senior management and the board of directors.

The principal elements of our program include the following:

  • Risk identification and assessment. We regularly assess risks to our critical systems, data, products and delivery operations, taking into account threat intelligence, the results of vulnerability scanning and penetration testing, changes in our technology environment, and developments in the regulatory landscape.
  • Secure product development. Security requirements are built into the design, development and release of our software platforms and cloud services. This includes threat modeling, code review, automated security testing, software supply chain controls and coordinated processes for disclosing and remediating vulnerabilities.
  • Security operations. Our global security operations centers monitor our corporate environment and our cloud services continuously. In collaboration with our Managed and Professional Services segment, they also monitor the customer environments we operate under managed services and managed detection and response agreements.
  • Access and data protection controls. We apply identity governance, privileged access management, multi-factor authentication, encryption and data classification standards to limit access to systems and information according to role and sensitivity.
  • Incident response. We maintain a documented incident response plan supported by playbooks for specific threat scenarios. The plan defines roles, escalation thresholds and communication protocols, including procedures for notifying affected customers, regulators and law enforcement where appropriate. Incidents are escalated to senior management, the Chief Executive Officer and the board of directors according to their severity and potential impact.
  • Exercises and post-incident review. We regularly test our response capabilities through tabletop exercises and technical simulations involving security personnel, business leaders and members of senior management. Significant incidents are followed by structured reviews to determine root causes and improve controls.
  • Training and awareness. All employees complete security and privacy training when they join the Company and periodically thereafter. Personnel with privileged access, software engineers and incident responders receive additional role-based training. Simulated phishing campaigns supplement our awareness efforts.
  • Resilience and continuity. We maintain business continuity and disaster recovery plans for our corporate operations, delivery centers and cloud services, and we design our infrastructure to support recovery from disruptive events.

Use of Third Parties

We engage external assessors, consultants and auditors, where appropriate, to test our controls, conduct penetration testing, evaluate the maturity of our program and support incident response. Several of our cloud services and managed services offerings are also subject to independent audits and attestations requested by customers or required by regulators.

We rely on suppliers, subcontractors, software vendors and third-party cloud infrastructure providers in our operations and in delivering services to customers. Our third-party risk management process assigns these parties risk tiers based on the access and data they handle. It requires security due diligence before engagement, imposes contractual security and notification obligations, and provides for periodic reassessment of higher-risk providers. When a third party reports a security incident, we evaluate its potential effect on our systems, data and customers and take appropriate action.

Material Effects of Cybersecurity Threats

Like other large technology providers, we face frequent attempts by threat actors to gain unauthorized access to our systems and to the environments we operate. We have experienced cybersecurity incidents in the past. As of the date of this report, we are not aware of any risks from cybersecurity threats, including as a result of previous incidents, that have materially affected, or are reasonably likely to materially affect, our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. However, we cannot provide assurance that we will not experience a material incident in the future. Item 1A, "Risk Factors," describes the cybersecurity risks we face and how they could affect us.

Cybersecurity Governance

Board Oversight

Our board of directors oversees cybersecurity risk as part of its broader risk oversight responsibilities and has assigned primary oversight of cybersecurity, data protection and information technology risk to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee reviews the design and effectiveness of our information security program, significant cybersecurity risks and incidents, the results of internal and external assessments, and management's plans to address identified risks. The Chief Information Security Officer briefs the Audit Committee at each regularly scheduled meeting, and the chair of the Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full board of directors. The full board also receives periodic presentations on cybersecurity strategy and the threat landscape. Outside of scheduled meetings, management informs the Audit Committee chair, and as appropriate the full board, of significant incidents in accordance with the escalation criteria in our incident response plan.

Management's Role

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads our global security organization. The Chief Information Security Officer is responsible for our security strategy and standards, security operations, product and cloud security, incident response, and security governance, risk and compliance. The Chief Information Security Officer reports to our Chief Technology Officer and has direct access to the Audit Committee.

Our current Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience leading security programs at large global technology and services organizations. That experience includes building security operations capabilities, managing responses to significant incidents, and overseeing security for cloud services used by regulated customers. The Chief Information Security Officer holds recognized professional certifications in information security and is supported by leaders with expertise in application security, cloud and infrastructure security, threat intelligence, identity and access management, and regulatory compliance.

We also maintain a management-level cybersecurity risk committee chaired by the Chief Information Security Officer. Its members include senior leaders from legal, privacy, internal audit, finance, engineering and each of our reportable segments. The committee reviews significant risks, monitors remediation progress and helps ensure that cybersecurity considerations are reflected in business decisions. It also supports the assessment of whether an incident may be material for disclosure purposes, in consultation with our General Counsel and Chief Financial Officer.

TRENDS AND UNCERTAINTIES

Several conditions shaped our operating environment in fiscal 2024. We expect most of them to continue to influence our results.

Customer Spending Patterns. Large enterprise and public sector customers continued to put technology investment ahead of many other categories of spending. However, they applied greater scrutiny to discretionary projects, particularly short-duration consulting and implementation work. Customers generally favored investments that promise measurable cost savings, stronger security or faster adoption of data and artificial intelligence capabilities. Longer approval cycles for new projects tempered growth in parts of our services business. In contrast, demand for multi-year managed services and subscription software that supports core operations remained comparatively steady.

Artificial Intelligence. Customer interest in artificial intelligence, and particularly in generative models applied to enterprise data, grew substantially during the year. This creates opportunity for our Cloud and Data Platforms segment, whose data management, analytics and model lifecycle tools support these initiatives. It also creates opportunity for our Managed and Professional Services segment, which helps customers design and deploy such solutions. At the same time, customers increasingly expect productivity gains from automation to be reflected in pricing for labor-based services, as discussed in Item 1A. We are embedding artificial intelligence in our delivery operations, both to protect our services margins and to give customers the efficiencies they seek. We expect this dynamic to remain a significant influence on our services pricing and mix.

Hybrid and Multi-Cloud Adoption. Customers continued to run workloads across their own data centers and several public cloud environments. Governance, cost management and data residency requirements are becoming more complex as a result. We believe this supports demand for our hybrid cloud platform and for cloud migration and operations services. Customers' efforts to optimize cloud spending, however, can reduce consumption-based revenue in particular periods.

Cybersecurity. The frequency and sophistication of cyberattacks continued to rise, and regulators in several jurisdictions expanded disclosure and resilience requirements. Together, these factors sustained demand for our identity, threat detection and managed detection and response offerings. They also increased our own costs to secure the environments we operate for customers.

Labor Costs and Workforce Productivity. Our cost structure remains labor-intensive. At December 31, 2024, we had about 163,900 employees. Wage pressure for specialized technical skills persisted, especially in cloud engineering, data science and security. Lower attrition and improved utilization in our delivery centers helped offset these pressures in fiscal 2024. Further improvements in workforce productivity will be important to sustaining our margins.

Tax Environment. Many jurisdictions in which we operate have enacted, or are in the process of enacting, legislation implementing a global minimum tax framework. These rules began to apply to us in fiscal 2024 in certain jurisdictions and did not materially affect our effective tax rate for the year. Their effect in future periods will depend on further legislative action, administrative guidance, and the geographic mix of our earnings. They could increase our effective tax rate.

Macroeconomic and Geopolitical Conditions. Interest rates, inflation, foreign currency movements and geopolitical tensions continued to affect customer confidence and the cost of operating our global delivery network. We cannot predict how these conditions will evolve. A deterioration could lead customers to defer or reduce technology investment.

Profitability grew faster than revenue. Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $50,321 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $49,221 million in fiscal 2023. Gross profit was $29,732 million, compared with $27,797 million. Gross margin was 37.1%, compared with 36.1% in fiscal 2023.

Cost of revenue consists mainly of compensation and related costs for our services delivery and cloud operations personnel, subcontractor costs, cloud hosting and data center costs, depreciation and amortization of delivery-related assets, and third-party software and hardware passed through to customers. Cost of revenue grew more slowly than revenue in fiscal 2024, mainly for three reasons:

  • Mix. A larger share of revenue came from our CDP and EAS segments, whose software offerings carry higher gross margins than our labor-intensive MPS offerings.
  • Delivery productivity. Expanded use of automation and artificial intelligence tools in service desk, infrastructure management and application maintenance engagements reduced the effort required to meet contractual service levels. A more favorable geographic mix of delivery resources and improved workforce utilization also contributed.
  • Cloud operations efficiency. Optimization of our cloud hosting arrangements and better capacity planning helped contain the cost of delivering our subscription and consumption-based services as usage grew.

Wage increases for delivery personnel, particularly in specialized technical roles, partly offset these benefits. So did transition costs on new managed services contracts and higher hosting costs to support growth in cloud-delivered offerings. We expect continued mix shift and productivity programs to support gross margin over time. However, pricing pressure on services renewals and the cost of computing capacity for artificial intelligence workloads could limit further improvement.

Research and Development

Research and development expense was $11,023 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $10,546 million in fiscal 2023. It represented 13.8% of revenue in fiscal 2024, compared with 13.7% in fiscal 2023.

The increase reflects continued investment in engineering headcount and computing resources, especially for artificial intelligence capabilities across our CDP and EAS portfolios. These include model development, evaluation and monitoring tools, embedded assistants in our enterprise applications, and enhanced analytics in our security platform. We also invested in extending our hybrid cloud platform to additional infrastructure environments and in strengthening the security and resilience of our cloud-delivered services. Productivity initiatives within our engineering organization partly offset these investments, including consolidation of overlapping development efforts and increased use of automated software development and testing tools.

We regard research and development as essential to our competitive position and expect to keep investing at significant levels. The composition and pace of that investment may change as technology and customer demand evolve.

Selling, General and Administrative

Selling, general and administrative expense was $2,402 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $2,310 million in fiscal 2023. It represented 3.0% of revenue in both years.

The increase reflects higher sales compensation associated with new subscription bookings and managed services contract signings, investment in go-to-market capabilities for our data and artificial intelligence offerings, and general cost inflation. Continued simplification of our corporate functions, consolidation of facilities, and greater use of shared service centers and automation in finance, human resources and procurement partly offset these increases. Our selling, general and administrative expense remains low relative to revenue. This reflects our concentration on large, long-standing customer relationships, which require less selling effort per dollar of revenue than broad-based sales models.

Other Operating Expense

We recorded no other operating expense in fiscal 2024 or fiscal 2023.

Operating Income

Operating income was $16,307 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $14,941 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 9.1%. Operating margin was 20.4%, compared with 19.4% in fiscal 2023.

The improvement came mainly from gross margin expansion, driven by mix and delivery productivity, while operating expenses held steady as a share of revenue. Because operating expenses grew roughly in line with revenue, nearly all of the gross margin improvement carried through to operating margin.

Interest Expense

Interest expense was $504 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $482 million in fiscal 2023. The increase reflects a higher average level of long-term debt during the year and the refinancing of certain maturing obligations at prevailing market rates. Long-term debt was $11,192 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $10,707 million at December 31, 2023.

Income Before Income Taxes

Income before income taxes was $15,803 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $14,459 million in fiscal 2023, reflecting the higher operating income described above, partly offset by higher interest expense.

Provision for Income Taxes and Effective Tax Rate

The provision for income taxes was $1,487 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,385 million in fiscal 2023. Our effective tax rate was 9.4% in fiscal 2024, compared with 9.6% in fiscal 2023.

Our effective tax rate in both years was below the US federal statutory rate. The main reasons were research and development tax credits, preferential tax treatment of certain income from intellectual property and technology development activities, earnings in jurisdictions with lower statutory rates, and tax deductions related to stock-based compensation. The modest decrease in fiscal 2024 reflects higher research and development credits and the favorable resolution of certain prior-year tax matters. A greater share of earnings in higher-tax jurisdictions, and the initial application of global minimum tax rules in certain jurisdictions, partly offset these benefits.

Our future effective tax rate could be affected by changes in the geographic mix of our earnings, further implementation of global minimum tax legislation, changes in tax law, the outcome of tax audits, and changes in the valuation of our deferred tax assets.

Net Income

Net income was $14,316 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $13,074 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 9.5%. Net margin was 17.9%, compared with 17.0% in fiscal 2023. Net income grew faster than revenue because of the operating margin expansion described above and the slightly lower effective tax rate.

CRITICAL ACCOUNTING ESTIMATES

We prepare our consolidated financial statements in accordance with US generally accepted accounting principles. Doing so requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses and the related disclosures. We base our estimates on historical experience and on other assumptions we believe are reasonable under the circumstances, and we evaluate them on an ongoing basis. Actual results may differ from these estimates. We consider the following accounting estimates critical because they involve significant judgment and could materially affect our financial statements if actual results differ from our assumptions. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to the consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

Revenue Recognition

Many of our customer contracts include several performance obligations, such as software subscriptions, licenses, support, professional services and managed services. For each such contract, we must identify the distinct performance obligations and allocate the transaction price among them based on relative standalone selling prices.

When standalone selling prices are not directly observable, we estimate them using information such as pricing practices, the size and nature of the customer, geographic region, and expected costs plus a margin. Changes in these estimates could affect how revenue is allocated among performance obligations and when it is recognized.

For fixed-price professional services engagements, we generally recognize revenue over time, measuring progress on a cost-to-cost basis. This requires us to estimate the total costs to complete each engagement. Total cost estimates are reviewed regularly, and changes are reflected in the period they become known. When estimated total costs exceed the expected contract revenue, we record the full anticipated loss immediately.

Many of our contracts also contain variable consideration, which we include in the transaction price only to the extent a significant reversal of recognized revenue is not probable. Examples include service level credits, volume-based pricing, usage-based fees and performance incentives. Estimating variable consideration requires judgment about future customer usage and our own performance.

Capitalized Contract Costs

We capitalize incremental costs of obtaining customer contracts, mainly sales commissions. We also capitalize costs to fulfill contracts, such as transition and setup costs incurred at the start of managed services engagements, when those costs are expected to be recovered. We amortize these costs over the expected period of benefit, which can extend beyond the initial contract term when we anticipate renewals.

Determining the period of benefit requires judgment about customer renewal patterns and the useful life of the related technology. We periodically assess capitalized costs for recoverability. An impairment is recognized when the carrying amount exceeds the remaining consideration we expect to receive, less related costs. Early terminations or lower-than-expected renewal rates could accelerate amortization or result in impairment charges.

Goodwill and Acquired Intangible Assets

Goodwill was $3,819 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level at least annually, and more often if events or changes in circumstances indicate the carrying amount may not be recoverable. We may first perform a qualitative assessment. If we perform a quantitative test, we estimate the fair value of each reporting unit, mainly using discounted cash flow analyses supported by market-based valuation methods.

These analyses require significant judgments, including forecasts of revenue growth, operating margins and capital requirements, discount rates that reflect the risks of each reporting unit, and terminal growth assumptions. Based on our most recent annual test, the estimated fair value of each reporting unit exceeded its carrying amount, and we recorded no goodwill impairment in fiscal 2024 or fiscal 2023. Several developments could lead to impairment charges in future periods:

  • adverse changes in market conditions;
  • faster-than-expected disruption of our services business by artificial intelligence;
  • loss of significant customer relationships; or
  • increases in discount rates.

When we acquire businesses, we must also estimate the fair value of acquired intangible assets, such as developed technology and customer relationships, and determine their useful lives. These valuations rely on assumptions about future cash flows, customer attrition, technology obsolescence and discount rates.

Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Determining our provision for income taxes, deferred tax assets and liabilities, and liabilities for uncertain tax positions requires significant judgment.

We recognize the tax benefit of an uncertain tax position only if it is more likely than not to be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. We measure the benefit as the largest amount that is more likely than not to be realized on ultimate settlement. Our tax positions are subject to examination by tax authorities in many jurisdictions, including those concerning transfer pricing for intercompany transactions involving our intellectual property and our global delivery network. The outcome of these examinations could differ from our estimates.

We assess whether our deferred tax assets are realizable based on projected future taxable income, the reversal of existing temporary differences, and available tax planning strategies. We record valuation allowances where realization is not more likely than not.

Changes in tax laws, including the implementation of global minimum tax rules, together with changes in the geographic mix of our earnings and the resolution of tax audits, could materially affect our effective tax rate. That rate was 9.4% in fiscal 2024.

Loss Contingencies

We are involved from time to time in legal proceedings, claims, regulatory inquiries and contract disputes arising in the ordinary course of business. We record a liability when a loss is probable and the amount can be reasonably estimated, and we disclose matters where a material loss is reasonably possible. Assessing the likelihood and amount of potential losses requires judgment about the facts, the applicable law and the probable outcome of negotiations or litigation. We reassess these matters each reporting period, and actual outcomes may differ from our estimates.
