Item 1A. Risk Factors

Investing in our common stock involves risk. The risks and uncertainties described below are those we currently consider material to our business. Before making an investment decision, you should consider them carefully, together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8. Any of these risks could materially and adversely affect our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows or the trading price of our common stock. Additional risks that we do not currently know of, or that we currently consider immaterial, could also impair our business. Our actual results could differ materially from those expressed or implied in the forward-looking statements in this report as a result of the factors described below.

Summary of Risk Factors

The following summary highlights some of the principal risks we face. It is not complete, and you should read it together with the full discussion that follows.

- We face intense competition across each of our segments, and pricing pressure or loss of market share could reduce our revenue and margins.
- Our revenue growth has been moderate, and we may not be able to sustain or increase it.
- Rapid advances in artificial intelligence could reduce demand for portions of our labor-based services, compress pricing, or require investments that do not generate adequate returns.
- Our results depend on renewing and expanding multi-year relationships with large enterprise and public sector customers.
- Fixed-price and managed services contracts expose us to cost overruns, service level penalties and early termination.
- Our cost structure is labor-intensive, and we may be unable to attract and retain qualified personnel at acceptable cost.
- A cybersecurity incident affecting our systems or the customer environments we operate could result in significant liability and reputational harm.
- Interruptions in our cloud services or in the third-party infrastructure on which we rely could harm our customers and our business.
- Our indebtedness and our capital return commitments reduce our financial flexibility.
- Changes in tax laws, including the global minimum tax, could materially increase our effective tax rate.
- Evolving regulation of data privacy, cybersecurity and artificial intelligence could increase our costs and limit the use of our offerings.
- Adverse macroeconomic and geopolitical conditions could reduce technology spending by our customers.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

The markets in which we compete are intensely competitive, and failure to compete effectively could reduce our revenue, margins and market share.

Each of our segments competes in a crowded and rapidly changing market. In Cloud and Data Platforms, we compete with large public cloud providers that bundle infrastructure, data and artificial intelligence services, with independent software vendors, and with open source projects that customers can adopt at little or no licensing cost. In Enterprise Applications and Security, we compete with large application vendors, specialized software-as-a-service providers and a growing number of well-funded security companies. In Managed and Professional Services, we compete with global consulting and outsourcing firms, offshore-based service providers, regional specialists and the internal technology departments of our customers. Many competitors have greater resources in particular markets, more established brands in specific product categories, or lower cost delivery models.

Competitors may introduce products or services that are superior to ours, price their offerings more aggressively, bundle software with infrastructure or services in ways we cannot match, or consolidate to form larger and more capable rivals. Large customers frequently use competitive bidding when contracts come up for renewal, which can force us to accept lower prices or less favorable terms to retain business. If we cannot differentiate our offerings, maintain our pricing, or respond quickly to competitive developments, our revenue and operating margins could decline.

Our revenue growth has been moderate, and we may not be able to sustain or accelerate it.

Our revenue grew at a modest rate in fiscal 2024, reflecting the scale of our existing business, the mature character of certain services and software offerings, and the time required for newer cloud, data and artificial intelligence offerings to contribute meaningfully. Growth in our higher-margin software businesses may not be large enough to offset slower growth, or declines, in legacy services, perpetual license and support revenue. Customers may also delay or reduce discretionary projects, consolidate vendors, or bring work in-house.

Because our revenue base is large, even significant new business may not produce a meaningful increase in our overall growth rate. If we cannot grow revenue, we may be unable to keep expanding operating income through productivity and cost discipline alone, and investors may view our prospects less favorably. That view could adversely affect the trading price of our common stock.

Advances in artificial intelligence could disrupt demand for our services, compress pricing and require substantial investment with uncertain returns.

Artificial intelligence, including generative models and autonomous software agents, is rapidly changing how software is built and how technology environments are operated. Much of our Managed and Professional Services revenue comes from labor-based activities, such as application development and maintenance, testing, service desk operations and infrastructure management, which customers increasingly expect to be automated. Customers may demand that productivity gains from automation be passed through to them as lower prices. They may also shift from time-and-materials arrangements to outcome-based pricing, or perform more work themselves with artificial intelligence tools offered by third parties. Any of these developments could reduce our services revenue faster than we can replace it.

At the same time, we are investing significantly in artificial intelligence capabilities across our platforms and delivery operations. These investments may not produce products that achieve market acceptance. They may become obsolete as foundation models and competing tools evolve, or they may cost more than expected to develop and operate, including the cost of the computing capacity required to train and run models. If we fail to anticipate the direction of the technology, or if competitors with greater access to models, data or computing resources outpace us, our competitive position and results of operations could be harmed.

Our business depends on renewing and expanding relationships with large customers, and the loss or reduction of significant relationships could harm our results.

A substantial portion of our revenue comes from large enterprise and public sector customers under multi-year master agreements, subscriptions and managed services contracts. These customers generally have no obligation to renew when their contracts expire. Many contracts allow customers to reduce scope or terminate for convenience on relatively short notice, sometimes subject to termination fees that may not fully compensate us. Renewals frequently involve competitive procurements, price concessions and changes in scope.

Our largest relationships often span more than one segment. As a result, dissatisfaction in one area, such as a security incident, a missed service level or a failed implementation, could affect our revenue across several segments at once. Mergers, divestitures, financial distress or changes in leadership at a large customer could also lead to reduced spending or the loss of the relationship. The loss of, or a significant reduction in business from, one or more of our largest customers could adversely affect our revenue, profitability and cash flows.

Fixed-price, outcome-based and managed services contracts expose us to cost overruns, penalties and losses.

Many of our professional services engagements and most of our managed services arrangements are priced on a fixed-fee, unit-price or outcome basis rather than on time and materials. To price these contracts, we must estimate the effort, staffing, technology and time required to deliver over multi-year terms. Our estimates may prove inaccurate because of unforeseen technical complexity, customer-caused delays, wage inflation, changes in scope or failures by subcontractors. When our costs exceed our estimates, our margins on those contracts decline, and in some cases we must record losses.

Our managed services agreements also commonly include service level commitments with financial credits or penalties for underperformance. In addition, we often incur significant transition costs at the start of a contract that we recover only over its term. If a customer terminates early, if we fail to meet service levels, or if we cannot achieve the productivity improvements assumed in our pricing, our profitability could be materially reduced.

Our transition toward subscription and consumption-based models may cause our results to fluctuate and may not be successful.

We continue to shift our software business from perpetual licenses toward subscription and consumption-based arrangements. Under these models we recognize revenue over the contract term or as customers use our services, rather than largely at the outset. This can lower near-term revenue compared with perpetual license sales of similar value, and it makes our results more dependent on renewals and actual usage. Consumption-based revenue can be difficult to forecast because it depends on customer workloads, which may fall when customers optimize their cloud spending or face budget pressure.

Delivering cloud-based services also requires us to invest in infrastructure and operations, or to pay third-party cloud providers, which can lower our gross margins on those offerings. If customers do not adopt our subscription and cloud offerings at the rate we expect, or if the cost to deliver them is higher than anticipated, our revenue growth and margins could suffer.

Our research and development investments may not produce successful products or enhancements.

We invest a significant and growing share of our revenue in research and development, and we expect to continue doing so. Technology in our markets changes quickly, and customer preferences can shift unpredictably. Our investments may not result in new offerings or enhancements that customers value, products may be delayed, or competitors may introduce comparable or superior capabilities first. Because research and development costs are largely incurred before the related revenue, investments that fail to gain market acceptance would reduce our operating income without a corresponding benefit.

Acquisitions, investments and divestitures could disrupt our business and may not achieve their intended benefits.

As part of our capital allocation strategy, we pursue selective acquisitions to add technology, talent or market access, and we may divest businesses that no longer fit our strategy. Acquisitions involve risks, including difficulty integrating products, systems, personnel and cultures; loss of key employees or customers of the acquired business; assumption of unknown liabilities, including security vulnerabilities and compliance deficiencies; and diversion of management attention. We may pay more than an acquired business turns out to be worth, which could result in impairment charges. Divestitures may require us to provide transition services, retain certain liabilities, or accept stranded costs. Any of these outcomes could adversely affect our results of operations.

Our business with government customers is subject to specific risks.

We provide software and services to national, state and local government agencies. Government contracts are subject to budget and appropriation cycles, political priorities and legislative changes, any of which can delay, reduce or cancel programs. Government customers typically may terminate contracts for convenience, audit our costs and compliance, and impose specialized requirements relating to security clearances, data handling, pricing and accounting. A violation of procurement rules could lead to fines, contract termination, or suspension or debarment from future government work. Government shutdowns or funding delays could defer revenue and lengthen the time it takes to collect receivables.

Risks Related to Our Operations and Workforce

Our labor-intensive cost structure makes us vulnerable to wage inflation, attrition and talent shortages.

We employ approximately 163,900 people, the majority of whom work in our Managed and Professional Services segment. Personnel costs are the largest component of our cost of revenue, which is substantially greater than our gross profit. As a result, our margins are sensitive to changes in compensation levels, utilization and workforce productivity. Competition for skilled technical personnel, particularly in cloud engineering, cybersecurity, data science and artificial intelligence, is intense, and wage inflation in our principal delivery locations can rise faster than we are able to raise prices.

High attrition increases our recruiting and training costs, disrupts client engagements, and can weaken service quality. If we cannot attract, develop and retain qualified personnel at acceptable cost, or keep utilization at appropriate levels, our ability to deliver and grow our services business would be impaired and our operating margins could decline. The loss of key executives or senior technical leaders could also disrupt our operations and the execution of our strategy.

Our global delivery model exposes us to geopolitical, economic and operational risks in the countries where we operate.

We deliver services through onshore, nearshore and offshore delivery centers in many countries, and a significant portion of our workforce is located outside the United States. Our operations could be disrupted by political instability, armed conflict, civil unrest, terrorism, natural disasters, public health emergencies, or deterioration in infrastructure in those countries. Changes in immigration and work visa policies could limit our ability to deploy personnel where customers need them. In addition, some customers, legislators and regulators have opposed offshore outsourcing, and restrictions on cross-border delivery or data transfers could reduce demand for our offerings or increase the cost to deliver them.

A cybersecurity incident affecting our systems or the customer environments we manage could result in significant liability, loss of customers and reputational harm.

We develop security products, operate security monitoring services, and manage critical infrastructure and applications for many customers. As a result, we are an attractive target for nation-state actors, criminal organizations and other malicious parties. Our systems, our products, and the customer environments we operate could be compromised through software vulnerabilities, supply chain attacks, ransomware, social engineering, insider misconduct or errors by our personnel or third parties. Attackers increasingly use artificial intelligence to scale and refine their techniques. Because we often hold privileged access to customer systems, a single breach of our environment could spread to many customers.

A significant incident could expose us to claims for damages, regulatory investigations and penalties, contractual liability, remediation costs and the loss of current and prospective customers. Because security is central to the value of our offerings, a breach could damage our reputation more than it would damage a company outside the security market. Our insurance may not be sufficient to cover all losses, and contractual limitations of liability may not be enforceable in every case.

Interruptions or performance problems in our services or the infrastructure on which they depend could harm our business.

Our customers rely on our cloud platforms, applications and managed services to run mission-critical operations. These services depend on our data centers, our network and software systems, and capacity purchased from third-party public cloud and colocation providers. Outages, capacity constraints, software defects, failed upgrades, power failures or disruptions at third-party providers could make our services unavailable or degrade their performance. We do not control the operations of the third-party providers on which we depend, and changes in their pricing, terms or service quality could increase our costs or affect our ability to serve customers. Service disruptions could lead to service level credits, contract terminations, claims and damage to our reputation.

Defects, errors or vulnerabilities in our software or in the solutions we implement could result in claims and loss of market acceptance.

Complex software and the systems we build for customers may contain errors, defects or security vulnerabilities, particularly when first released or deployed into heterogeneous customer environments. Our artificial intelligence capabilities may produce inaccurate, biased or otherwise inappropriate outputs. Errors could cause customers to experience data loss, operational failures or regulatory violations, and could lead to warranty claims, litigation, delays in revenue recognition and the expense of remediation. Our contracts may not adequately limit our liability, and defending claims could be costly regardless of their merit.

We depend on technology partners, subcontractors and suppliers whose performance we do not control.

We integrate third-party software, hardware and cloud services into our offerings, resell or implement third-party platforms, and engage subcontractors to supplement our delivery capacity. If these parties fail to perform, raise prices, change their licensing terms, end their relationships with us, or become competitors, we may be unable to fulfill our commitments to customers on acceptable terms. Many of our partners also compete with us in other markets, which could limit the value of these relationships. In addition, vulnerabilities in third-party components, including open source software, could expose our products and customers to security risk.

Our internal systems and business processes may not keep pace with the scale and complexity of our operations.

We rely on enterprise systems for financial reporting, billing, resource planning and contract management across many jurisdictions. Upgrading or replacing these systems, or integrating the systems of acquired businesses, involves risks of implementation delays, cost overruns, and errors in financial or operational data. Failures in these systems could impair our ability to bill accurately, report results on time, or manage our workforce efficiently.

Risks Related to Financing, Capital Structure and Capital Allocation

Our indebtedness could limit our financial flexibility and expose us to interest rate and refinancing risk.

We have a significant amount of long-term debt outstanding, and we increased our borrowings during fiscal 2024. Servicing this debt requires a portion of our operating cash flow to be used for interest payments, which reduces the cash available for investment, acquisitions, dividends and share repurchases. Our debt agreements contain covenants that could restrict our ability to incur additional indebtedness, grant liens, or engage in certain transactions. A failure to comply with these covenants could result in an event of default.

When our debt matures, we may need to refinance it at higher interest rates or on less favorable terms, depending on conditions in the credit markets and our credit ratings. A downgrade of our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs, limit our access to capital, and affect how customers and partners perceive our financial strength, particularly for long-term managed services commitments.

Our capital return program may not continue at current levels, and capital returns reduce the resources available for other purposes.

We return a significant portion of our operating cash flow to stockholders through quarterly dividends and share repurchases, and in fiscal 2024 the cash we returned to stockholders through these programs was substantially greater than our capital expenditures. Our Board of Directors may change, suspend or discontinue dividends and repurchases at any time based on our results, cash requirements, investment opportunities, debt obligations and other factors. Any reduction could cause the trading price of our common stock to decline. At the same time, sustaining capital returns at their current level reduces our cash available for acquisitions, research and development, or debt reduction, and may require additional borrowing if operating cash flow declines.

Fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates could adversely affect our reported results.

We generate a substantial portion of our revenue and incur a substantial portion of our costs in currencies other than the U.S. dollar. In particular, our offshore delivery centers incur personnel costs in local currencies, while many related customer contracts are denominated in U.S. dollars, euros or other currencies. Changes in exchange rates can affect our reported revenue, operating margins and the value of our foreign assets and liabilities. Our hedging activities may not fully offset these effects, and hedging itself carries costs and counterparty risk.

We may be required to record impairment charges for goodwill, intangible assets or other long-lived assets.

We carry goodwill and acquired intangible assets from past acquisitions, as well as substantial property and equipment, capitalized software, operating lease right-of-use assets, and capitalized contract costs, which together represent a significant portion of our total assets. We review these assets for impairment when events indicate their carrying value may not be recoverable, and we test goodwill at least annually. A decline in the performance of a reporting unit, the early termination of a major managed services contract, the consolidation of facilities, or a sustained decline in our market capitalization could require us to record impairment charges, which would reduce our reported earnings.

Delays or failures in collecting receivables could adversely affect our cash flows.

We carry a significant balance of accounts receivable and contract assets, reflecting the multi-year, milestone-based and periodic billing terms common to large enterprise and government contracts. Customers experiencing financial difficulty, government funding delays, or disputes over deliverables and service levels could lengthen collection periods or lead to write-offs. Slower collections would reduce our operating cash flow and could affect our ability to fund capital returns and other commitments.

Risks Related to Legal, Regulatory and Tax Matters

Changes in tax laws or challenges to our tax positions could materially increase our effective tax rate.

Our effective tax rate is low relative to the U.S. federal statutory rate. This reflects the geographic distribution of our earnings, tax incentives in certain jurisdictions, research and development credits, and other factors. Many countries have enacted, or are in the process of implementing, legislation based on the global minimum tax framework, which may reduce or eliminate the benefit of lower tax rates and incentives in certain jurisdictions. Changes in U.S. tax law, including changes to the treatment of foreign earnings or to the deductibility of research and development expenditures, could also increase our tax expense.

We are subject to examination by tax authorities in the United States and in many other jurisdictions, including with respect to transfer pricing among our subsidiaries. Tax authorities may disagree with our positions, and the final outcome of examinations could differ from the amounts we have recorded. A material increase in our effective tax rate would reduce our net income and cash flows.

Evolving privacy, data protection and cybersecurity regulations could increase our costs and limit the use of our offerings.

We, and our customers, process significant amounts of personal and sensitive data, and we operate in jurisdictions with extensive and differing privacy and data protection laws. These laws govern the collection, use, retention, cross-border transfer and security of data and are frequently amended and reinterpreted. Data localization requirements and restrictions on international data transfers could require us to change our delivery model, build additional infrastructure, or forgo certain business. New cybersecurity incident reporting and resilience obligations, including those applicable to providers of services to critical infrastructure and financial institutions, impose additional compliance burdens. Non-compliance could result in significant fines, litigation and restrictions on our operations.

Emerging regulation of artificial intelligence could restrict our offerings and expose us to liability.

Governments in the United States, Europe and elsewhere are adopting laws and guidelines governing the development, deployment and use of artificial intelligence, including requirements relating to transparency, risk assessment, human oversight and the use of training data. These rules may vary among jurisdictions and may classify certain uses of our tools, or certain uses by our customers, as high risk. Compliance could require us to modify products, limit features in certain markets, or incur significant costs. Claims that our models or training practices infringe intellectual property rights, violate privacy laws or produce discriminatory outcomes could result in litigation, regulatory action and reputational harm.

We may be unable to protect our intellectual property, and we may face claims that we infringe the rights of others.

Our success depends in part on our proprietary software, methodologies and know-how. We rely on a combination of patents, copyrights, trade secrets, trademarks and contractual protections, which may not prevent competitors from copying or independently developing similar technology, particularly in jurisdictions with weaker intellectual property protection. We also use and contribute to open source software, and some open source licenses could, under certain circumstances, require us to disclose source code or limit how we license our products. Third parties, including patent assertion entities, may claim that our offerings infringe their rights. Defending such claims can be costly and may require us to pay damages or royalties, redesign products, or indemnify customers.

We are subject to anti-corruption, trade control and other laws in many jurisdictions, and violations could result in significant penalties.

Our global operations subject us to anti-bribery laws, export controls, economic sanctions, and import and customs regulations. Export controls on advanced computing and artificial intelligence technologies are expanding and changing rapidly and could restrict our ability to sell certain products, or provide certain services, in some countries or to some customers. Although we maintain compliance programs, our employees, partners or agents could take actions in violation of these laws. Violations could result in criminal and civil penalties, loss of export privileges, debarment from government contracting, and reputational harm.

We are involved in litigation and regulatory proceedings that could be costly and could adversely affect our business.

In the ordinary course of business, we are party to legal proceedings involving contract disputes with customers and suppliers, employment matters, intellectual property claims, and regulatory inquiries. Disputes with customers over large implementation or managed services engagements can involve significant claimed damages. The outcome of litigation is inherently uncertain, and an unfavorable resolution could require us to pay substantial amounts, change our business practices, or record charges that adversely affect our results of operations.

General Risks

Adverse macroeconomic and geopolitical conditions could reduce customer spending on technology.

Our results depend on the level of technology spending by enterprises and governments, which is influenced by economic growth, interest rates, inflation, credit availability, business confidence and public sector budgets. In periods of economic uncertainty, customers may delay or cancel projects, reduce the scope of managed services, optimize cloud consumption, or seek price concessions. Geopolitical tensions, trade disputes and tariffs could disrupt supply chains, restrict market access, and dampen demand in affected regions. Because a large portion of our cost base consists of personnel and long-term commitments that cannot be reduced quickly, a decline in revenue could reduce our profitability disproportionately.

The trading price of our common stock may be volatile.

The market price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly. Factors that could affect it include variations in our quarterly results, changes in guidance or analyst expectations, announcements by competitors, developments in artificial intelligence and its perceived effect on our business model, changes in our capital return program, and general market conditions. Securities class action litigation has often followed periods of volatility in a company's stock price, and such litigation could result in substantial costs and divert management attention.

Catastrophic events and climate change could disrupt our operations.

Natural disasters, extreme weather, pandemics, power or telecommunications failures, and other catastrophic events could disrupt our delivery centers, data centers and offices, and the operations of our customers and suppliers. Some of our significant delivery locations are in regions exposed to severe weather and climate-related risks. In addition, evolving climate-related disclosure requirements and stakeholder expectations regarding the energy consumption of data centers and artificial intelligence workloads could increase our compliance costs and operating expenses.

If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, investors could lose confidence in our financial statements.

We must maintain effective internal control over financial reporting and disclosure controls and procedures. The complexity of our revenue arrangements, including multi-element contracts and estimates of progress on fixed-price engagements, requires significant judgment and robust processes. Material weaknesses in our controls could result in misstatements, delays in filing our periodic reports, regulatory scrutiny, and a decline in the trading price of our common stock.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could discourage a takeover that stockholders may consider favorable.

Provisions of the Delaware General Corporation Law, together with certain provisions of our certificate of incorporation and bylaws, could make it more difficult for a third party to acquire us or to effect changes in our Board of Directors and management. These provisions could discourage transactions that would provide our stockholders with a premium for their shares and could limit the price that investors are willing to pay for our common stock.
