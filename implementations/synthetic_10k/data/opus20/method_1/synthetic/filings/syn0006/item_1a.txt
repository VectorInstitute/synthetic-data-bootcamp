Item 1A. Risk Factors

Investing in our common stock involves a high degree of risk. Before making an investment decision, you should weigh the risks described in this section together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 7, Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations, and our consolidated financial statements and the accompanying notes. The factors below are those we currently regard as material to our business, but they are not exhaustive. Developments we do not now anticipate, or that we presently consider immaterial, could also harm us. The occurrence of any of these risks could materially and adversely affect our business, results of operations, financial condition, cash flows and prospects, and the market price of our common stock could fall as a result. Many of the risks below are interrelated, and the occurrence of one may heighten the effect of others.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

The semiconductor industry is highly cyclical, and a downturn in demand in any of our end markets could significantly reduce our revenue and profitability.

The semiconductor industry has historically experienced sharp swings in demand. Periods of rapid expansion, tight supply and rising prices have been followed by periods of excess capacity, inventory corrections and price declines. These cycles result from changes in end-customer demand, capacity additions across the industry, inventory practices of manufacturers and distributors, and broader economic conditions. Our three segments serve data center, communications, automotive, industrial and embedded markets, and each follows its own cycle. Strength in one market may not offset weakness in another. In fiscal 2024, growth in our data center business was partially offset by inventory corrections at certain industrial customers and distributors and by cautious spending among some communications service providers. A downturn affecting several of our end markets at once could cause revenue, gross margin and operating income to fall faster than we can reduce costs.

Our recent revenue growth has depended heavily on investment in artificial intelligence infrastructure, and that investment may slow, change direction or shift to competing solutions.

A substantial portion of our revenue growth in fiscal 2024 came from accelerator products and related server processors in our Data Center and Computing segment. Demand for these products depends on large capital commitments by cloud service providers and other operators building artificial intelligence training and inference capacity. The pace of this spending is uncertain. Operators may pause or reduce purchases if they conclude that installed capacity exceeds near-term demand, if the commercial returns on artificial intelligence applications fall short of expectations, if power and data center capacity become limiting constraints, or if financing conditions tighten. Advances in model efficiency or software could also reduce the amount of hardware needed for a given workload.

Even if aggregate spending continues to grow, customers may favor architectures, interconnect standards or software ecosystems other than ours. Our growth in this area should not be viewed as indicative of future results. A slowdown in artificial intelligence infrastructure spending, or a loss of share within that market, would disproportionately affect our revenue and results of operations.

A limited number of customers account for a significant portion of our revenue, and the loss of, or reduced purchases by, any major customer could harm our results.

Our Data Center and Computing and Networking and Communications segments sell to a concentrated group of cloud service providers, server and networking system manufacturers and contract manufacturers. These customers have substantial purchasing power. They often negotiate aggressively on price, require us to hold inventory or dedicate capacity on their behalf, and are generally not obligated to purchase minimum quantities over extended periods. Their orders may be rescheduled or cancelled on short notice. Several of our largest customers have developed, or are developing, their own custom processors, accelerators and networking silicon, alone or with design-services partners. If these efforts succeed, they could displace our products in some of our highest-volume sockets. Because our products are often designed into a customer's platform for several years, losing a single design engagement can affect revenue for an extended period.

We face intense competition, and competitive pressure could erode our pricing and margins.

We compete with large, diversified semiconductor companies, specialized fabless designers, vertically integrated system companies and the in-house design teams of our customers. Some competitors have greater financial resources, larger software ecosystems, broader access to leading-edge foundry capacity or longer-standing relationships with key customers. In data center computing and networking, product cycles are short and leadership in performance, power efficiency and software support can shift quickly. In our analog, power and embedded markets, competitors with lower cost structures, including suppliers that benefit from government support in their home markets, may price aggressively, particularly for mature products. Our gross margin declined in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023, reflecting in part product mix, the costs of advanced packaging and memory content in our accelerator products, and pricing dynamics. Continued competitive pressure could further compress our margins.

Our cost structure is dominated by research and development and fixed manufacturing costs, so modest shortfalls in revenue can have an outsized effect on profitability.

We invest heavily in research and development, which represents a significant share of our revenue and grew faster in absolute terms than our selling, general and administrative expenses in fiscal 2024. Leading-edge product development requires large teams of engineers, expensive design tools, mask sets and pre-production wafers, often years before the related products generate revenue. Our internal fabrication and assembly facilities also carry substantial fixed costs. Our operating and net margins are relatively narrow, and operating income grew more slowly than revenue in fiscal 2024. As a result, a revenue shortfall, an unfavorable shift in mix or an increase in manufacturing costs could reduce operating income and net income significantly, because much of our cost base cannot be adjusted quickly. Reducing research and development spending to protect near-term profitability could weaken our competitive position in future product generations.

Our success depends on timely development of new products and process technologies, and we may fail to anticipate or respond to technological change.

Our markets are characterized by rapid changes in architecture, process technology, packaging, interface standards and software. We must make large, long-term bets on product definitions, process nodes and packaging approaches well before market demand is clear. Delays in tape-out, design errors, yield problems at our foundry partners or in our own facilities, or failure of a new product to meet performance or power targets could cause us to miss market windows or lose design engagements to competitors. Even successful design wins may not translate into the volumes we expect, because customers may delay or cancel their own product programs. Our products are also only as valuable as the software that supports them, and failure to keep our compilers, libraries and drivers competitive could limit adoption of our hardware.

We depend on distributors for a meaningful portion of our sales, and changes in channel inventory or distributor relationships could cause volatility in our results.

Our Automotive, Industrial and Embedded segment serves a large number of smaller customers mainly through distribution. Distributors carry inventory based on their own expectations of demand and may sharply reduce purchases when end demand softens or when they believe their inventory is too high, as occurred with certain distributors in fiscal 2024. We may have limited visibility into end-customer demand and channel inventory levels. Distributor agreements commonly include price protection, stock rotation and return rights, which can reduce our revenue or require reserves. The loss of a significant distributor, or financial difficulty at a distributor, could disrupt our access to customers and adversely affect collection of our receivables.

Acquisitions and strategic investments may not achieve their intended benefits and could disrupt our business.

We have acquired, and expect to continue to pursue, businesses, technologies and engineering teams that complement our portfolio. Acquisitions involve risks, including difficulty integrating products, roadmaps, information systems and personnel; loss of key employees or customers of the acquired business; assumption of unknown liabilities; diversion of management attention; and regulatory review that may delay, condition or prevent a transaction. We may pay a premium that is not recovered through future cash flows, and we may finance acquisitions with additional debt or equity, which would increase leverage or dilute existing stockholders. Our goodwill increased in fiscal 2024, and future acquisitions would likely add further goodwill and intangible assets subject to impairment risk.

Risks Related to Our Operations and Supply Chain

We rely on a small number of third-party foundries and packaging subcontractors for our most advanced products, and some of these suppliers are sole sources.

Our most advanced processors, accelerators and networking devices are manufactured by independent foundries using leading-edge process technologies available from very few suppliers worldwide, and in some cases from only one. We also rely on a limited number of subcontractors for advanced packaging, substrates, high-bandwidth memory integration and certain test operations. We do not control these suppliers' capacity allocation, pricing, yields or delivery schedules. In periods of high demand, we compete for capacity with customers and companies that are larger than we are or that have greater purchasing leverage. Long-term capacity agreements may require prepayments or minimum purchase commitments that we must honor even if demand declines. If a key supplier experiences a production problem, raises prices substantially, prioritizes other customers or exits a technology, we may be unable to qualify an alternative in time to avoid lost sales, and transitioning a design to another supplier typically requires significant engineering time and expense.

Our supply chain and operations are concentrated in regions exposed to geopolitical tension and natural hazards.

A substantial portion of our wafer fabrication, advanced packaging and test capacity, including that of our foundry and subcontract partners, is located in a limited number of locations in Asia. Some of these locations are subject to geopolitical tensions, including cross-strait and regional disputes, and to earthquakes, typhoons, flooding, drought and power or water shortages. An escalation of political or military conflict, the imposition of blockades, sanctions or trade restrictions, or a major natural disaster in any of these regions could severely curtail our ability to obtain finished products for an extended period. Our insurance may not cover, or may only partially cover, losses from these events, and the disruption could also affect our customers and their demand for our products.

Our internal manufacturing operations require significant capital investment and expose us to underutilization risk.

We operate wafer fabrication, assembly and test facilities in North America, Europe and Asia, and we are investing to expand and modernize capacity, including converting certain facilities to larger wafer diameters and adding specialty power semiconductor processes. These projects require substantial capital expenditures over several years and may encounter construction delays, cost overruns, equipment shortages, permitting challenges or difficulty achieving targeted yields. Because our internal facilities have high fixed costs, periods of weak demand in our analog, power and embedded businesses could leave capacity underutilized, raising our unit costs and reducing gross margin. We may also be required to record charges for restructuring, asset impairments or facility consolidation if we determine that capacity exceeds our long-term needs.

Our inventory levels have grown, and we may be required to record write-downs if demand weakens or products become obsolete.

Inventories increased in fiscal 2024, reflecting higher revenue, longer lead times for advanced components and strategic purchases intended to secure supply. Our manufacturing cycle times are long, and we generally commit to wafer starts and component purchases months before we receive customer orders. If demand falls short of our forecasts, if customers transition to newer products faster than we expect, or if pricing declines, we may be required to write down inventory to net realizable value, which would reduce gross margin. Conversely, if we underestimate demand we may be unable to meet customer requirements, damaging relationships and ceding share to competitors. Inventory of high-value accelerator products containing costly memory and packaging content presents particular write-down risk.

Shortages or price increases for materials and components, including memory and advanced substrates, could increase our costs and limit our output.

We depend on a broad range of materials and components, including silicon wafers, specialty gases, chemicals, substrates, precious metals and high-bandwidth memory. Several of these inputs are produced by a small number of suppliers, and the supply of high-bandwidth memory and advanced substrates has at times been constrained by industry-wide demand for artificial intelligence hardware. Price increases for these inputs, energy, logistics or labor could raise our cost of revenue, and we may be unable to pass these increases to customers, particularly under negotiated pricing arrangements. Our cost of revenue grew faster than our revenue in fiscal 2024, and further cost inflation could continue to pressure our gross margin.

Competition for highly skilled engineers and other personnel is intense, and we may not be able to attract and retain the talent we need.

Our future success depends on our ability to recruit and retain experienced semiconductor designers, process and packaging engineers, software developers and senior management. Demand for these individuals, particularly those with expertise in artificial intelligence architectures, high-speed interconnect, advanced packaging and power semiconductors, is intense, and competitors and customers actively recruit from our workforce. Equity compensation is an important part of our retention strategy, and a decline in our stock price could reduce its effectiveness. Restrictions on immigration and work authorization in the jurisdictions where we operate could limit our access to qualified personnel. The loss of key employees, or a failure to manage a workforce of our size across many countries, could delay product development and harm our business.

Cybersecurity incidents, including attacks targeting our intellectual property, could harm our business and reputation.

Our design databases, process recipes, source code and customer information are highly valuable and are attractive targets for state-sponsored actors, criminal organizations and malicious insiders. We also share sensitive technical information with foundries, subcontractors, design partners and customers whose security practices we do not control. Our products themselves may contain hardware or firmware vulnerabilities that could be exploited after deployment in our customers' systems. A successful cyberattack, ransomware event or discovery of a significant product vulnerability could disrupt manufacturing and operations, result in theft of trade secrets, require costly remediation and mitigation, expose us to litigation and regulatory penalties, and damage our relationships with customers.

Product defects, quality failures or safety issues could result in significant costs and liability.

Our products are highly complex and may contain defects or errors that are not detected until after shipment, sometimes only after deployment at scale in customer systems. Many of our automotive, industrial, medical and aerospace products are used in safety-critical applications and must meet demanding functional safety and quality standards. A defect could result in product recalls, replacement costs, warranty claims, indemnification obligations, lost revenue and reputational harm, and could expose us to product liability claims that exceed our insurance coverage. Customers in the automotive market in particular may seek to recover substantial costs associated with vehicle recalls attributable to components we supplied.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

Our indebtedness has increased, and our debt obligations could limit our flexibility and adversely affect our financial condition.

We have a substantial amount of long-term debt, which increased in fiscal 2024 as we borrowed to help fund capital investment and other investing activities. Our interest expense also increased during the year and represents a meaningful portion of our operating income. Our indebtedness requires us to dedicate part of our cash flow to interest and principal payments, reducing funds available for research and development, capital expenditures, acquisitions and returns to stockholders. It could also increase our vulnerability to industry downturns and limit our ability to respond to competitive developments. Our debt agreements contain covenants that restrict certain activities, and a breach could result in acceleration of amounts outstanding. As our debt matures, we may be required to refinance at interest rates higher than those on our existing obligations, and any variable-rate borrowings expose us to further increases in market rates.

Our operating cash flow has not been sufficient to fund our investing activities, and we may depend on access to external capital.

In fiscal 2024, cash used in investing activities, including capital expenditures and other investments, substantially exceeded the cash generated by our operations. Growth in accounts receivable, inventories and other working capital items absorbed a meaningful portion of operating cash flow. We funded the difference primarily through additional borrowings and, to a lesser extent, net issuances of common stock. If we continue to pursue capacity expansion, long-term supply commitments and acquisitions at current levels, we may need to raise additional capital. Our ability to do so on acceptable terms depends on conditions in the credit and equity markets, our credit ratings and our operating performance, none of which is assured. If we are unable to obtain financing when needed, we may be forced to delay or scale back strategic investments.

A downgrade in our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs and restrict our access to capital.

Our credit ratings reflect the rating agencies' assessments of our leverage, profitability, cash flow and business risk. Increases in our indebtedness, declines in our margins or cash flow, or large debt-financed acquisitions could lead the agencies to lower our ratings. A downgrade would likely increase the interest rates on future borrowings, could trigger higher rates under certain existing facilities, and could make suppliers and customers less willing to extend favorable terms or enter into long-term commitments with us.

Goodwill and other non-current assets represent a large share of our total assets, and impairment charges could materially reduce our results.

Goodwill, acquired intangible assets and other non-current assets together account for a substantial majority of our total assets, and these balances increased in fiscal 2024. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually and review other long-lived assets when events indicate their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. A sustained decline in our market capitalization, a deterioration in the expected performance of an acquired business or reporting unit, adverse industry conditions or higher discount rates could require us to record significant non-cash impairment charges. Given our relatively narrow net margin, an impairment charge could result in a net loss for the period in which it is recorded and could reduce stockholders' equity.

We may change or suspend our dividend, and future equity issuances could dilute existing stockholders.

We currently pay a cash dividend, but its declaration, amount and timing are at the discretion of our board of directors and depend on our earnings, cash requirements, debt obligations and other factors. Given our capital investment needs and leverage, our board may reduce or suspend the dividend at any time. In fiscal 2024, issuances of our common stock, including under equity compensation plans, exceeded our share repurchases. We expect to continue issuing shares under our equity plans and may issue additional equity to finance acquisitions or strengthen our balance sheet, either of which would dilute the ownership interests of existing stockholders.

Risks Related to Legal, Regulatory and Tax Matters

Export controls, sanctions and trade restrictions could limit our ability to sell products and source supplies.

The United States and other governments have imposed, and continue to expand, export controls on advanced semiconductors, semiconductor manufacturing equipment and related technology, particularly with respect to certain countries and end users. These controls restrict sales of some of our accelerators, high-performance processors and networking products, and may require licenses that are delayed or denied. Future controls could extend to additional products, performance thresholds or destinations with little notice, and could prompt affected customers to shift to domestic or alternative suppliers permanently. Other governments have responded with retaliatory measures, including restrictions on critical minerals, investigations of foreign suppliers and preferences for local products. Tariffs and trade disputes could also increase our costs, disrupt our supply chain and reduce demand for our customers' systems.

We may become involved in intellectual property disputes that are costly and could limit our ability to sell products.

The semiconductor industry is characterized by vigorous protection of patents, trade secrets and other intellectual property, and by frequent litigation. We have been, and may in the future be, subject to claims that our products infringe the rights of competitors, non-practicing entities or others. Defending such claims is expensive and diverts management and engineering attention. An adverse outcome could require us to pay substantial damages or royalties, redesign products, cease selling certain products or indemnify customers. At the same time, our own intellectual property may be difficult to protect in some jurisdictions, and others may independently develop similar technologies or misappropriate our trade secrets.

Environmental, health and safety regulations could increase our operating costs and expose us to liability.

Our fabrication facilities use hazardous materials and specialty chemicals, consume significant amounts of water and energy, and generate greenhouse gas emissions and regulated waste. We are subject to environmental laws in each jurisdiction where we operate, including evolving restrictions on certain fluorinated chemicals and substances used in semiconductor manufacturing, and expanding climate-related disclosure and emissions requirements. Compliance could require costly process changes, capital investment or substitution of materials for which qualified alternatives may not exist. Violations, contamination at current or former sites, or changes in regulation could result in fines, remediation costs, production interruptions and reputational harm.

Changes in tax laws and fluctuations in our effective tax rate could adversely affect our results.

We are subject to income and other taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate decreased in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023, but it may fluctuate significantly based on the geographic mix of our earnings, changes in tax laws and treaties, the treatment of research and development expenditures, the availability of tax credits and incentives, and the outcome of tax audits. Implementation of a global minimum tax framework in jurisdictions where we operate could increase our tax burden. We also receive, or may seek, government grants and incentives for manufacturing and research investments. These programs typically carry conditions regarding investment levels, employment, operations and future activities in certain countries, and failure to meet these conditions could require repayment and limit our strategic flexibility.

General Risks

Adverse macroeconomic conditions, interest rates and currency fluctuations could reduce demand and increase our costs.

Demand for our products depends on spending by businesses and consumers on computing, communications, vehicles and industrial equipment. Slower economic growth, elevated inflation, high interest rates, tightening credit conditions or financial market volatility could cause customers to defer purchases and capital projects. Higher interest rates also raise our own borrowing costs. We conduct business in many currencies, and changes in exchange rates can affect our reported revenue and expenses, the cost of our foreign operations and the competitiveness of our products relative to suppliers based in other countries.

Catastrophic events, public health crises and climate change could disrupt our operations and those of our customers and suppliers.

Natural disasters, extreme weather, pandemics, terrorism, armed conflicts and failures of critical infrastructure could interrupt our manufacturing, logistics and engineering operations or those of our partners. Climate change may increase the frequency and severity of such events and could affect the availability of water and energy that our fabrication facilities require. Our business continuity plans may not prevent significant disruption, and our insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate widely in response to variations in our quarterly results, changes in guidance or analyst expectations, announcements by competitors or customers, developments in artificial intelligence and data center markets, changes in trade policy, and general market conditions. Semiconductor stocks have historically experienced pronounced volatility, often unrelated to the operating performance of individual companies. Securities class action litigation has sometimes followed periods of price volatility and could result in substantial costs.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could delay or prevent a change in control.

As a Delaware corporation, we are subject to statutory provisions that may restrict business combinations with significant stockholders. Our certificate of incorporation and bylaws also contain provisions, including advance notice requirements and the ability of our board to issue preferred stock, that could make an acquisition of the Company more difficult without the approval of our board of directors. These provisions could discourage transactions that some stockholders consider beneficial and could affect the price investors are willing to pay for our common stock.
