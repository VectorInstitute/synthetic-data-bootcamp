PART I

Item 1. Business

Overview

Novix Aerospace, Inc. is a Delaware corporation headquartered in Princeton, New Jersey. We design, engineer, manufacture and support systems, components and services for the commercial aviation, defense and space markets. Throughout this Annual Report on Form 10-K, the terms "Novix," the "Company," "we," "us" and "our" refer to Novix Aerospace, Inc. and its consolidated subsidiaries collectively. Where a statement applies only to the parent entity, the context indicates that limitation.

Our products are installed on commercial transport aircraft, regional and business jets, rotorcraft, military fixed-wing and rotary-wing platforms, missiles, satellites and launch vehicles. We also provide aftermarket parts, repair, overhaul, upgrades and technical support for the installed base of equipment we have delivered over several decades. We serve airframe manufacturers, engine manufacturers, airlines, aircraft lessors, independent maintenance providers, defense prime contractors, the United States government and allied foreign governments.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, our consolidated revenue was $21,485 million, an increase of 7.5% over the prior fiscal year. Our consolidated financial statements and the accompanying notes appear in Part II, Item 8, "Financial Statements and Supplementary Data," of this Form 10-K. Management's analysis of our results of operations, liquidity and capital resources appears in Part II, Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations."

Our Mission and Strategy

Our mission is to make flight safer, more efficient and more capable for the people and institutions that depend on it. We pursue that mission through four strategic priorities:

  • Technology leadership. We invest in engineering and research and development to keep our products at or near the leading edge of performance, reliability, weight and energy efficiency. Our focus areas include electrified and more-electric aircraft architectures, advanced flight controls, thermal management, secure connected avionics and resilient space subsystems.
  • Operational excellence. We apply a common operating system across our factories and service centers. It emphasizes standardized work, lean manufacturing, quality management and continuous improvement, with the goals of on-time delivery, first-pass yield and cost competitiveness.
  • Lifecycle value. Many of our products remain in service for decades after initial delivery. We seek to capture value across the full life of each platform through spare parts, repairs, overhauls, upgrades and long-term service agreements.
  • Disciplined capital allocation. We balance organic investment in capacity, technology and talent with selective acquisitions that broaden our capabilities or extend our positions on key platforms, while returning capital to stockholders.

Reportable Segments

We manage our business through three reportable segments: Commercial Systems, Defense and Space, and Aftermarket Services. These segments reflect how our chief operating decision maker evaluates performance and allocates resources. Additional segment information, including segment revenue and operating profit, is included in Part II, Item 7 and in the Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements in Part II, Item 8 of this Form 10-K.

Commercial Systems

The Commercial Systems segment designs and manufactures original equipment for commercial transport, regional, business and general aviation aircraft and for commercial rotorcraft. Its principal product lines include primary and secondary flight control actuation, cockpit and cabin avionics, environmental control and cabin pressurization systems, electric power generation and distribution, fuel and fluid management systems, and engine-mounted components and accessories.

Commercial Systems products are typically selected early in an aircraft development program, often years before the first delivery. Once selected, we generally become the sole-source or primary supplier of that system for the production life of the platform. Revenue in this segment is driven mainly by aircraft production rates at our airframe and engine manufacturer customers, the mix of platforms in production, and the content we supply on each aircraft. The segment also performs non-recurring engineering work on new and derivative programs, which is frequently funded in whole or in part by the customer.

Defense and Space

The Defense and Space segment provides systems, subsystems and components for military aircraft, rotorcraft, unmanned systems, missiles and munitions, ground vehicles, naval platforms, satellites and launch vehicles. Its principal product lines include mission computers and processors, sensors and electro-optical systems, actuation and motion control for weapons and flight surfaces, ruggedized power and thermal management systems, secure communications and data link equipment, and spacecraft subsystems such as power conditioning, attitude control components and radiation-tolerant electronics.

This segment sells to defense prime contractors and directly to the United States government, principally the Department of Defense and its military departments and agencies, as well as civil space agencies. It also sells to allied foreign governments, both directly and through the United States government's Foreign Military Sales program. Defense and Space revenue is influenced by government budget priorities, the timing of contract awards and the production rates of the platforms on which we participate. Much of this segment's work requires facility and personnel security clearances.

Aftermarket Services

The Aftermarket Services segment supports the global installed base of commercial and military equipment manufactured by Novix and, in certain cases, by third parties. Its offerings include:

  • spare and replacement parts;
  • component repair and overhaul;
  • exchange and rotable pool programs;
  • retrofit and upgrade kits;
  • technical publications, training and field service engineering;
  • digital health monitoring and predictive maintenance tools; and
  • long-term, flight-hour-based service agreements.

Aftermarket demand is driven primarily by the size and age of the installed fleet, aircraft utilization (including revenue passenger miles and flight hours for commercial operators and readiness requirements for military operators), maintenance cycles and the regulatory requirement to maintain airworthiness. Aftermarket sales generally carry higher margins than original equipment sales and have historically been less sensitive to new aircraft production rates. They are, however, sensitive to changes in air traffic and fleet utilization.

Principal Products and Services

Across our segments, we organize our technical capabilities into the following principal product and service families.

Flight Controls and Actuation

We design and manufacture electromechanical, electrohydraulic and hydraulic actuators, flight control computers, pilot controls and associated sensors. These products move and position primary flight surfaces, high-lift devices, landing gear doors, thrust reversers, weapons bays and missile control fins. We are developing next-generation fly-by-wire and power-by-wire actuation intended to reduce weight and hydraulic complexity on future aircraft.

Avionics and Mission Systems

Our avionics portfolio includes integrated flight decks, displays, flight management and guidance systems, mission and display processors, communications and navigation equipment, and data concentrators. In defense markets, we supply open-architecture mission computers, sensor processing systems and secure data links designed to meet military environmental, security and certification standards.

Power, Thermal and Environmental Systems

We provide electric generators, starter-generators, power conversion and distribution equipment, batteries and battery management systems, air management and cabin pressurization systems, and liquid and vapor-cycle cooling systems. These products are increasingly important as aircraft and defense platforms adopt more-electric architectures and as onboard electronics generate greater heat loads.

Engine and Airframe Components

We manufacture fuel systems, pumps, valves, heat exchangers, sensors, ignition systems, engine control components and precision-machined structural parts for engine and airframe manufacturers. Many of these products are produced to customer design under build-to-print arrangements. Others are proprietary Novix designs.

Space Systems

We supply power electronics, actuation, thermal control, sensors and radiation-hardened processing for satellites, crewed and uncrewed spacecraft and launch vehicles operated by government and commercial customers.

Services

Through Aftermarket Services and, for certain government programs, Defense and Space, we provide repair and overhaul, sustainment, logistics, engineering services, training and data-driven fleet support. We operate repair facilities in the Americas, Europe, the Middle East and Asia-Pacific, which allows us to support customers close to their operating bases.

Customers

Our customer base includes:

  • commercial airframe manufacturers;
  • aircraft engine manufacturers;
  • business and general aviation aircraft manufacturers;
  • rotorcraft manufacturers;
  • passenger and cargo airlines;
  • aircraft leasing companies;
  • independent maintenance, repair and overhaul providers;
  • defense prime contractors;
  • the United States government; and
  • allied foreign governments and their defense ministries.

A relatively small number of large airframe and engine manufacturers account for a substantial portion of our Commercial Systems original equipment sales. This reflects the concentrated structure of the commercial aircraft industry. Similarly, the United States government, both as a direct customer and as the ultimate customer under subcontracts with prime contractors, represents a substantial portion of Defense and Space revenue. The loss of, or a significant reduction in purchases by, any of these major customers could have a material adverse effect on our business. See Part I, Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

Contracts with the United States government and with prime contractors under government programs are generally structured as fixed-price, cost-reimbursable or time-and-materials arrangements. Under fixed-price contracts, we bear the risk that actual costs exceed the contract price. Under cost-reimbursable contracts, we are reimbursed for allowable costs and earn a fee, which reduces our cost risk but generally limits our margin opportunity. United States government contracts typically permit the government to terminate a contract, in whole or in part, for its convenience. In that case we are generally entitled to recover allowable costs incurred and a reasonable profit on work performed, but not anticipated profit on unperformed work. Government contracts are also typically funded incrementally, subject to annual congressional appropriations.

Commercial original equipment contracts are commonly long-term supply agreements covering the production life of a platform. They usually include pricing schedules, escalation provisions, delivery requirements and warranty obligations. Aftermarket sales are made under catalog pricing, negotiated multi-year agreements, and flight-hour or fleet-management service contracts.

Sales and Marketing

We sell our products and services mainly through our own direct sales organization. It is structured around key customer accounts, platforms and geographic regions. Our customer teams combine business development, program management, engineering and contracts personnel. They engage with airframe and engine manufacturers from the earliest stages of new aircraft concept development through production and sustainment. For major programs, we often co-locate engineering staff with customer teams during development and certification.

In the defense market, our business development teams work closely with prime contractors, United States government program offices and international customers to identify requirements, respond to requests for proposals and pursue competitive and sole-source opportunities. International defense sales are conducted directly, through the Foreign Military Sales program, or through local partners where required. All international defense sales are subject to applicable export authorizations.

In the aftermarket, we market directly to airlines, lessors, military operators and maintenance providers. We supplement this with a network of authorized distributors and service partners. We maintain customer support centers that operate around the clock to address aircraft-on-ground situations. Our digital customer portals provide parts ordering, repair status tracking, technical documentation and fleet health analytics.

We also participate in major international air shows, defense exhibitions and industry conferences, and we sponsor customer advisory boards to gather feedback on product performance and future requirements.

Backlog

Our backlog consists of firm orders for which work has not yet been performed. It includes both funded and unfunded portions of government contracts. A substantial portion of our backlog relates to long-term commercial original equipment programs and multi-year defense contracts and is expected to be converted into revenue over several years. Backlog is subject to changes in customer delivery schedules, program cancellations, contract modifications and, for government contracts, the availability of appropriated funds. Accordingly, backlog may not be indicative of future revenue in any particular period.

Competition

The markets in which we operate are highly competitive. We compete with large, diversified aerospace and defense systems suppliers, specialized component and subsystem manufacturers, and the in-house capabilities of certain airframe manufacturers, engine manufacturers and prime contractors. In the aftermarket, we also compete with independent repair stations, parts manufacturer approval (PMA) suppliers that produce alternative replacement parts, and surplus and used serviceable material dealers. Some of our competitors have greater financial, technical or marketing resources in particular product areas. Some of our customers are also our competitors in certain markets.

The principal competitive factors in our markets include:

  • technical performance, reliability, weight and power efficiency;
  • total cost of ownership over the life of the platform;
  • price;
  • certification experience and the ability to support new aircraft through regulatory approval;
  • program management capability and on-time delivery;
  • quality, safety and regulatory compliance record;
  • breadth and responsiveness of aftermarket support; and
  • for defense work, security clearances, past performance and the ability to meet domestic sourcing requirements.

Competition is particularly intense at the time of platform selection. Once a supplier is chosen, it often holds that position for the life of the program. For this reason, our success depends heavily on winning positions on new aircraft and defense platforms and on derivatives of existing platforms. We believe our engineering depth, certification experience, extensive installed base and global support network position us well against our competitors.

We conduct research and development to create new products, enhance existing products and develop manufacturing processes. Company-funded research and development includes both independent research and development and customer-supported development to the extent we bear the cost. We also perform research and development under contracts with customers, including the United States government. That work is generally recognized as revenue and cost of revenue rather than as research and development expense.

Our current development priorities include:

  • electrified propulsion and power systems for future aircraft;
  • advanced thermal management for high-power electronics;
  • autonomous and optionally piloted flight control technologies;
  • secure, cyber-resilient avionics with open system architectures;
  • additive manufacturing of complex components; and

We maintain engineering and technology centers in the United States, Europe and Asia. We collaborate with universities, national laboratories and industry consortia on early-stage research.

Raw Materials and Suppliers

We purchase a wide range of raw materials, components and services from a global supplier base. Principal materials include aluminum, titanium, nickel-based superalloys, steel, composites, specialty chemicals and electronic components such as semiconductors, printed circuit boards and connectors. Some materials and components are available from only a limited number of qualified suppliers. Certain suppliers must hold specific regulatory approvals or security credentials.

In recent periods, the aerospace supply chain has experienced constraints in castings, forgings, electronic components and skilled labor, and these constraints have at times affected our delivery performance. We mitigate supply risk through long-term agreements, dual sourcing where practicable, strategic inventory positions, supplier development programs and close monitoring of critical suppliers. We generally seek to pass through material cost increases under escalation provisions in our contracts, although our ability to do so varies by contract type and customer.

Seasonality

Our business is not highly seasonal, but certain patterns affect quarterly results. Commercial original equipment deliveries and aftermarket sales are often lower in the third quarter, reflecting planned summer production shutdowns at our facilities and those of our customers, particularly in Europe, as well as reduced airline maintenance activity during peak summer travel periods. Deliveries and revenue are frequently higher in the fourth quarter as customers seek to meet annual delivery commitments.

In the defense market, the timing of United States government contract awards and funding is influenced by the federal government's fiscal year, which ends on September 30. When appropriations are delayed and the government operates under continuing resolutions, new program starts and production increases may be postponed. This can shift revenue between quarters or fiscal years.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Our results depend heavily on production rates in the commercial aircraft industry, which is cyclical and concentrated among a small number of airframe and engine manufacturers.

Revenue in our Commercial Systems segment is driven mainly by the rates at which our airframe and engine manufacturer customers build and deliver aircraft. Those rates depend on airline profitability, passenger and cargo traffic, fuel prices, the availability and cost of aircraft financing, fleet retirement decisions and general economic conditions. All of these factors are outside our control. Historically, the commercial aerospace industry has gone through pronounced cycles. Economic downturns, pandemics, terrorist events and regional conflicts have caused airlines to defer or cancel orders, and those deferrals and cancellations have led manufacturers to cut production. In recent years, airframe and engine manufacturers have also had difficulty meeting announced production rates because of their own quality, certification and supply chain problems. When a customer cuts or delays production, we generally have limited contractual protection. We may also be left holding inventory, tooling and capacity sized for higher volumes.

The commercial aircraft industry is highly concentrated. A small number of large manufacturers account for a substantial portion of our Commercial Systems original equipment sales. A production halt, quality-related stoppage, regulatory grounding, labor strike or financial distress at any one of these customers could quickly and significantly reduce our revenue and operating income. These customers also have considerable bargaining power. They regularly seek price reductions, longer payment terms, greater risk sharing and expanded warranty commitments, any of which could compress our margins.

Our aftermarket business is sensitive to air traffic, fleet utilization and the retirement of older aircraft.

Aftermarket Services sales generally carry higher margins than original equipment sales and contribute disproportionately to our profitability. Demand for spare parts, repairs and overhauls depends on how intensively commercial operators fly their fleets and on military readiness requirements. Reduced air travel or reduced flight hours would lower aftermarket demand, and so would airline consolidation, operators drawing down spare parts inventories, or accelerated retirement of aircraft that carry our content. Operators facing financial pressure may defer maintenance, use surplus or used serviceable material in place of new parts, or shift work to independent repair providers. In addition, regulators may approve alternative replacement parts manufactured by third parties, which could erode our share of aftermarket demand on platforms where we are the original equipment supplier. Because aftermarket earnings are a significant source of our operating margin and cash flow, even a moderate decline in this business could have a disproportionate effect on our consolidated results.

A substantial portion of our Defense and Space revenue depends on United States government spending, which is subject to budget, political and policy uncertainty.

The United States government buys from us both as a direct customer and as the ultimate customer under subcontracts with defense prime contractors, and in both capacities it represents a substantial portion of our Defense and Space revenue. Government spending priorities can shift with changes in administration, Congress, threat assessments and fiscal policy. Programs on which we participate could be reduced, delayed, restructured or cancelled. Funding for government contracts is generally appropriated annually, and Congress has frequently been unable to complete appropriations before the start of the government fiscal year. When that happens, continuing resolutions restrict new program starts and production increases, and they can delay contract awards and payments. Debt ceiling disputes, government shutdowns and sequestration-style spending caps could have similar or more severe effects.

We also sell to allied foreign governments, both directly and through the Foreign Military Sales program. These sales depend on foreign budgets, United States foreign policy, export approvals and diplomatic relationships, any of which may change with little notice.

We face intense competition, and our competitive position depends on winning positions on new platforms that may not enter production for many years.

We compete with large, diversified aerospace and defense suppliers, specialized component manufacturers and, increasingly, with in-house capabilities at our own customers. Some competitors have greater financial resources, broader product portfolios or lower cost structures than we do. In the aftermarket, we also compete with independent repair providers, parts manufacturers approved by regulators and airline-affiliated maintenance organizations.

Commercial and defense system selections are typically made years before first delivery, and an incumbent supplier generally holds its position for the production life of the platform. If we fail to win positions on new aircraft, engines, defense platforms or spacecraft, our revenue could decline over time as existing programs mature and go out of production, and the effect may not become apparent until many years after the competitive outcome is decided. To win new positions we must often commit to aggressive pricing, extensive non-recurring engineering investment and demanding performance specifications. These commitments may not produce the returns we expect if the platform is delayed, sells fewer units than forecast or is cancelled.

Our future success depends on our ability to develop and commercialize new technologies, including electrified, more-electric and secure connected systems.

Our strategy relies on technology leadership in areas such as electrified and more-electric aircraft architectures, advanced flight controls, thermal management, secure connected avionics and resilient space subsystems. Research and development represents a significant and recurring part of our cost structure, and we expect to continue investing substantially in these areas. Some of these technologies may not mature as expected, may cost more to develop than we anticipate, may not achieve certification on schedule, or may be overtaken by competing approaches. Customer adoption of new propulsion and power architectures, including hybrid-electric and alternative-fuel aircraft, is uncertain. If we invest in technologies that the market does not adopt, or fail to invest in technologies that it does adopt, our competitive position and results of operations could suffer.

Geopolitical conflict, trade restrictions and international operations expose us to risks beyond our control.

We operate manufacturing, engineering and repair facilities in the Americas, Europe, the Middle East and Asia-Pacific, and we sell to customers around the world. Our international activities are subject to political and economic instability, armed conflict, sanctions, changes in trade policy, currency fluctuations, restrictions on repatriating cash, differing labor and employment regimes, and the challenges of managing a geographically dispersed workforce.

Tariffs and retaliatory trade measures on aerospace products, raw materials or components could raise our costs and reduce the competitiveness of products we export. They could also disrupt the bilateral arrangements under which civil aircraft parts have historically moved between jurisdictions with reduced trade barriers. Escalating tensions between major economies could limit our access to certain markets, customers or suppliers. Ongoing regional conflicts have also contributed to shortages and price volatility in certain metals and energy, and to airspace closures that affect airline operations and aftermarket demand.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Fixed-price contracts expose us to the risk of cost overruns and losses.

A significant portion of our commercial supply agreements and government contracts are priced on a fixed or largely fixed basis, sometimes with escalation provisions that may not fully reflect our actual cost increases. Under these arrangements we bear the risk that our costs to design, certify and produce the product will exceed the contract price. Fixed-price development contracts carry particular risk, because they require us to estimate the cost of maturing technologies that have not yet been fully designed or tested.

Cost growth can arise from technical difficulties, design changes, labor inefficiencies, supplier failures, inflation in materials and wages, and schedule delays caused by customers or regulators. If our estimated costs at completion exceed the expected revenue on a contract, we must recognize a loss provision for the full amount of the expected loss in the period in which we determine that the loss is probable. Because cost of revenue represents a large majority of our revenue, relatively small percentage increases in our costs can have a meaningful effect on our gross and operating margins. Changes in contract estimates could cause our results to fluctuate significantly from period to period.

Our use of contract accounting requires significant estimates, and changes in those estimates could materially affect our reported results.

We recognize revenue on many long-term contracts over time based on the progress we have made toward completion. This approach requires us to estimate total contract revenue and total costs at completion, including the effects of variable consideration, award and incentive fees, learning curves, labor productivity and future material prices. These estimates involve substantial judgment and are revised as programs progress. Revisions may result in cumulative catch-up adjustments that increase or reduce operating income in the period recognized, and individual adjustments on large programs could be significant. Estimating these amounts is particularly difficult on programs that combine new technology, long durations and multiple contract phases.

Disruptions in our supply chain, including shortages of raw materials, castings, forgings and electronic components, could impair our ability to deliver products on time and at expected cost.

We depend on a broad base of suppliers for specialty metals such as titanium, nickel-based alloys and aluminum, as well as castings, forgings, precision-machined parts, semiconductors, printed circuit assemblies and other electronic components. Many of these materials and parts are available from only a limited number of qualified sources, and some come from a single source. Qualifying a new supplier can take months or years because of certification requirements. The aerospace supply chain has experienced persistent capacity constraints, labor shortages, quality escapes and financial distress among smaller sub-tier suppliers.

Supply disruptions have caused, and may continue to cause, late deliveries to our customers, contractual penalties, expediting costs and increased inventory levels as we buffer against shortages. Our inventories increased during fiscal 2024, reflecting in part these conditions as well as higher production requirements. A prolonged disruption, the failure of a key supplier, or sharp price increases that we cannot pass on to customers could materially and adversely affect our results of operations and cash flows. Some of our suppliers are also subject to export control, sanctions and domestic-sourcing restrictions, which can further limit our alternatives.

Product defects, quality failures or safety incidents involving aircraft or systems that contain our products could result in significant liability, costs and reputational harm.

Our products are critical to the safe operation of aircraft, spacecraft and defense systems. A defect in design, manufacturing or materials, or a lapse in our quality management or documentation practices, could result in product recalls, regulatory directives, retrofit campaigns, warranty claims, production stoppages and lost business. An accident involving an aircraft or system on which we supply content, even one not caused by our product, could lead to lawsuits, government investigations and damage to our reputation. Recent heightened regulatory scrutiny of quality systems throughout the aerospace supply chain has increased the likelihood and potential cost of audits, findings and corrective actions. Our insurance coverage may not be adequate to cover all claims, and insurance may not remain available on acceptable terms. Losses not covered by insurance could be substantial.

Our operations depend on attracting and retaining skilled engineers, technicians and production workers, and labor disruptions or shortages could affect our performance.

We employ about 25,300 people. Our business requires engineers, certified technicians, machinists, inspectors and program managers with specialized skills, and many positions in our Defense and Space segment require security clearances. Competition for this talent is intense, both within aerospace and from other technology-intensive industries. We rely on information technology systems to design, manufacture and support our products, to manage our supply chain and to process financial transactions. Some of our products, including connected avionics, data links and digital health monitoring tools, themselves process and transmit data. As a defense contractor and supplier of critical aerospace systems, we are a frequent target of sophisticated cyber attacks, including attacks by nation-state actors seeking access to controlled technical data and classified information. Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Risk Management and Strategy

Our information systems, engineering data and connected products are central to how we design, manufacture and support equipment for commercial aviation, defense and space customers. They support our product development, factory operations, supply chain coordination, aftermarket services and financial reporting. We face a broad range of cybersecurity threats. These include opportunistic attacks such as phishing, credential theft, ransomware and malicious code. They also include sustained, well-resourced campaigns by organized criminal groups and state-sponsored actors seeking access to defense technology, export-controlled data and intellectual property. Because our Defense and Space segment handles controlled unclassified information and, in some cases, classified information, and because our avionics and mission systems are increasingly networked, we view cybersecurity as both an enterprise risk and a product integrity matter.

Program Framework and Processes

Our information security organization maintains a written cybersecurity program designed to identify, assess, prioritize, mitigate and monitor cybersecurity risks across our corporate networks, operational technology in our factories and repair facilities, and the products we deliver. The program draws on widely recognized industry and government frameworks. It is also designed to support our obligations as a contractor to the United States government. Those obligations include requirements under the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement for safeguarding covered defense information, rapidly reporting cyber incidents, and preparing for assessment under the Department of Defense's Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification program. We are preparing the relevant parts of our business for certification at the levels our contracts require.

Key elements of the program include:

  • asset inventory, network segmentation and access controls, including multi-factor authentication and privileged access management;
  • continuous monitoring of our networks and endpoints by a security operations function that operates around the clock;
  • vulnerability management and patching, prioritized according to threat intelligence and the criticality of affected systems;
  • secure development practices and product security reviews for avionics, mission systems and other connected products, including software supply chain controls;
  • data protection measures for export-controlled, proprietary and customer information;
  • an insider threat program that combines technical monitoring with personnel security practices; and
  • mandatory cybersecurity awareness training for employees, supplemented by targeted training and simulated phishing exercises for higher-risk roles.

Use of Third Parties

We engage independent assessors, consultants and auditors to evaluate the design and operating effectiveness of our security controls. These engagements include penetration testing, red team exercises, maturity assessments and readiness reviews for government certification requirements, and we use the findings to direct remediation and investment. We also exchange threat information with government agencies, industry information sharing and analysis organizations, and peer companies in the aerospace and defense sector. This exchange improves our awareness of emerging tactics and indicators of compromise.

Supply Chain and Service Provider Risk

We depend on a large and geographically dispersed network of suppliers, subcontractors, software vendors and cloud and other service providers. A cybersecurity incident affecting any of them could disrupt our production, expose our data or compromise the integrity of components we incorporate into our products. We evaluate the security posture of suppliers based on the sensitivity of the data they access and the criticality of what they provide. Our methods include security questionnaires, contractual security and privacy terms and, for selected suppliers, external security monitoring. Where required, we flow down government cybersecurity requirements to our subcontractors and require them to notify us promptly of incidents that could affect our information or deliverables. We also work with smaller suppliers to help them understand and meet these obligations, recognizing that compliance can be burdensome for them.

Integration with Enterprise Risk Management

Cybersecurity risk is a defined category within our enterprise risk management process. That process assesses significant risks to the Company periodically and assigns executive owners to each. The information security organization provides threat and control assessments as inputs to this process. Where a cybersecurity risk is rated as elevated, the responsible owners develop mitigation plans, and progress is tracked and reported to senior management. The results of the enterprise risk assessment, including cybersecurity risks, are reviewed with the Board of Directors.

Effect of Cybersecurity Threats

Like other companies in our industry, we experience attempted intrusions and other cybersecurity events from time to time. As of the date of this report, we have not identified any cybersecurity threats, including any prior incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. However, our program cannot eliminate all risk, and a future incident could have a material adverse effect on us. Although we maintain cybersecurity insurance, it may not cover all costs or losses arising from an incident. For further discussion, see Part I, Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

Governance

Board Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees the Company's risk management, including cybersecurity risk. The Board has delegated primary oversight of cybersecurity and information technology risk to its Audit and Risk Committee. The committee receives regular reports from the Chief Information Security Officer and other members of senior management. These reports cover the threat environment, the status of key security initiatives, the results of independent assessments, compliance with government cybersecurity requirements, and significant incidents. The committee chair reports on these matters to the full Board. The full Board also receives a cybersecurity briefing at least annually and is informed between meetings of any incident that management assesses as potentially significant. Oversight of security matters relating to classified programs is coordinated with directors who hold appropriate clearances.

Management's Role and the Chief Information Security Officer

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads the corporate information security organization and is responsible for cybersecurity strategy, policy, security architecture, operations, threat detection and incident response across the enterprise. The Chief Information Security Officer reports to our Chief Information Officer and has direct access to the Audit and Risk Committee. The current Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience leading security operations and risk programs in the aerospace, defense and government contracting sectors, and holds recognized professional security certifications. The information security organization works closely with our product security, legal, compliance, government security, internal audit and business segment leaders. A management-level cybersecurity steering group, which includes senior leaders from these functions, reviews cybersecurity risks, priorities and investments.

Incident Response and Escalation

We maintain a documented incident response plan that sets out procedures for detecting, containing, investigating, remediating and recovering from cybersecurity incidents. Incidents are assessed for severity according to defined criteria and escalated as appropriate to the Chief Information Security Officer, senior management and our legal function. The legal function, together with a cross-functional team, evaluates materiality and our disclosure obligations, as well as notification requirements to customers, government agencies and regulators. We test the plan through exercises that involve both technical teams and senior leadership, and we update it based on lessons learned and changes in the threat environment.

Item 7. Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations

Overview

Novix Aerospace, Inc. is a Delaware corporation headquartered in Princeton, New Jersey. We design, engineer, manufacture and support systems, components and services for the commercial aviation, defense and space markets. At the end of fiscal 2024 we employed about 25,300 people. Our products fly on commercial transport aircraft, regional and business jets, rotorcraft, military fixed-wing and rotary-wing platforms, missiles, satellites and launch vehicles. Through our aftermarket operations we also support the installed base of equipment we have delivered over several decades.

We manage the business through three reportable segments:

  • Commercial Systems supplies original equipment to airframe and engine manufacturers. Its revenue depends mainly on aircraft production rates and on the content we provide on each platform.
  • Defense and Space provides systems, subsystems and components to defense prime contractors, the United States government, civil space agencies and allied foreign governments. Its revenue depends on government budget priorities, the timing of contract awards and platform production rates.
  • Aftermarket Services provides spare parts, repair and overhaul, exchange programs, upgrades, technical support and long-term service agreements. Its revenue depends on fleet size, aircraft utilization and maintenance cycles.

Revenue

Consolidated revenue increased 7.5% to $21,485 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $19,982 million in fiscal 2023. Several factors drove the increase:

  • Higher original equipment volume in Commercial Systems, as our airframe and engine manufacturer customers increased deliveries on several narrowbody, widebody and business jet programs.
  • Continued growth in Aftermarket Services, supported by higher flight hours across the global commercial fleet and increased repair and overhaul activity.
  • Higher sales in Defense and Space on munitions, mission systems and space subsystem programs.
  • Revenue from businesses acquired during fiscal 2024 and the prior year.

Pricing contributed favorably, particularly in the aftermarket, where catalog price adjustments and negotiated escalation provisions partly offset inflation in our costs.

In Commercial Systems, revenue grew mainly because of higher shipments of flight control actuation, power generation and distribution equipment, and environmental control systems to airframe manufacturers. Growth in engine-mounted components and accessories also contributed. Customer production instability tempered the segment's growth. Several manufacturers delivered below their previously announced rates, which led us to adjust delivery schedules and, in some cases, hold finished goods and work-in-process inventory longer than planned. Non-recurring engineering revenue on new and derivative programs was broadly consistent with the prior year.

In Defense and Space, revenue growth reflected higher production on munitions actuation and guidance components, increased deliveries of open-architecture mission computers and sensor processors, and growth in space power conditioning and radiation-tolerant electronics. Foreign Military Sales and direct sales to allied governments contributed meaningfully. The timing of contract awards under continuing resolutions delayed some planned production increases into later periods.

In Aftermarket Services, revenue growth was driven by higher demand for spare and replacement parts and for component repair and overhaul. Airlines continued to fly aging fleets intensively while new aircraft deliveries remained constrained. Flight-hour-based service agreements also grew as more aircraft came under long-term contracts. Military sustainment activity increased as well, reflecting readiness requirements.

Segment revenue and segment operating profit for each reportable segment are presented in the Segment Information note to our consolidated financial statements in Part II, Item 8.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $14,831 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $13,958 million in fiscal 2023. Most of the increase reflected the higher sales volume described above, along with the cost structures of acquired businesses. Gross profit increased to $6,654 million from $6,024 million.

Gross margin improved to 31.0% in fiscal 2024 from 30.1% in fiscal 2023, for four main reasons:

  • Mix shift. A larger share of our revenue came from aftermarket parts and services, which generally carry higher margins than original equipment sales.
  • Pricing. Price realization in the aftermarket and escalation provisions in certain long-term supply agreements helped offset cost inflation.
  • Productivity. Our common operating system produced gains in standardized work, lean manufacturing and first-pass yield, which reduced scrap, rework and expediting costs at several facilities.
  • Cost absorption. Higher factory volumes spread fixed manufacturing overhead over more units.

Several factors partially offset these improvements. Inflation in direct labor wages, purchased components and certain raw materials continued. Supply chain disruptions caused production inefficiencies and premium freight costs. Unfavorable changes in estimates at completion on certain fixed-price development contracts in Defense and Space increased costs, as did early-production inefficiencies on new commercial programs, which typically carry lower margins until they reach mature rates. Depreciation and amortization charged to cost of revenue also increased, reflecting capacity investments and acquisitions.

Research and Development

Research and development expense was $1,328 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,234 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, research and development held steady at 6.2% in both years, consistent with our strategy of maintaining technology leadership while growing the business.

Spending in fiscal 2024 focused on the following areas:

  • electrified and more-electric aircraft architectures, including high-voltage power generation, conversion and distribution;
  • power-by-wire and fly-by-wire actuation;
  • advanced thermal management for platforms with growing electronic heat loads;
  • secure connected avionics and open-architecture mission systems; and
  • resilient, radiation-tolerant space subsystems.

The figures above include only company-funded research and development. Customer-funded development, including non-recurring engineering on commercial programs and development work under government contracts, is recognized in revenue and cost of revenue.

Selling, General and Administrative

Selling, general and administrative expense was $2,024 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,805 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, it increased to 9.4% from 9.0%. The increase reflected:

  • higher compensation and benefits costs, including incentive compensation tied to stronger operating performance;
  • the administrative cost structures of acquired businesses and related integration activities;
  • continued investment in information technology, cybersecurity and enterprise systems; and
  • higher selling and business development costs supporting new platform pursuits and international growth.

We expect integration activities and enterprise system investments to support improved administrative efficiency over time. However, the timing and amount of those benefits are uncertain.

Other Operating Expense

Other operating expense was $1,289 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,199 million in fiscal 2023. Other operating expense consists primarily of amortization of intangible assets recognized in business combinations, including customer relationships, program positions and developed technology. It also includes restructuring charges, acquisition and integration-related transaction costs, and other items not allocated to cost of revenue or operating expense categories. The increase in fiscal 2024 mainly reflected amortization of intangible assets from recent acquisitions and transaction and integration costs for businesses acquired during the year. Lower amortization on certain older intangible assets that became fully amortized partially offset the increase.

Operating Income

Operating income increased 12.7% to $2,013 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,786 million in fiscal 2023. Operating margin expanded to 9.4% from 8.9%. The improvement was driven primarily by higher revenue and the gross margin expansion described above. Higher selling, general and administrative expense and higher other operating expense partially offset these gains.

By segment, Aftermarket Services contributed the largest share of operating margin expansion, reflecting volume growth and favorable pricing. Commercial Systems benefited from higher volume and productivity but was constrained by early-production costs and customer schedule volatility. Defense and Space profitability reflected higher volume, offset in part by unfavorable estimate changes on certain fixed-price development programs.

Interest Expense

Interest expense was $213 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $187 million in fiscal 2023. The increase reflected higher average borrowings outstanding during the year, principally debt incurred to fund acquisitions. Long-term debt was $4,734 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $4,161 million at December 31, 2023. Higher interest rates on borrowings refinanced or newly issued during the year also contributed.

Income Taxes

The provision for income taxes was $319 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $286 million in fiscal 2023. The higher provision reflected higher income before income taxes, which was $1,800 million in fiscal 2024 compared with $1,599 million in fiscal 2023.

Our effective tax rate decreased to 17.7% in fiscal 2024 from 17.9% in fiscal 2023. Both years' rates were below the United States federal statutory rate. The main reasons were federal research and development tax credits, the deduction for foreign-derived intangible income, and the mix of earnings across jurisdictions with different tax rates. The modest decrease in fiscal 2024 primarily reflected a more favorable geographic mix of earnings and higher research credits, partially offset by fewer discrete benefits related to the resolution of prior-year tax matters.

Our future effective tax rate may vary because of changes in the jurisdictional mix of earnings, changes in tax law (including the continuing global adoption of minimum tax regimes), the outcome of tax examinations and the treatment of discrete items.

Net Income

Net income increased 12.8% to $1,481 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,313 million in fiscal 2023. Net margin increased to 6.9% from 6.6%. The improvement reflected higher operating income and a slightly lower effective tax rate, partially offset by higher interest expense.

Critical Accounting Estimates

We prepare our consolidated financial statements in conformity with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. This requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses and the related disclosures. We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions and other assumptions we believe are reasonable. Actual results may differ from these estimates.

The estimates described below involve a significant level of judgment and could have a material effect on our financial condition or results of operations. Our significant accounting policies are described in the Summary of Significant Accounting Policies note to our consolidated financial statements in Part II, Item 8.

Revenue Recognition on Long-Term Contracts

A significant portion of our revenue, particularly in Defense and Space and in certain Commercial Systems development and production arrangements, is recognized over time as we perform. For these contracts we generally use the cost-to-cost input method, which measures progress as the ratio of costs incurred to date to total estimated costs at completion.

Under this method we must estimate total contract revenue, including variable consideration such as incentive fees, award fees, unpriced change orders and escalation provisions. We must also estimate total contract costs, including labor, materials, subcontractor costs, overhead and the effects of technical risk and schedule.

We review these estimates regularly. Changes are recognized on a cumulative catch-up basis in the period in which they are identified. If estimated total costs on a contract exceed estimated total revenue, we recognize a provision for the entire loss in the period in which the loss becomes probable.

Estimates are inherently uncertain for fixed-price development contracts, contracts involving immature technologies and programs subject to customer schedule changes. Changes in estimates could be material to the results of a particular period.

For flight-hour-based service agreements in Aftermarket Services, we estimate expected fleet utilization, maintenance event timing and repair costs over the contract term. Those estimates affect the pattern of revenue recognition and the measurement of contract assets and liabilities.

Goodwill and Intangible Assets

Goodwill was $15,718 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $14,500 million at December 31, 2023, and represents a substantial portion of our total assets of $33,984 million. We test goodwill for impairment annually at the reporting unit level, and more often if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value.

We estimate reporting unit fair values using discounted cash flow analyses, corroborated by market-based approaches where appropriate. These analyses require significant assumptions, including projected revenue growth, operating margins, capital expenditures, working capital requirements, terminal growth rates and discount rates. They reflect our assumptions about customer production rates, defense budgets and aftermarket demand.

Our most recent annual test did not result in an impairment. However, adverse changes in market conditions, the loss of significant platform positions, prolonged reductions in customer production rates or increases in discount rates could reduce estimated fair values and result in future impairment charges.

We also review definite-lived intangible assets and other long-lived assets for impairment when events or circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. Determining the appropriate useful lives over which to amortize intangible assets also requires judgment.

Business Combinations

When we acquire a business, we allocate the purchase price to the identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed based on their estimated fair values at the acquisition date. Any excess is recorded as goodwill.

Valuing acquired intangible assets, such as customer relationships, program positions and developed technology, requires significant estimates. These include projected revenue and margins, customer attrition rates, royalty rates, technology obsolescence assumptions and discount rates. We often engage independent valuation specialists to assist with these valuations.

Changes in estimates during the measurement period may adjust the amounts recognized for goodwill and other assets and liabilities. The resulting fair values and estimated useful lives affect future amortization expense, which we report in other operating expense.

Pension and Other Postretirement Benefits

We sponsor defined benefit pension and other postretirement benefit plans covering certain current and former employees. Measuring the related obligations and expense requires assumptions about discount rates, the expected long-term return on plan assets, mortality, compensation increases and healthcare cost trends.

We set the discount rate by reference to yields on high-quality corporate bonds with maturities matching projected benefit payments. We base the expected return on plan assets on the plans' target asset allocation and long-term capital market expectations. Changes in these assumptions, and differences between actual and assumed experience, could materially affect our reported benefit obligations, the funded status of our plans, pension expense and future funding requirements.

For pension costs allocable to United States government contracts, the recovery of those costs through contract pricing is subject to government cost accounting standards. Those standards may differ in timing from expense recognition under generally accepted accounting principles.

Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and many foreign jurisdictions. Determining our provision for income taxes, deferred tax assets and liabilities, and liabilities for uncertain tax positions requires significant judgment. This includes interpreting complex tax laws and regulations, assessing the realizability of deferred tax assets and evaluating the likelihood that tax positions will be sustained on examination.

We record valuation allowances when we believe it is more likely than not that some portion of a deferred tax asset will not be realized. We recognize the benefit of an uncertain tax position only when it is more likely than not to be sustained, measured at the largest amount that is more likely than not to be realized on ultimate settlement.

Changes in tax law, the outcome of examinations and changes in our assessment of realizability could materially affect our effective tax rate and results of operations in the period in which they occur.
