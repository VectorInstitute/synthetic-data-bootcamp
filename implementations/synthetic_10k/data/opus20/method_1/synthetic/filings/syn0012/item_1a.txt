Item 1A. Risk Factors

An investment in our securities involves risk. The risk factors below describe the matters we currently believe could materially affect Venzen, including our business, financial condition, results of operations, cash flows and the market price of our common stock. Read them together with the other information in this Annual Report on Form 10-K, including Item 1, "Business," Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," Item 7A, "Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures About Market Risk," and our consolidated financial statements and related notes in Item 8.

The risks described here are not the only ones we face. Risks we do not currently know about, or that we now consider immaterial, may also adversely affect us. Many of the risks below are interrelated, and the occurrence of one may increase the likelihood or severity of others. The order in which the risks appear does not indicate their relative likelihood or potential magnitude.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Demand for our equipment and systems depends on cyclical end markets, and a downturn in any of them could reduce our revenue and profitability.

A large portion of our revenue comes from sales of new equipment and systems. That demand depends on the level of nonresidential construction, capital spending by industrial and process customers, investment by electric utilities and independent power producers, and production volumes of rail, commercial vehicle, marine and aerospace manufacturers. These markets are cyclical and sensitive to a range of conditions, including:

- interest rates and the availability of project financing;
- commodity and energy prices;
- business confidence and corporate profitability;
- public infrastructure budgets.

Customers often defer or cancel capital projects when economic conditions weaken or financing becomes more expensive. They may also reduce discretionary maintenance and retrofit spending.

Our aftermarket and services businesses have historically been more stable than equipment sales. However, they are not immune to economic pressure, and customers facing financial stress may extend replacement cycles or reduce service scope. Because our Climate and Building Technologies, Industrial Automation and Flow Control, and Transportation and Power Systems segments are exposed to different cycles, a downturn in one end market may not be offset by strength in another. A broad economic contraction affecting several end markets at once could have a material adverse effect on our results.

Increases in the cost, or reductions in the availability, of raw materials, components and energy could reduce our margins.

Cost of revenue represents the substantial majority of our revenue. Much of that cost consists of purchased materials and components, including:

- steel, copper and aluminum;
- electronic components and semiconductors;
- motors, compressors and castings;
- refrigerants, battery cells and specialty materials used in power conversion and energy storage products.

Prices for many of these inputs are volatile and can be affected by global supply and demand, trade policy, tariffs, geopolitical events, currency movements and production disruptions. We also consume significant amounts of electricity, natural gas and fuel in our manufacturing operations and service fleet.

We seek to offset cost increases through pricing actions, productivity programs, hedging of certain commodities and supplier negotiations. These measures may not fully or promptly offset rising costs. Many of our longer-duration contracts and engineered-to-order projects are priced well before materials are purchased, which limits our ability to pass through subsequent increases. Pricing actions may also reduce demand or prompt customers to shift to competitors or lower-cost alternatives.

We operate in highly competitive markets, and failure to compete effectively could reduce our market share, pricing and profitability.

We compete with large, diversified multinational industrial companies, specialized regional manufacturers, low-cost producers in emerging economies and, increasingly, technology and software companies offering building management, automation and analytics platforms. Some competitors have greater resources in particular product lines or geographies, lower cost structures, or closer relationships with certain customers or channel partners. Competitors may also be willing to accept lower margins to gain share.

Competition is based on product performance, energy efficiency, reliability, price, delivery times, technical support, breadth of offering, installed base and service coverage. Consolidation among competitors, distributors or customers could change the competitive landscape in ways that disadvantage us. If we cannot keep pace with competitors on technology, cost or service, we could lose share or be forced to reduce prices, either of which could adversely affect our results.

Our success depends on our ability to develop and commercialize new technologies, and our investments in research, development and engineering may not produce the results we expect.

Our strategy relies on extending our positions in energy efficiency, electrification, automation and decarbonization. These markets are characterized by rapid technological change, evolving regulatory standards and shifting customer preferences. Examples include the transition to lower global-warming-potential refrigerants, the growth of heat pumps and electrified heating, increasing adoption of connected and software-defined equipment, and new approaches to grid-scale energy storage.

We invest a meaningful portion of our revenue in research and development every year, and we expect to continue doing so. Development programs are uncertain and may take longer or cost more than anticipated. New products may fail to achieve market acceptance, be eclipsed by competing technologies or face unforeseen regulatory obstacles. If we misjudge the direction or pace of technological change, or fail to bring competitive products to market in a timely manner, our existing products could become obsolete. In that event, we may be unable to recover our development investments.

Fixed-price, engineered-to-order and performance-based contracts expose us to the risk of cost overruns and unmet performance commitments.

A portion of our revenue is generated under fixed-price contracts for engineered systems, integrated solutions and installation projects. It also includes performance-based contracts under which we guarantee specified levels of energy savings, operational savings, equipment availability or system output over a defined period.

Our pricing for these contracts depends on estimates of labor, materials, subcontractor costs, schedule and the performance of equipment in the field. Actual outcomes may differ from those estimates because of:

- design errors or unanticipated technical problems;
- site conditions and permitting delays;
- weather;
- supplier or subcontractor failures;
- changes in customer usage patterns;
- inflation.

If costs exceed our estimates, or if guaranteed performance levels are not achieved, we may incur reduced margins or losses. We may also be required to make payments to customers or pay liquidated damages, and our reputation could suffer. Because we recognize revenue and profit on many of these contracts over time based on estimated costs to complete, changes in our estimates can cause significant cumulative adjustments in the period in which they are identified.

Our backlog and order trends may not reliably indicate future revenue or profitability.

We report and monitor backlog and order levels as indicators of future activity, particularly in our engineered equipment, power systems and transportation businesses. Orders may be canceled, delayed, reduced in scope or modified by customers, sometimes with limited compensation to us. This risk is greater during periods of economic uncertainty or when customers face financing or permitting difficulties.

Strong order intake in certain markets, such as data center infrastructure and grid modernization, may reflect temporary factors, including customer stockpiling or project pull-forward, and may not continue at the same level. Projects in backlog may also be less profitable than anticipated if costs rise before execution.

Growth in certain high-demand end markets may not be sustained, and we may not realize returns on capacity we add to serve them.

Our recent growth has benefited from strong demand in markets such as data centers, electrical grid upgrades, energy storage and building electrification. To serve these markets, we have expanded and may continue to expand manufacturing capacity, engineering resources and service coverage.

Demand in these markets could moderate because of changes in customer investment plans, technological developments that reduce power or cooling requirements, shifts in government incentives, or broader economic conditions. If demand weakens after we have added capacity, we may experience underutilized facilities, excess inventory and pricing pressure, which could adversely affect our margins and returns on invested capital.

We rely on independent distributors, dealers, contractors and representatives for a significant portion of our sales.

We sell many of our standardized products through independent wholesale distributors, dealers, contractors and manufacturers' representatives. These channel partners are generally not bound by long-term commitments to purchase from us, and many also carry competing products. Their purchasing decisions are affected by their own inventory levels, financial condition and access to credit. Destocking by distributors can cause our sales to decline more sharply than end-market demand.

Consolidation among channel partners, the loss of key distributors or dealers, or the failure of channel partners to effectively market, install and support our products could reduce our sales. It could also weaken our position in local markets and impair our ability to grow our installed base and related aftermarket revenue.

Our growth strategy for aftermarket, service and software revenue may not be successful.

We seek to grow recurring revenue from parts, services, multi-year service agreements and subscription software associated with our installed base. Success depends on attracting and retaining customers who might otherwise use independent service providers, in-house maintenance staff or competitors' platforms. It also depends on recruiting and retaining qualified field technicians and successfully developing and deploying software offerings.

Subscription software requires up-front investment, and revenue is recognized over time, which may depress near-term margins. Customers may be reluctant to adopt connected offerings because of cybersecurity, data ownership or cost concerns. If we do not grow these revenue streams as planned, our results may become more sensitive to the cyclicality of new equipment sales.

Acquisitions, joint ventures and divestitures involve risks that could adversely affect our business and results.

We have developed our portfolio in part through acquisitions. During fiscal 2024 we completed several acquisitions in building controls, industrial automation software and power conversion, and we expect to continue pursuing acquisitions and other strategic transactions.

These transactions involve risks, including:

- difficulties integrating operations, systems, technologies, controls and personnel;
- loss of key employees, customers or suppliers of acquired businesses;
- diversion of management attention;
- failure to achieve anticipated synergies, growth or cost savings;
- assumption of unknown or contingent liabilities, including environmental, product liability, tax and compliance matters;
- overpayment relative to the value ultimately realized.

Acquired software businesses may operate under different business models and cultures that are difficult to integrate into an industrial organization. Divestitures may result in losses, ongoing indemnification obligations, stranded costs and transition service obligations. Any of these factors could adversely affect our results and could lead to impairment of goodwill or other intangible assets.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Our global operations expose us to economic, political, trade and currency risks.

We manufacture, engineer, sell and service products across North America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa, Latin America and the Asia Pacific region. Our international operations are subject to risks that include:

- changes in trade policies, tariffs, quotas, export controls and sanctions;
- political instability, armed conflict and terrorism;
- nationalization and expropriation;
- differing labor practices and legal systems;
- restrictions on the repatriation of earnings;
- difficulties enforcing contracts and intellectual property rights;
- potentially adverse tax consequences.

Escalating trade tensions among major economies could increase the cost of components we import, reduce demand for products we export, or require us to reconfigure supply chains at significant expense.

A significant portion of our revenue and costs is denominated in currencies other than the U.S. dollar. Fluctuations in exchange rates affect the reported value of our foreign revenue, earnings, assets and liabilities, and can affect the competitiveness of products manufactured in one currency and sold in another. Our hedging activities may not fully mitigate these exposures.

Supply chain disruptions, including reliance on single or limited sources of supply, could impair our ability to meet customer demand.

We depend on a global network of suppliers for raw materials, components and subassemblies. Some critical components, including certain semiconductors, power electronics, compressors, battery cells and precision castings, are available from a single or limited number of qualified suppliers. Qualifying alternative sources can take significant time, particularly for components used in safety-critical or certified applications such as aerospace, rail and fire and life safety products.

Supply disruptions could delay deliveries, increase costs, require expedited freight or redesigns, or result in lost sales and contractual penalties. Such disruptions may arise from supplier financial distress, capacity constraints, natural disasters, labor actions, logistics bottlenecks, cyberattacks, geopolitical events or trade restrictions. Building safety stock to mitigate these risks increases our inventory and working capital requirements and our exposure to obsolescence.

Cybersecurity incidents affecting our information systems or connected products could disrupt our operations, harm customers and expose us to liability.

We rely on information technology systems to run our manufacturing, engineering, supply chain, financial and customer-facing operations. Our products increasingly include embedded controls, network connectivity and cloud-based software that monitor and operate customers' buildings, industrial processes and power infrastructure. Some of these assets are part of critical infrastructure, which makes them attractive targets for sophisticated threat actors, including nation-state groups and criminal organizations using ransomware.

A successful cyberattack on our systems, our products or the systems of our suppliers and service providers could cause a range of harms, including:

- operational disruptions;
- theft of intellectual property or confidential data;
- physical damage or safety incidents at customer sites;
- regulatory investigations and fines;
- litigation and damage to our reputation.

Our recent acquisitions of software and controls businesses expand the systems and products we must secure and integrate. Our security measures may not prevent every incident, and insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

Product defects, safety incidents, warranty claims and recalls could result in significant costs and reputational harm.

Many of our products perform critical functions, including:

- fire detection and suppression;
- refrigeration of food and pharmaceuticals;
- control of hazardous industrial processes;
- distribution of electrical power;
- braking, propulsion and thermal management in transportation applications.

A defect or failure in any of these products could cause property damage, personal injury, loss of life, environmental harm or significant business interruption for customers.

We are subject to product liability claims, warranty obligations and, in some cases, mandatory recalls. Our warranty reserves are based on historical experience and engineering estimates, and actual costs could exceed them, particularly for new products or technologies with limited field history. Energy storage and power conversion products may present fire and thermal risks. Claims and recalls could result in significant costs, divert management attention, damage customer relationships and harm our reputation.

Our success depends on attracting and retaining skilled employees, and labor shortages or disputes could disrupt our operations.

We employ approximately 72,500 people, many of whom are skilled manufacturing workers, field service technicians, application engineers and software developers. Competition for these workers is intense in many of our markets, and shortages of qualified HVAC, electrical and controls technicians have constrained, and may continue to constrain, our ability to grow our service business and execute projects. Rising wages and benefit costs increase our cost structure.

Certain of our employees are represented by labor unions or works councils, particularly outside the United States. We may be unable to negotiate acceptable collective bargaining agreements, and strikes, work stoppages or other labor actions could disrupt production or service delivery. Labor actions at suppliers, customers or logistics providers could also adversely affect us.

Disruptions at our manufacturing facilities, including those caused by natural disasters and climate-related events, could adversely affect our ability to serve customers.

We operate a large network of manufacturing, distribution and service facilities, and we carry a substantial base of property, plant and equipment. Some products are manufactured at a single facility or depend on specialized equipment that would be difficult to replace quickly. Events that could disrupt operations at one or more facilities include fires, explosions, equipment failures, severe weather, flooding, earthquakes, extended utility outages, pandemics and civil unrest.

The physical effects of climate change, including more frequent or intense storms, heat waves, droughts and wildfires, may increase the likelihood and severity of these disruptions, both at our facilities and throughout our supply chain. Our insurance coverage may be insufficient to cover all losses, including lost profits.

Our operating system, productivity programs and restructuring actions may not deliver the expected benefits.

We rely on our operating system, including lean manufacturing, continuous improvement, disciplined pricing and supply chain management, to improve productivity and offset cost inflation. From time to time we also undertake restructuring actions, including facility consolidations, footprint optimization and workforce reductions.

These initiatives may cost more, take longer or produce smaller savings than planned. They may disrupt operations, impair customer service or affect employee morale and retention. If we fail to achieve expected productivity gains, our margins could decline, particularly in periods when we cannot fully recover cost increases through pricing.

Implementation of new enterprise resource planning and other business systems could disrupt our operations.

We are upgrading and consolidating several of our enterprise resource planning, order management and financial systems, including systems of acquired businesses. These implementations are complex, costly and time-consuming. Implementation problems could disrupt order processing, manufacturing, invoicing, collections and financial reporting, and could affect the effectiveness of our internal control over financial reporting. Delays or cost overruns could also divert resources from other priorities.

Risks Related to Financing and Our Capital Structure

Our indebtedness and other obligations could limit our flexibility and adversely affect our financial condition.

Our long-term debt increased during fiscal 2024, and interest expense rose compared with fiscal 2023. Our indebtedness requires us to dedicate a portion of our cash flow to interest and principal payments, which reduces the funds available for capital expenditures, research and development, acquisitions and returns to stockholders.

Our debt agreements contain covenants that may restrict certain activities, and a failure to comply with them could result in acceleration of amounts outstanding. We may incur additional indebtedness to finance acquisitions or for other purposes, which would increase these risks. Higher interest rates would increase the cost of refinancing maturing debt and of any variable-rate borrowings.

Our current liabilities exceed our current assets, and we depend on continued access to operating cash flow and credit markets to meet short-term obligations.

As of the end of fiscal 2024, our total current liabilities exceeded our total current assets, and the gap widened compared with the prior year end. We manage our working capital to fund operations, investments, dividends and debt service. We rely on cash generated from operations, our cash balances and access to commercial paper, credit facilities and debt capital markets to meet obligations as they come due.

A decline in operating cash flow could make it more difficult or more costly to meet short-term obligations. Such a decline could result from weaker demand, lower margins, slower customer collections, increased inventory or changes in supplier payment terms. A disruption in credit markets, a tightening of lending conditions or a downgrade of our credit ratings could have the same effect.

A downgrade in our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to capital.

Our credit ratings affect the cost and availability of our borrowings, including commercial paper. They also affect the terms on which suppliers, customers, surety providers and other counterparties are willing to do business with us. Rating agencies may lower our ratings for several reasons, including increased leverage, acquisitions financed with debt, sustained returns of capital to stockholders, weaker operating performance or adverse industry conditions.

A downgrade could increase our interest costs and restrict our access to certain markets. It could also require us to post collateral or letters of credit, or reduce our ability to obtain performance bonds needed for project work.

We have a substantial amount of goodwill and other intangible assets, and an impairment could significantly reduce our reported earnings and stockholders' equity.

Goodwill represents a significant portion of our total assets and a substantial majority of our total stockholders' equity, and it increased during fiscal 2024 as a result of acquisitions. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually, and more often if events or circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. Our assessments rely on assumptions about future revenue growth, margins, discount rates and market conditions.

Several developments could require us to record a non-cash impairment charge, including:

- adverse changes in economic conditions or end-market demand;
- underperformance of acquired businesses;
- increases in interest rates and discount rates;
- declines in our market capitalization.

Such a charge could be material to our results of operations and would reduce our stockholders' equity.

Our significant long-term liabilities could require future cash outlays that exceed our expectations.

In addition to long-term debt, we carry substantial other non-current liabilities. These can include obligations for pension and other postretirement benefits, deferred taxes, long-term warranty and product liability reserves, environmental remediation, deferred revenue on multi-year service agreements and other contingencies. The amounts recorded for these obligations depend on actuarial, legal and engineering assumptions, including discount rates, investment returns, mortality, claims experience and remediation costs.

Changes in these assumptions, adverse market performance of plan assets, new claims or changes in law could increase our liabilities. Any of these developments could require additional cash contributions or payments that reduce funds available for other purposes.

We may not continue to pay dividends or return capital to stockholders at current levels.

We have historically paid regular cash dividends, which represent a significant use of our operating cash flow. Any future dividends or share repurchases will depend on our results of operations, cash flows, financial condition, capital requirements, debt obligations, acquisition opportunities and other factors our Board of Directors considers relevant. Our Board of Directors may reduce, suspend or discontinue dividends or repurchases at any time. Any reduction or suspension could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

Our capital-intensive asset base requires continuing investment, and inadequate investment could impair our competitiveness.

We operate a large base of property, plant and equipment across many facilities. Maintaining, modernizing and expanding these assets requires ongoing capital expenditures. If we underinvest relative to the needs of our asset base or the requirements of growing markets, we may face lower productivity, higher maintenance costs, quality problems or insufficient capacity. If we invest in capacity that is not fully utilized, our returns on invested capital and our margins could decline.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

We are subject to extensive environmental, health and safety laws, and compliance costs or liabilities could be significant.

Our operations and products are subject to environmental, health and safety laws in each jurisdiction where we operate. These laws govern, among other things:

- air emissions and wastewater discharges;
- the handling and disposal of hazardous materials and wastes;
- workplace safety;
- the content and energy efficiency of our products.

We have incurred, and may in the future incur, costs to investigate and remediate contamination at current and former facilities and at third-party disposal sites, sometimes regardless of fault. New information, more stringent standards or changes in enforcement could increase these costs.

Regulations phasing down the use of high global-warming-potential refrigerants, strengthening minimum energy efficiency standards and restricting certain chemical substances require ongoing product redesign and recertification. They can also create transition-related inventory and pricing risks. Failure to comply could result in fines, penalties, product restrictions or loss of market access.

Our contracts with government entities subject us to additional risks.

We derive a portion of our revenue from contracts with federal, state, local and foreign government agencies, including military and defense customers, either directly or as a subcontractor. Government contracts are generally subject to termination for convenience and to audits and investigations. They also impose detailed requirements relating to cost accounting, procurement, pricing, cybersecurity, domestic sourcing and ethics.

Government spending depends on budget appropriations, which may be delayed, reduced or redirected because of political or fiscal pressures. Violations of government contracting requirements could result in contract termination, repayment obligations, civil or criminal penalties, or suspension or debarment from future government business.

We are subject to anti-corruption, trade control and sanctions laws, and violations could result in substantial penalties.

We are subject to anti-bribery laws in the United States and other jurisdictions, as well as export control, customs, economic sanctions and anti-boycott regulations. We operate in some countries with elevated corruption risk, and we rely in part on distributors, agents and other third parties whose conduct we cannot fully control.

Sanctions and export control regimes are complex and change frequently, particularly with respect to advanced technologies, dual-use products and certain countries. Despite our compliance programs, violations by our employees or third parties could result in significant fines, criminal sanctions, loss of export privileges, reputational damage and restrictions on our ability to do business.

Changes in tax laws or adverse outcomes of tax examinations could increase our effective tax rate and tax liabilities.

We are subject to income and other taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate decreased in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023, and it could increase in future periods because of several factors:

- changes in the geographic mix of our earnings;
- changes in tax laws, regulations or interpretations;
- changes in the valuation of deferred tax assets and liabilities;
- the outcome of tax audits.

The implementation of global minimum tax rules in many jurisdictions, changes to the U.S. taxation of foreign earnings and other legislative developments could increase our tax burden and compliance costs. Tax authorities regularly examine our returns, and the final resolution of these examinations could differ materially from our recorded positions.

We are subject to litigation and other legal proceedings that could result in significant costs.

In the ordinary course of business, we are involved in lawsuits, claims, investigations and other proceedings relating to:

- commercial and contractual disputes;
- product liability and warranty;
- intellectual property;
- employment;
- environmental matters;
- government regulation.

Some of these matters involve claims for substantial or indeterminate amounts and may take years to resolve. The outcome of legal proceedings is inherently uncertain, and adverse judgments or settlements in excess of our reserves or insurance coverage could materially affect our results of operations and cash flows. Defending these matters also requires significant management attention and expense.

Failure to protect our intellectual property, or claims that we infringe the rights of others, could harm our competitive position.

We rely on patents, trademarks, copyrights, trade secrets and contractual protections to safeguard our technology, particularly in controls, software, power electronics and advanced equipment designs. These protections may be challenged, invalidated or circumvented, and intellectual property laws in some countries offer limited protection. Employees, contractors or competitors may misappropriate our trade secrets.

Third parties may assert that our products infringe their intellectual property. Such claims could require us to pay damages or royalties, redesign products or cease selling certain offerings. The growing software and connectivity content of our products increases our exposure to these claims.

Evolving data privacy, artificial intelligence and product security regulation could increase our costs and restrict our offerings.

Our connected products, software platforms and service operations collect and process data about customers' facilities, equipment and, in some cases, individuals. Laws governing data privacy, cross-border data transfers, the cybersecurity of connected devices and the use of artificial intelligence are expanding and vary significantly across jurisdictions.

Compliance with these requirements may require changes to product design, data storage and business practices. Non-compliance could result in fines, litigation and restrictions on our ability to offer certain products or services.

Climate-related regulation and stakeholder expectations could increase our costs and affect demand for our products.

We are subject to increasing regulation and stakeholder scrutiny relating to greenhouse gas emissions, climate-related disclosure and sustainability performance. New disclosure requirements in the United States, Europe and other jurisdictions may increase our compliance costs and legal exposure. Carbon pricing or emissions limits could increase our energy and materials costs.

Many of our growth opportunities depend on policies and incentives that promote energy efficiency, electrification and renewable energy. Changes or reversals of these policies could reduce demand for certain of our products. If we fail to meet our publicly stated sustainability goals, or if those goals are perceived as inadequate, our reputation and relationships with customers, investors and employees could suffer.

General Risks

Inflation, interest rates and broader macroeconomic conditions could adversely affect our business.

Persistent inflation increases our costs for labor, materials, freight and services. Higher interest rates increase our borrowing costs and can dampen customer investment in construction, industrial and infrastructure projects. Economic slowdowns, financial market volatility, bank failures or sovereign debt concerns could reduce demand, impair customers' ability to pay, and limit our access to capital on acceptable terms.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to many factors. These include our operating results relative to expectations, changes in analysts' estimates, announcements of acquisitions or other strategic actions, developments affecting our end markets or competitors, and general market and economic conditions. Many of these factors are beyond our control and may be unrelated to our operating performance.

The loss of key executives or ineffective succession planning could adversely affect our business.

Our success depends in part on the experience and leadership of our senior management team and the leaders of our segments and key functions. The unexpected departure of one or more key executives, or an inability to attract and develop qualified successors, could disrupt our operations and impair our ability to execute our strategy.

Pandemics and other public health crises could disrupt our operations, supply chain and customer demand.

A pandemic or other widespread health crisis could cause facility closures, workforce shortages, supply chain interruptions and reduced customer activity, as such events have done in the past. Government responses, including travel restrictions and shutdown orders, could restrict our ability to manufacture products and deliver on-site services. The duration and severity of any future crisis, and its effect on our results, are difficult to predict.

Changes in accounting standards, or in the estimates and judgments underlying our financial statements, could affect our reported results.

Our financial statements depend on significant estimates and judgments relating to:

- revenue recognized over time on long-term contracts;
- warranty and other reserves;
- pension obligations;
- income taxes;
- goodwill and intangible asset valuation;
- business combination accounting.

Changes in these estimates, or in accounting standards and their interpretation, could materially affect our reported results and financial position. If we fail to maintain effective internal control over financial reporting, including at newly acquired businesses, we may be unable to report our results accurately or on a timely basis, which could erode investor confidence.

Provisions of Delaware law and our governing documents could discourage or delay a change in control.

As a Delaware corporation, we are subject to provisions of Delaware law that may discourage, delay or prevent a change in control that stockholders might consider favorable. Provisions of our certificate of incorporation and bylaws, including those governing advance notice of stockholder proposals and the ability of our Board of Directors to issue preferred stock, may have a similar effect. These provisions could limit the price that investors might be willing to pay for shares of our common stock.
