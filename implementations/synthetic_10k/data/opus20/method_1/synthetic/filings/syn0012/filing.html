PART I

Item 1. Business

General

Venzen Industries, Inc. ("Venzen," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") is a diversified industrial technology company. We design, manufacture, sell and service engineered products, systems and solutions for commercial, industrial, infrastructure, energy and transportation markets around the world. Our offerings help customers improve the efficiency, safety, reliability and environmental performance of the buildings, plants, networks and fleets they own or operate. We combine a broad base of proprietary hardware with embedded controls, software and lifecycle services. This allows us to support customers from initial system design and installation through long-term operation, maintenance, modernization and replacement.

Venzen is incorporated in the State of Delaware, and our corporate headquarters is located in Charlotte, North Carolina. We have manufacturing, engineering, distribution and service locations across North America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa, Latin America and the Asia Pacific region. We sell to customers in a large number of countries through a combination of direct sales and service personnel, independent distributors, dealers, manufacturers' representatives and original equipment manufacturer relationships.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024 ("fiscal 2024"), the Company reported revenue of $48,137 million, an increase of 8.0% compared with the fiscal year ended December 31, 2023 ("fiscal 2023"). Growth in fiscal 2024 reflected demand across our principal end markets, contributions from businesses acquired during the year, pricing actions and continued expansion of our aftermarket and services offerings. A discussion of our results of operations appears in Item 7, "Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations," of this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

Our long-term strategy rests on several priorities:

  • Extend our positions in end markets supported by durable secular trends, including energy efficiency, electrification, automation, infrastructure renewal and the decarbonization of buildings, industry and transportation.
  • Grow our installed base and the recurring aftermarket, service and software revenue associated with it.
  • Invest in research, development and engineering to sustain technology leadership and bring differentiated products to market more quickly.
  • Apply our operating system, which emphasizes lean manufacturing, continuous improvement, disciplined pricing and supply chain resilience, to improve productivity and quality across our businesses.
  • Allocate capital with discipline among organic investment, targeted acquisitions that complement our existing portfolio, and the return of capital to stockholders.

Portfolio Development

Venzen has developed its current portfolio through a combination of organic growth and acquisitions. These actions have broadened our technology base, expanded our geographic reach and deepened our presence in attractive end markets. We have also divested businesses that we concluded were not core to our long-term strategy or that we believed would be better positioned under different ownership. During fiscal 2024, we completed several acquisitions that strengthened our capabilities in building controls, industrial automation software and power conversion. Their results have been included in our consolidated financial statements from the respective dates of acquisition. We continue to evaluate the composition of our portfolio regularly. We may pursue additional acquisitions, joint ventures, strategic investments or dispositions when we believe they will enhance long-term value for our stockholders.

Business Segments

We manage our operations through three operating segments, each of which is a reportable segment. Our segments are organized around the markets they serve and the technologies they employ. This structure is consistent with the manner in which our chief operating decision maker reviews financial information, evaluates performance and allocates resources.

  • Climate and Building Technologies. This segment designs, manufactures, sells, installs and services heating, ventilation and air-conditioning ("HVAC") equipment, commercial refrigeration systems, building automation and controls, energy management software, and fire and life safety products. Its customers include owners and operators of commercial offices, healthcare facilities, educational institutions, data centers, retail and hospitality properties, government buildings and multi-family residential developments. Climate and Building Technologies also provides a broad range of technical services, including preventive maintenance, repair, retrofit, energy audits, remote monitoring and equipment replacement. These services are delivered through a network of company-operated service branches and authorized third-party service providers.
  • Industrial Automation and Flow Control. This segment designs and manufactures motion control systems, programmable logic and distributed control systems, sensors and instrumentation, industrial valves, pumps, actuators and fluid handling equipment, together with related software and services. Its products are used in process industries such as chemicals, oil and gas, water and wastewater, pharmaceuticals, food and beverage, and mining. They are also used in discrete manufacturing industries such as automotive, packaging, electronics and general machinery. The segment's offerings help customers automate production, monitor and optimize the performance of critical assets, reduce unplanned downtime and comply with safety and environmental requirements.
  • Transportation and Power Systems. This segment designs, manufactures and services power generation and distribution equipment, electrical switchgear, power conversion and energy storage systems, and engineered components and subsystems for rail, commercial vehicle, marine and aerospace applications. Its customers include electric utilities, independent power producers, transit authorities, railcar and locomotive builders, commercial vehicle manufacturers, airframers and their suppliers, and operators of large industrial and commercial facilities. Transportation and Power Systems also provides field services, spare parts, refurbishment and long-term service agreements that support the performance of equipment over extended operating lives.

Financial information about our reportable segments, including revenue and segment operating results, is included in the segment information note in the Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements in Item 8, "Financial Statements and Supplementary Data," of this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

Principal Products and Services

Our products and services span the full lifecycle of the assets our customers depend on. Our principal offerings include the following.

Equipment and systems. We manufacture a wide range of engineered equipment. This includes chillers, rooftop units, air handlers, heat pumps, variable refrigerant flow systems, commercial refrigeration cases and compressors, fire detection panels and suppression equipment, industrial valves and pumps, drives and motors, control hardware, medium- and low-voltage switchgear, transformers, inverters, battery energy storage enclosures, and propulsion, braking and thermal management components for transportation applications. Many of these products are designed to meet stringent customer specifications and industry standards. A significant portion is configured or engineered to order.

Controls and software. We provide embedded controls, building and plant management platforms, and cloud-based analytics applications. These connect our equipment, and in many cases third-party equipment, to provide visibility into system performance. Our software offerings support remote monitoring, fault detection and diagnostics, predictive maintenance, energy optimization, asset performance management and regulatory reporting. Software is increasingly delivered on a subscription basis, which we believe enhances the recurring nature of our revenue and strengthens our relationships with customers.

Aftermarket parts and services. Our large installed base generates ongoing demand for replacement parts, consumables, upgrades and services. We offer scheduled maintenance, emergency repair, inspection and testing, commissioning, retrofit and modernization, training and multi-year service agreements. We believe our service capabilities, technical expertise and proximity to customers are important competitive advantages. We also believe aftermarket activity generally provides a more stable source of revenue than new equipment sales, which tend to be more sensitive to economic and construction cycles.

Integrated solutions. For larger and more complex projects, we combine equipment, controls, software and services into integrated solutions designed to achieve specified outcomes. These outcomes may include energy savings, emissions reductions, uptime commitments or capacity expansions. In certain cases, we enter into performance-based contracts under which we guarantee specified levels of energy or operational savings over a defined period.

We serve a large and diverse customer base, ranging from small and mid-sized businesses to multinational corporations, utilities and government entities. Our principal customer groups include:

  • Commercial and institutional building owners, property managers, general and mechanical contractors, and design engineers.
  • Industrial manufacturers and process operators, engineering, procurement and construction firms, and system integrators.
  • Electric utilities, independent power producers, renewable energy developers and data center operators.
  • Original equipment manufacturers in the rail, commercial vehicle, marine and aerospace industries, together with fleet operators and transit agencies.
  • Federal, state, local and foreign government agencies, including military and defense customers.

No single customer accounted for 10% or more of our consolidated revenue in fiscal 2024 or fiscal 2023. Such contracts are generally subject to termination for convenience and to audit, and they impose specific compliance obligations. Sales, Marketing and Distribution

We go to market through multiple channels tailored to the requirements of each business, product line and geography.

Direct sales and service. Our direct sales organization includes account managers, application engineers and solution specialists. They work closely with customers, consulting engineers and specifiers to design systems that meet project requirements. In our Climate and Building Technologies segment in particular, a network of company-operated sales and service branches provides direct access to building owners and facility managers. This supports both new equipment sales and recurring service relationships.

Distributors, dealers and representatives. We sell a significant portion of our standardized products through independent wholesale distributors, dealers, contractors and manufacturers' representatives. These channel partners extend our geographic coverage, hold local inventory and provide installation and first-line support. We maintain partner programs that provide training, technical support, marketing resources and incentive arrangements to strengthen these relationships.

Original equipment manufacturers and system integrators. Many of our components and subsystems are incorporated into products manufactured by others. We work with original equipment manufacturers on long-term supply programs, often beginning early in the customer's product development cycle. System integrators also specify and install our automation and control products as part of broader industrial projects.

Digital channels. We continue to invest in digital commerce capabilities, online configuration tools and customer portals. These allow customers and partners to select products, check availability, place orders, track shipments, access technical documentation and manage service requests.

Our marketing activities include participation in industry trade shows and technical conferences, digital and print advertising, application and training centers, and thought leadership on topics such as energy efficiency, electrification and industrial productivity. Our major brands are well recognized in the markets they serve. We believe they are associated with quality, reliability and technical expertise.

Competition

The markets in which we compete are highly competitive. In most of our product lines, we face a mix of large multinational industrial conglomerates, specialized global manufacturers and numerous regional and local competitors. In certain markets, we also compete with emerging technology companies, software providers and new entrants offering digital, connected or electrified alternatives to traditional products. In our services businesses, we compete with independent service providers, mechanical and electrical contractors, and the in-house maintenance organizations of our customers.

The principal competitive factors in our markets include product performance and quality, technology and innovation, energy efficiency, price, total cost of ownership, delivery lead times, breadth of product offering, application engineering capability, service coverage and responsiveness, brand reputation, and the ability to provide integrated solutions. For large projects, competitive factors also include project management expertise, financial strength and, in some cases, the availability of financing or performance guarantees. A substantial portion of our business is awarded through competitive bidding or negotiated contracts.

We believe we are well positioned to compete because of the breadth of our portfolio, our installed base, our global manufacturing and service footprint, our engineering and software capabilities, and the strength of our brands and channel relationships. Competitive pressures, however, may affect our pricing, margins and market share. These factors are discussed in Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

These activities focus on improving the energy efficiency and environmental performance of our products, transitioning to lower global warming potential refrigerants, advancing electrification and power electronics, developing sensing, connectivity and analytics capabilities, and enhancing the cybersecurity of our connected offerings. We also collaborate with universities, research institutions, customers and suppliers on selected development programs. In addition, we maintain an investment program that evaluates early-stage technologies relevant to our markets.

Raw Materials and Supply Chain

We purchase a wide variety of raw materials, components and services from suppliers around the world. Principal raw materials include steel, aluminum, copper and other metals, plastics and resins, and refrigerants. Key purchased components include semiconductors, electronic assemblies, motors, compressors, castings, forgings and batteries. We generally source materials and components from multiple suppliers, although certain items are available only from a limited number of qualified sources. We seek to mitigate supply risk through dual sourcing, long-term supply agreements, regional manufacturing, strategic inventory positioning and supplier development programs. We also use pricing actions and, in some cases, contractual pass-through mechanisms to manage the effects of commodity price fluctuations. We believe our sources of supply are adequate for our current requirements. However, disruptions in the availability of key materials or components could adversely affect our operations, as discussed in Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

Seasonality

Our business is subject to seasonal patterns that vary by segment and geography. In our Climate and Building Technologies segment, demand for HVAC equipment and related services in the Northern Hemisphere is generally higher in the second and third calendar quarters, reflecting cooling season demand and higher construction activity during warmer months. Demand for heating products and services tends to increase in the fourth and first quarters. In our Industrial Automation and Flow Control and Transportation and Power Systems segments, revenue is influenced by the timing of customer capital spending, project completions and shipments of large engineered systems. These are often concentrated in the fourth quarter as customers seek to complete projects and use available budgets before the end of their fiscal years. Weather conditions, the timing of large orders and shifts in project schedules may cause our quarterly results to vary. Accordingly, results for any interim period are not necessarily indicative of results for the full year.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Demand for our equipment and systems depends on cyclical end markets, and a downturn in any of them could reduce our revenue and profitability.

A large portion of our revenue comes from sales of new equipment and systems. That demand depends on the level of nonresidential construction, capital spending by industrial and process customers, investment by electric utilities and independent power producers, and production volumes of rail, commercial vehicle, marine and aerospace manufacturers. These markets are cyclical and sensitive to a range of conditions, including:

  • interest rates and the availability of project financing;
  • commodity and energy prices;
  • business confidence and corporate profitability;
  • public infrastructure budgets.

Customers often defer or cancel capital projects when economic conditions weaken or financing becomes more expensive. They may also reduce discretionary maintenance and retrofit spending.

Our aftermarket and services businesses have historically been more stable than equipment sales. However, they are not immune to economic pressure, and customers facing financial stress may extend replacement cycles or reduce service scope. Because our Climate and Building Technologies, Industrial Automation and Flow Control, and Transportation and Power Systems segments are exposed to different cycles, a downturn in one end market may not be offset by strength in another. A broad economic contraction affecting several end markets at once could have a material adverse effect on our results.

Increases in the cost, or reductions in the availability, of raw materials, components and energy could reduce our margins.

Cost of revenue represents the substantial majority of our revenue. Much of that cost consists of purchased materials and components, including:

  • steel, copper and aluminum;
  • electronic components and semiconductors;
  • motors, compressors and castings;
  • refrigerants, battery cells and specialty materials used in power conversion and energy storage products.

Prices for many of these inputs are volatile and can be affected by global supply and demand, trade policy, tariffs, geopolitical events, currency movements and production disruptions. We also consume significant amounts of electricity, natural gas and fuel in our manufacturing operations and service fleet.

We seek to offset cost increases through pricing actions, productivity programs, hedging of certain commodities and supplier negotiations. These measures may not fully or promptly offset rising costs. Many of our longer-duration contracts and engineered-to-order projects are priced well before materials are purchased, which limits our ability to pass through subsequent increases. Pricing actions may also reduce demand or prompt customers to shift to competitors or lower-cost alternatives.

We operate in highly competitive markets, and failure to compete effectively could reduce our market share, pricing and profitability.

We compete with large, diversified multinational industrial companies, specialized regional manufacturers, low-cost producers in emerging economies and, increasingly, technology and software companies offering building management, automation and analytics platforms. Some competitors have greater resources in particular product lines or geographies, lower cost structures, or closer relationships with certain customers or channel partners. Competitors may also be willing to accept lower margins to gain share.

Competition is based on product performance, energy efficiency, reliability, price, delivery times, technical support, breadth of offering, installed base and service coverage. Consolidation among competitors, distributors or customers could change the competitive landscape in ways that disadvantage us. If we cannot keep pace with competitors on technology, cost or service, we could lose share or be forced to reduce prices, either of which could adversely affect our results.

Our success depends on our ability to develop and commercialize new technologies, and our investments in research, development and engineering may not produce the results we expect.

Our strategy relies on extending our positions in energy efficiency, electrification, automation and decarbonization. These markets are characterized by rapid technological change, evolving regulatory standards and shifting customer preferences. Examples include the transition to lower global-warming-potential refrigerants, the growth of heat pumps and electrified heating, increasing adoption of connected and software-defined equipment, and new approaches to grid-scale energy storage.

We invest a meaningful portion of our revenue in research and development every year, and we expect to continue doing so. Development programs are uncertain and may take longer or cost more than anticipated. New products may fail to achieve market acceptance, be eclipsed by competing technologies or face unforeseen regulatory obstacles. If we misjudge the direction or pace of technological change, or fail to bring competitive products to market in a timely manner, our existing products could become obsolete. In that event, we may be unable to recover our development investments.

Fixed-price, engineered-to-order and performance-based contracts expose us to the risk of cost overruns and unmet performance commitments.

A portion of our revenue is generated under fixed-price contracts for engineered systems, integrated solutions and installation projects. It also includes performance-based contracts under which we guarantee specified levels of energy savings, operational savings, equipment availability or system output over a defined period.

Our pricing for these contracts depends on estimates of labor, materials, subcontractor costs, schedule and the performance of equipment in the field. Actual outcomes may differ from those estimates because of:

  • design errors or unanticipated technical problems;
  • site conditions and permitting delays;
  • weather;
  • supplier or subcontractor failures;
  • changes in customer usage patterns;
  • inflation.

If costs exceed our estimates, or if guaranteed performance levels are not achieved, we may incur reduced margins or losses. We may also be required to make payments to customers or pay liquidated damages, and our reputation could suffer. Because we recognize revenue and profit on many of these contracts over time based on estimated costs to complete, changes in our estimates can cause significant cumulative adjustments in the period in which they are identified.

Our backlog and order trends may not reliably indicate future revenue or profitability.

We report and monitor backlog and order levels as indicators of future activity, particularly in our engineered equipment, power systems and transportation businesses. Orders may be canceled, delayed, reduced in scope or modified by customers, sometimes with limited compensation to us. This risk is greater during periods of economic uncertainty or when customers face financing or permitting difficulties.

Strong order intake in certain markets, such as data center infrastructure and grid modernization, may reflect temporary factors, including customer stockpiling or project pull-forward, and may not continue at the same level. Projects in backlog may also be less profitable than anticipated if costs rise before execution.

Growth in certain high-demand end markets may not be sustained, and we may not realize returns on capacity we add to serve them.

Our recent growth has benefited from strong demand in markets such as data centers, electrical grid upgrades, energy storage and building electrification. To serve these markets, we have expanded and may continue to expand manufacturing capacity, engineering resources and service coverage.

Demand in these markets could moderate because of changes in customer investment plans, technological developments that reduce power or cooling requirements, shifts in government incentives, or broader economic conditions. If demand weakens after we have added capacity, we may experience underutilized facilities, excess inventory and pricing pressure, which could adversely affect our margins and returns on invested capital.

We rely on independent distributors, dealers, contractors and representatives for a significant portion of our sales.

We sell many of our standardized products through independent wholesale distributors, dealers, contractors and manufacturers' representatives. These channel partners are generally not bound by long-term commitments to purchase from us, and many also carry competing products. Their purchasing decisions are affected by their own inventory levels, financial condition and access to credit. Destocking by distributors can cause our sales to decline more sharply than end-market demand.

Consolidation among channel partners, the loss of key distributors or dealers, or the failure of channel partners to effectively market, install and support our products could reduce our sales. It could also weaken our position in local markets and impair our ability to grow our installed base and related aftermarket revenue.

Our growth strategy for aftermarket, service and software revenue may not be successful.

We seek to grow recurring revenue from parts, services, multi-year service agreements and subscription software associated with our installed base. Success depends on attracting and retaining customers who might otherwise use independent service providers, in-house maintenance staff or competitors' platforms. It also depends on recruiting and retaining qualified field technicians and successfully developing and deploying software offerings.

Subscription software requires up-front investment, and revenue is recognized over time, which may depress near-term margins. Customers may be reluctant to adopt connected offerings because of cybersecurity, data ownership or cost concerns. If we do not grow these revenue streams as planned, our results may become more sensitive to the cyclicality of new equipment sales.

Acquisitions, joint ventures and divestitures involve risks that could adversely affect our business and results.

We have developed our portfolio in part through acquisitions. During fiscal 2024 we completed several acquisitions in building controls, industrial automation software and power conversion, and we expect to continue pursuing acquisitions and other strategic transactions.

These transactions involve risks, including:

  • difficulties integrating operations, systems, technologies, controls and personnel;
  • loss of key employees, customers or suppliers of acquired businesses;
  • diversion of management attention;
  • failure to achieve anticipated synergies, growth or cost savings;
  • assumption of unknown or contingent liabilities, including environmental, product liability, tax and compliance matters;
  • overpayment relative to the value ultimately realized.

Acquired software businesses may operate under different business models and cultures that are difficult to integrate into an industrial organization. Divestitures may result in losses, ongoing indemnification obligations, stranded costs and transition service obligations. Any of these factors could adversely affect our results and could lead to impairment of goodwill or other intangible assets.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Our global operations expose us to economic, political, trade and currency risks.

We manufacture, engineer, sell and service products across North America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa, Latin America and the Asia Pacific region. Our international operations are subject to risks that include:

  • changes in trade policies, tariffs, quotas, export controls and sanctions;
  • political instability, armed conflict and terrorism;
  • nationalization and expropriation;
  • differing labor practices and legal systems;
  • restrictions on the repatriation of earnings;
  • difficulties enforcing contracts and intellectual property rights;
  • potentially adverse tax consequences.

Escalating trade tensions among major economies could increase the cost of components we import, reduce demand for products we export, or require us to reconfigure supply chains at significant expense.

A significant portion of our revenue and costs is denominated in currencies other than the U.S. dollar. Fluctuations in exchange rates affect the reported value of our foreign revenue, earnings, assets and liabilities, and can affect the competitiveness of products manufactured in one currency and sold in another. Our hedging activities may not fully mitigate these exposures.

Supply chain disruptions, including reliance on single or limited sources of supply, could impair our ability to meet customer demand.

We depend on a global network of suppliers for raw materials, components and subassemblies. Some critical components, including certain semiconductors, power electronics, compressors, battery cells and precision castings, are available from a single or limited number of qualified suppliers. Qualifying alternative sources can take significant time, particularly for components used in safety-critical or certified applications such as aerospace, rail and fire and life safety products.

Supply disruptions could delay deliveries, increase costs, require expedited freight or redesigns, or result in lost sales and contractual penalties. Such disruptions may arise from supplier financial distress, capacity constraints, natural disasters, labor actions, logistics bottlenecks, cyberattacks, geopolitical events or trade restrictions. Building safety stock to mitigate these risks increases our inventory and working capital requirements and our exposure to obsolescence.

Cybersecurity incidents affecting our information systems or connected products could disrupt our operations, harm customers and expose us to liability.

We rely on information technology systems to run our manufacturing, engineering, supply chain, financial and customer-facing operations. Our products increasingly include embedded controls, network connectivity and cloud-based software that monitor and operate customers' buildings, industrial processes and power infrastructure. Some of these assets are part of critical infrastructure, which makes them attractive targets for sophisticated threat actors, including nation-state groups and criminal organizations using ransomware.

A successful cyberattack on our systems, our products or the systems of our suppliers and service providers could cause a range of harms, including:

  • operational disruptions;
  • theft of intellectual property or confidential data;
  • physical damage or safety incidents at customer sites;
  • regulatory investigations and fines;
  • litigation and damage to our reputation.

Our recent acquisitions of software and controls businesses expand the systems and products we must secure and integrate. Our security measures may not prevent every incident, and insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

Product defects, safety incidents, warranty claims and recalls could result in significant costs and reputational harm.

Many of our products perform critical functions, including:

  • fire detection and suppression;
  • refrigeration of food and pharmaceuticals;
  • control of hazardous industrial processes;
  • distribution of electrical power;
  • braking, propulsion and thermal management in transportation applications.

A defect or failure in any of these products could cause property damage, personal injury, loss of life, environmental harm or significant business interruption for customers.

We are subject to product liability claims, warranty obligations and, in some cases, mandatory recalls. Our warranty reserves are based on historical experience and engineering estimates, and actual costs could exceed them, particularly for new products or technologies with limited field history. Energy storage and power conversion products may present fire and thermal risks. Claims and recalls could result in significant costs, divert management attention, damage customer relationships and harm our reputation.

Our success depends on attracting and retaining skilled employees, and labor shortages or disputes could disrupt our operations.

We employ approximately 72,500 people, many of whom are skilled manufacturing workers, field service technicians, application engineers and software developers. Competition for these workers is intense in many of our markets, and shortages of qualified HVAC, electrical and controls technicians have constrained, and may continue to constrain, our ability to grow our service business and execute projects. Rising wages and benefit costs increase our cost structure.

Certain of our employees are represented by labor unions or works councils, particularly outside the United States. We may be unable to negotiate acceptable collective bargaining agreements, and strikes, work stoppages or other labor actions could disrupt production or service delivery. Labor actions at suppliers, customers or logistics providers could also adversely affect us.

Disruptions at our manufacturing facilities, including those caused by natural disasters and climate-related events, could adversely affect our ability to serve customers.

We operate a large network of manufacturing, distribution and service facilities, and we carry a substantial base of property, plant and equipment. Some products are manufactured at a single facility or depend on specialized equipment that would be difficult to replace quickly. Events that could disrupt operations at one or more facilities include fires, explosions, equipment failures, severe weather, flooding, earthquakes, extended utility outages, pandemics and civil unrest.

The physical effects of climate change, including more frequent or intense storms, heat waves, droughts and wildfires, may increase the likelihood and severity of these disruptions, both at our facilities and throughout our supply chain. Our insurance coverage may be insufficient to cover all losses, including lost profits.

Our operating system, productivity programs and restructuring actions may not deliver the expected benefits.

We rely on our operating system, including lean manufacturing, continuous improvement, disciplined pricing and supply chain management, to improve productivity and offset cost inflation. From time to time we also undertake restructuring actions, including facility consolidations, footprint optimization and workforce reductions.

These initiatives may cost more, take longer or produce smaller savings than planned. They may disrupt operations, impair customer service or affect employee morale and retention. If we fail to achieve expected productivity gains, our margins could decline, particularly in periods when we cannot fully recover cost increases through pricing.

Implementation of new enterprise resource planning and other business systems could disrupt our operations.

We are upgrading and consolidating several of our enterprise resource planning, order management and financial systems, including systems of acquired businesses. These implementations are complex, costly and time-consuming. Implementation problems could disrupt order processing, manufacturing, invoicing, collections and financial reporting, and could affect the effectiveness of our internal control over financial reporting. Delays or cost overruns could also divert resources from other priorities.

Risks Related to Financing and Our Capital Structure

Our indebtedness and other obligations could limit our flexibility and adversely affect our financial condition.

Our long-term debt increased during fiscal 2024, and interest expense rose compared with fiscal 2023. Our indebtedness requires us to dedicate a portion of our cash flow to interest and principal payments, which reduces the funds available for capital expenditures, research and development, acquisitions and returns to stockholders.

Our debt agreements contain covenants that may restrict certain activities, and a failure to comply with them could result in acceleration of amounts outstanding. We may incur additional indebtedness to finance acquisitions or for other purposes, which would increase these risks. Higher interest rates would increase the cost of refinancing maturing debt and of any variable-rate borrowings.

Our current liabilities exceed our current assets, and we depend on continued access to operating cash flow and credit markets to meet short-term obligations.

As of the end of fiscal 2024, our total current liabilities exceeded our total current assets, and the gap widened compared with the prior year end. We manage our working capital to fund operations, investments, dividends and debt service. We rely on cash generated from operations, our cash balances and access to commercial paper, credit facilities and debt capital markets to meet obligations as they come due.

A decline in operating cash flow could make it more difficult or more costly to meet short-term obligations. Such a decline could result from weaker demand, lower margins, slower customer collections, increased inventory or changes in supplier payment terms. A disruption in credit markets, a tightening of lending conditions or a downgrade of our credit ratings could have the same effect.

A downgrade in our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to capital.

Our credit ratings affect the cost and availability of our borrowings, including commercial paper. They also affect the terms on which suppliers, customers, surety providers and other counterparties are willing to do business with us. Rating agencies may lower our ratings for several reasons, including increased leverage, acquisitions financed with debt, sustained returns of capital to stockholders, weaker operating performance or adverse industry conditions.

A downgrade could increase our interest costs and restrict our access to certain markets. It could also require us to post collateral or letters of credit, or reduce our ability to obtain performance bonds needed for project work.

We have a substantial amount of goodwill and other intangible assets, and an impairment could significantly reduce our reported earnings and stockholders' equity.

Goodwill represents a significant portion of our total assets and a substantial majority of our total stockholders' equity, and it increased during fiscal 2024 as a result of acquisitions. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually, and more often if events or circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. Our assessments rely on assumptions about future revenue growth, margins, discount rates and market conditions.

Several developments could require us to record a non-cash impairment charge, including:

  • adverse changes in economic conditions or end-market demand;
  • underperformance of acquired businesses;
  • increases in interest rates and discount rates;
  • declines in our market capitalization.

Such a charge could be material to our results of operations and would reduce our stockholders' equity.

Our significant long-term liabilities could require future cash outlays that exceed our expectations.

In addition to long-term debt, we carry substantial other non-current liabilities. These can include obligations for pension and other postretirement benefits, deferred taxes, long-term warranty and product liability reserves, environmental remediation, deferred revenue on multi-year service agreements and other contingencies. The amounts recorded for these obligations depend on actuarial, legal and engineering assumptions, including discount rates, investment returns, mortality, claims experience and remediation costs.

Changes in these assumptions, adverse market performance of plan assets, new claims or changes in law could increase our liabilities. Any of these developments could require additional cash contributions or payments that reduce funds available for other purposes.

We may not continue to pay dividends or return capital to stockholders at current levels.

We have historically paid regular cash dividends, which represent a significant use of our operating cash flow. Any future dividends or share repurchases will depend on our results of operations, cash flows, financial condition, capital requirements, debt obligations, acquisition opportunities and other factors our Board of Directors considers relevant. Our Board of Directors may reduce, suspend or discontinue dividends or repurchases at any time. Any reduction or suspension could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

Our capital-intensive asset base requires continuing investment, and inadequate investment could impair our competitiveness.

We operate a large base of property, plant and equipment across many facilities. Maintaining, modernizing and expanding these assets requires ongoing capital expenditures. If we underinvest relative to the needs of our asset base or the requirements of growing markets, we may face lower productivity, higher maintenance costs, quality problems or insufficient capacity. If we invest in capacity that is not fully utilized, our returns on invested capital and our margins could decline.

Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Overview

Venzen relies on information technology systems, operational technology and connected products to design, manufacture, sell and service our offerings. These systems also support our internal operations. Our controls and software, including remote monitoring and subscription-based analytics, are increasingly linked to customer buildings, plants, networks and fleets. As a result, cybersecurity is a core element of our enterprise risk management. Our program aims to protect the confidentiality, integrity and availability of our systems and data. It also aims to protect the security of the products and services we deliver to customers.

Risk Management and Strategy

Our cybersecurity program is governed by a set of enterprise policies and standards. These are aligned with recognized industry frameworks, including the cybersecurity framework published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and with standards applicable to industrial automation and control systems. The program is organized around the core functions of identifying, protecting against, detecting, responding to and recovering from cybersecurity threats. Its principal elements include the following:

  • Continuous security monitoring through a centralized security operations function. This function aggregates and analyzes activity across our corporate networks, cloud environments and endpoints, using automated tools supplemented by analysts who investigate potential threats.
  • Identity and access management controls designed to restrict access to systems and data based on role and need. These include multifactor authentication and privileged access safeguards.
  • Vulnerability management practices, including periodic scanning, prioritized remediation and penetration testing of critical systems and applications.
  • A dedicated operational technology security program. It addresses the specific risks of our manufacturing facilities, test laboratories and service operations, including segmentation of plant networks from enterprise systems.
  • A product security program that applies secure-by-design principles throughout the development lifecycle of our embedded controls, building and plant management platforms, and cloud-based software. It includes threat modeling, code review, security testing and a coordinated process for receiving and addressing vulnerability reports from customers and researchers.
  • Collection and evaluation of threat intelligence from internal sources, industry information-sharing organizations, government agencies and commercial providers, to identify threats relevant to our industry and operations.
  • Security awareness training for employees, with additional role-based training for personnel in higher-risk functions. Simulated phishing exercises reinforce this training.

Incident Response and Recovery

We maintain a written cybersecurity incident response plan. It defines roles, escalation paths and procedures for evaluating, containing, remediating and communicating about incidents. Under the plan, incidents are assessed for severity and potential business impact. Incidents that meet defined thresholds are escalated promptly to senior management. A cross-functional group that includes legal, finance, communications and business leadership evaluates whether an incident may be material and whether disclosure or notification obligations apply. We conduct tabletop exercises periodically to test the plan, and some of these exercises involve senior executives. Our business continuity and disaster recovery programs include recovery planning and testing for systems that support manufacturing, order fulfillment, field service and financial reporting.

Third-Party Risk

We engage independent assessors, consultants and auditors to evaluate the maturity and effectiveness of our cybersecurity program, test our controls and support incident response when needed. Management reviews their findings and tracks remediation actions through completion.

We depend on many suppliers, service providers, channel partners and software vendors, some of which host or have access to our data and systems. Our third-party risk management process evaluates the security practices of these parties before engagement and periodically afterward, scaled to the sensitivity of the data or access involved. Contracts with relevant providers generally include security and incident notification requirements. Businesses we acquire are assessed for cybersecurity risk during due diligence. We then integrate them into our security standards and monitoring over a planned transition period.

Some of our contracts with government entities, including defense customers, impose specific cybersecurity and data protection requirements. We maintain processes designed to comply with those obligations.

Assessment of Risks from Cybersecurity Threats

Cybersecurity risks are incorporated into our enterprise risk management process. Through that process, management identifies, evaluates and prioritizes risks that could affect our strategic and operating objectives. Like other companies of our size and complexity, we face threats such as ransomware, phishing, credential theft, malicious code, denial-of-service attacks and intrusions by sophisticated actors, including actors that may be state-sponsored. We also face risks arising from vulnerabilities in our suppliers' systems and in the connected products we sell.

We have experienced cybersecurity incidents in the past. As of the date of this report, however, we are not aware of any cybersecurity threats or incidents, including those resulting from previous incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect the Company, including our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. We cannot provide assurance that we will not experience such an incident in the future. For further discussion, see Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

Governance

Board Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees our enterprise risks, including cybersecurity risks. The Board has delegated primary oversight of cybersecurity and information security risk to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives regular reports from the Chief Information Security Officer on the threat environment, program performance, assessment results, significant incidents and the progress of key initiatives. The Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full Board, which also receives periodic cybersecurity briefings directly from management. Under our incident response plan, management notifies the Audit Committee chair, and the full Board as appropriate, of incidents that meet defined escalation criteria.

Management's Role

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads our enterprise cybersecurity organization. This organization is responsible for information technology, operational technology and product security strategy, as well as security operations and incident response. The Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience leading cybersecurity programs at large, multinational industrial and technology organizations, with a background in security architecture, threat management and operational technology protection. The individual holds recognized professional certifications in information security. The Chief Information Security Officer reports to our Chief Information Officer, who reports to our Chief Executive Officer. Security leaders embedded within each of our three segments support the Chief Information Security Officer and work with business, engineering and plant leadership to integrate security requirements into operations and product development.

We also maintain a Cybersecurity Risk Council, a cross-functional management committee chaired by the Chief Information Officer. Its members include senior leaders from legal, finance, internal audit, compliance, operations and each of our segments. The Council reviews cybersecurity risks, sets program priorities, monitors remediation of significant findings and helps ensure that cybersecurity considerations inform strategic planning, budgeting and investment decisions across the Company.

Overview

  • Climate and Building Technologies;
  • Industrial Automation and Flow Control;

Our revenue comes from new equipment and systems, controls and software, aftermarket parts and services, and integrated solutions. Many of these are delivered under multi-year agreements.

  • cash generation;

Results of Operations

The following discussion compares our consolidated results for fiscal 2024 with fiscal 2023. Information about the revenue and operating results of our reportable segments is included in the segment information note to the consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

Revenue

Revenue increased 8.0% to $48,137 million in fiscal 2024 from $44,567 million in fiscal 2023. The increase reflected higher volumes, favorable pricing and the contribution of acquired businesses.

By segment, the principal drivers were as follows:

  • In Climate and Building Technologies, demand remained firm for HVAC equipment, heat pumps and building automation in data centers, healthcare, education and other institutional buildings. Aftermarket service, retrofit and replacement activity continued to grow. These gains more than offset softer conditions in certain commercial office and new residential construction markets. Our transition of product lines to lower global-warming-potential refrigerants also supported replacement demand.
  • In Industrial Automation and Flow Control, revenue benefited from process industry investment, particularly in water and wastewater, pharmaceuticals and energy-related applications, and from software and services growth. Demand in some discrete manufacturing markets was more muted as customers moderated capital spending, and distributors in certain regions reduced inventory levels.
  • In Transportation and Power Systems, revenue grew on strong demand for electrical switchgear, transformers, power conversion and energy storage systems. Utilities, independent power producers and data center operators continued to invest in grid capacity and reliability. Rail and aerospace activity also improved. Field services and long-term service agreements contributed to growth as our installed base expanded.

Pricing contributed favorably across all three segments. It reflected actions taken during fiscal 2023 and fiscal 2024 to offset higher costs for materials, components, freight and labor. Revenue from businesses acquired during fiscal 2024 has been included in our results from the respective dates of acquisition. Foreign currency movements did not have a significant effect on the comparison.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $30,909 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $28,726 million in fiscal 2023. Gross profit increased to $17,228 million from $15,841 million. Gross margin was 35.8% in fiscal 2024, compared with 35.5% in fiscal 2023.

Cost of revenue rose principally because of higher sales volumes and the inclusion of acquired businesses. Cost inflation in certain inputs also contributed, including:

  • electrical steel and copper used in power distribution equipment;
  • semiconductors and electronic components used in controls and drives;
  • skilled labor in manufacturing and field service.

Depreciation associated with new and expanded manufacturing capacity was another factor.

The improvement in gross margin primarily reflected three things. First, pricing more than offset net cost inflation. Second, aftermarket, service and software revenue, which generally carries higher margins than new equipment, made up a larger share of the mix. Third, productivity gains came from lean manufacturing, sourcing initiatives and footprint optimization under our operating system.

Several factors partially offset these benefits. Start-up costs at facilities added to serve high-demand markets weighed on margin, as did inventory step-up and other purchase accounting effects from acquisitions. Unfavorable margin performance on a limited number of fixed-price projects was a further offset.

Research and Development

Research and development expense was $2,036 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,912 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, research and development expense was 4.2% in fiscal 2024, compared with 4.3% in fiscal 2023.

Spending rose as we continued to invest in the following areas:

  • heat pump and electrified heating platforms;
  • refrigerant transition programs;
  • connected controls and subscription software;
  • industrial automation and asset performance management applications;
  • power conversion and energy storage technologies.

Engineering teams from acquired businesses also added to expense. The modest decline as a share of revenue reflected revenue growth rather than any reduction in our commitment to innovation. We expect to continue investing a meaningful portion of revenue in research and development.

Selling, General and Administrative

Selling, general and administrative expense was $6,292 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $5,812 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, it was 13.1% in fiscal 2024, compared with 13.0% in fiscal 2023.

The increase reflected higher sales commissions and variable compensation consistent with higher revenue and improved performance. It also reflected continued expansion of our direct sales and service branch network, investments in digital commerce and customer-facing software tools, and the administrative cost structures of acquired businesses. Wage inflation and investments in information technology and cybersecurity further increased expense.

These increases were partially offset by productivity actions and continued consolidation of shared services functions. The slight increase as a share of revenue was largely attributable to acquired businesses that have not yet been fully integrated into our shared services model.

Other Operating Expense

Other operating expense was $2,888 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $2,674 million in fiscal 2023. This line includes:

  • amortization of acquired intangible assets;
  • restructuring and related costs;
  • acquisition and integration costs;
  • certain litigation and environmental charges;
  • other operating items.

The increase primarily reflected higher amortization of intangible assets recognized in fiscal 2024 acquisitions, together with transaction and integration costs for those acquisitions. These were partially offset by lower restructuring costs as several multi-year footprint programs approached completion.

Operating Income

Operating income increased 10.5% to $6,012 million in fiscal 2024 from $5,443 million in fiscal 2023. Operating margin was 12.5%, compared with 12.2%.

Operating income grew faster than revenue for three main reasons:

  • higher gross margin;
  • operating leverage on research and development expense;
  • disciplined management of selling, general and administrative costs.

Higher other operating expense related to acquisitions partially offset these effects.

Interest Expense

Interest expense was $420 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $367 million in fiscal 2023. The increase reflected a higher average level of borrowings, principally debt issued to help fund acquisitions completed during the year. Long-term debt increased to $9,336 million at December 31, 2024 from $8,148 million at December 31, 2023. Higher prevailing interest rates on new and refinanced borrowings also contributed.

Provision for Income Taxes and Effective Tax Rate

Income before income taxes was $5,592 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $5,076 million in fiscal 2023. The provision for income taxes was $1,186 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,142 million in fiscal 2023. Our effective tax rate was 21.2% in fiscal 2024, compared with 22.5% in fiscal 2023.

The lower rate primarily reflected a more favorable geographic mix of earnings. It also reflected increased benefits from research and development tax credits and energy-related tax incentives, and the favorable resolution of certain prior-year tax matters. Nondeductible acquisition-related costs and the effects of global minimum tax regimes enacted in certain jurisdictions where we operate partially offset these benefits.

Our future effective tax rate may vary because of several factors:

  • changes in the jurisdictional mix of earnings;
  • changes in tax laws, including continued implementation of global minimum tax rules;
  • the outcome of tax examinations;
  • discrete items.

Net Income

Net income increased 12.0% to $4,406 million in fiscal 2024 from $3,934 million in fiscal 2023. Net margin improved to 9.2% from 8.8%. The improvement reflected higher operating income and a lower effective tax rate, partially offset by higher interest expense.

Critical Accounting Estimates

Preparing financial statements in accordance with U.S. generally accepted accounting principles requires management to make estimates and assumptions. These affect reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses, and the disclosure of contingencies. We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions and other assumptions we believe are reasonable. Actual results may differ.

The estimates described below involve significant judgment or uncertainty and could have a material effect on our financial condition or results of operations. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to the consolidated financial statements in Item 8. Management has discussed these estimates with the Audit Committee of the Board of Directors.

Revenue Recognition on Long-Term Contracts

A portion of our revenue comes from engineered-to-order systems, integrated solutions, installation projects and long-term service agreements. For these contracts, revenue is recognized over time as control transfers to the customer. We generally measure progress using costs incurred relative to total estimated costs at completion.

This method requires estimates of total contract revenue and costs, including:

  • labor productivity;
  • material and subcontractor costs;
  • schedule;
  • variable consideration such as incentives, penalties and liquidated damages;
  • the likelihood of change orders and claims.

For performance-based contracts, we also estimate the expected level of energy or operational savings and any obligation to compensate customers for shortfalls.

We review estimates regularly and recognize changes on a cumulative catch-up basis in the period identified. When estimated costs exceed estimated revenue, we record the full anticipated loss immediately. Because of the number and complexity of these contracts, changes in estimates can significantly affect results in a particular period, as discussed in Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

Business Combinations

We account for acquisitions using the acquisition method. Assets acquired and liabilities assumed are recorded at their estimated fair values at the acquisition date, and any excess of the purchase price is recorded as goodwill.

Determining the fair value of identifiable intangible assets requires significant judgment. These assets include customer relationships, developed technology and trade names. Key assumptions include:

  • projected revenue growth and margins;
  • customer attrition rates;
  • royalty rates;
  • discount rates.

These assumptions also affect the useful lives over which finite-lived intangible assets are amortized, and therefore future other operating expense. Given the acquisitions completed in fiscal 2024, these estimates were especially significant this year.

Goodwill

Goodwill was $19,058 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level annually, and more often if events or circumstances indicate a reporting unit's fair value may have fallen below its carrying amount. We may first assess qualitative factors. When performing a quantitative test, we estimate fair value using discounted cash flow models, supported by market-based valuation approaches.

The key assumptions are:

  • forecasted revenue growth;
  • operating margins;
  • capital requirements;
  • terminal growth rates;
  • discount rates reflecting the risks of each reporting unit.

Our annual test in fiscal 2024 did not result in an impairment. Recently acquired reporting units generally have fair values closer to their carrying amounts, because their assets were recently measured at fair value. Adverse changes in market conditions, performance against forecasts or discount rates could lead to future impairment charges.

Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Determining our provision for income taxes requires judgment in several areas:

  • interpreting complex tax laws;
  • allocating income among jurisdictions;
  • assessing the realizability of deferred tax assets;
  • evaluating uncertain tax positions.

We record a valuation allowance when it is more likely than not that some portion of a deferred tax asset will not be realized. That assessment considers projected taxable income, the reversal of temporary differences and available tax planning strategies.

We recognize the benefit of an uncertain tax position only when it is more likely than not to be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. Changes in tax law, the outcome of audits or changes in our assessment could materially affect our effective tax rate in future periods.

Pension and Other Postretirement Benefits

We sponsor defined benefit pension and other postretirement benefit plans in the United States and several other countries. The related obligations are included in other non-current liabilities. Measuring these obligations and the related expense requires assumptions about discount rates, expected long-term returns on plan assets, rates of compensation increase, mortality and health care cost trends.

Discount rates are based on yields of high-quality corporate bonds with durations matching expected benefit payments. Expected returns reflect our target asset allocation and long-term capital market expectations. Differences between actual experience and our assumptions, or changes in assumptions, may materially affect the funded status of the plans, future expense and required contributions.

Product Warranties, Litigation and Environmental Matters

We provide warranties on many of our products and record estimated warranty costs when revenue is recognized. These estimates are based on historical claims experience, product failure rates and the cost of repairs or replacements. They also reflect specific known issues, and new product introductions may involve less claims history.

We are also subject to legal proceedings, product liability claims and environmental remediation obligations arising in the ordinary course of business. We record a liability when a loss is probable and reasonably estimable. Estimating these liabilities requires judgment about the outcome of proceedings, the scope of remediation, the allocation of costs among responsible parties and potential insurance recoveries. Actual outcomes could differ from our estimates.
