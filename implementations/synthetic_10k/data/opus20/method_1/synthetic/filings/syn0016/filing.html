PART I

Item 1. Business

General

Zenaltal Foods, Inc. (together with its consolidated subsidiaries, "Zenaltal," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") manufactures and markets branded and private label packaged foods. Our products are sold primarily in the United States and in selected international markets. We were incorporated in the State of Delaware, and our corporate headquarters are located in Princeton, New Jersey. Our common stock trades under the ticker symbol "ZTLN."

Our portfolio covers several categories that consumers buy regularly: shelf-stable meals, soups and broths, sauces, condiments, baking mixes, savory snacks, crackers, cookies and frozen bakery items. We also supply ingredient-based and bulk-format products to restaurants, institutions and other food manufacturers. We believe our brands hold well-recognized positions in their categories. Many of our product lines have been sold under the same trademarks for several generations, and consumer familiarity and trust in those trademarks are a foundation of our business.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024 ("fiscal 2024"), our total revenue was $15,185 million, a decrease of 6.7% compared with the fiscal year ended December 31, 2023 ("fiscal 2023"). The decrease mainly reflected:
- lower volumes in certain categories as consumers responded to cumulative food price inflation by trading down or reducing purchases;
- the exit from or rationalization of certain lower-margin product lines; and
- portfolio actions completed during the year.

We continue to focus on improving the profitability and resilience of our core business rather than pursuing volume at any cost. In the discussion that follows, references to a fiscal year refer to the twelve months ended December 31 of that year.

Our Strategy

Our strategy rests on four related priorities.

First, we aim to strengthen our core brands. We direct most of our marketing support, product renovation and in-store execution toward the brands and categories where we hold leading positions and believe we can earn attractive returns over time. We regularly review the role of each brand and stock-keeping unit ("SKU") in our portfolio. Where a product line does not meet our expectations for growth, margin or strategic fit, we may reduce its distribution, reformulate it, or divest or discontinue it.

Second, we aim to improve the productivity of our cost structure. We maintain multi-year programs to lower the cost of goods sold through procurement savings, network optimization, automation and waste reduction. We also seek to manage selling, general and administrative expenses with discipline. In recent years we have consolidated certain manufacturing and distribution operations, simplified our organizational structure and moved a number of transactional processes to shared service centers.

Third, we aim to be a preferred supplier to our retail and foodservice customers. We invest in category management, supply chain reliability and data-sharing capabilities to help customers grow their categories and manage their inventories efficiently. We believe consistent service levels and fill rates are increasingly important to how customers allocate shelf space and promotional support.

Fourth, we aim to allocate capital with discipline. We seek to balance:
- reinvestment in our manufacturing network and brands;
- a sustainable dividend;
- repurchases of our common stock; and
- a capital structure that preserves our financial flexibility.

During fiscal 2024, we returned capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases and reduced our outstanding long-term debt.

Principal Products and Services

We organize our product offerings around the following principal categories.

Meals and pantry staples. This category includes ready-to-serve and condensed soups, broths and stocks, shelf-stable entrees, pasta sauces, cooking sauces, gravies, canned vegetables and beans, and side dishes. These products are positioned as convenient, affordable meal solutions for families and individuals. Many of them are also used as recipe ingredients in home cooking.

Snacks and bakery. This category includes savory snacks such as crackers, pretzels, popcorn and baked chips, as well as sweet baked goods such as cookies, snack cakes and frozen bakery items. We sell these products in a range of formats, including multipacks, single-serve packages, family-size bags and club-store configurations, to address different consumption occasions and price points.

Condiments, dressings and baking. This category includes spreads, salad dressings, dips, condiments, baking mixes, frostings and related products. These products are sold primarily through retail grocery and mass merchandise channels and, in larger formats, to foodservice operators.

Foodservice and ingredient products. We supply bulk and portion-control formats of many of our retail products to restaurants, contract feeders, schools, health care facilities and other institutional operators. We also sell certain ingredients and semi-finished products to other food manufacturers. In addition, we manufacture a limited amount of private label product for retail customers, mainly in categories where we have available manufacturing capacity and a cost-competitive production base.

Across these categories, we renovate our products periodically to respond to changing consumer preferences. Recent renovation priorities include reduced sodium and sugar, simpler ingredient statements, higher protein content, plant-based offerings and packaging that is more convenient or more recyclable. Our research and development activities support these efforts and are conducted primarily at our technical center in New Jersey and at selected manufacturing locations. Research and development spending is modest relative to the size of our business. It concentrates on product formulation, process improvement, packaging development, food safety and quality assurance rather than on fundamental scientific research.

Reportable Segments

We manage our business through three operating segments, each of which is a reportable segment: North America Retail, Snacks and Bakery, and Foodservice and International. We define our segments based on how our chief operating decision maker reviews operating results, assesses performance and allocates resources. Certain corporate expenses, including those related to corporate headquarters functions, certain restructuring activities and other items not attributable to the ongoing operations of a particular segment, are not allocated to the segments. These amounts are reported separately as corporate and unallocated items. Financial information about our reportable segments is set forth in Part II, Item 8 of this Annual Report on Form 10-K in the notes to our consolidated financial statements.

North America Retail

The North America Retail segment manufactures and markets our meals and pantry staples, condiments, dressings and baking products to retail customers in the United States and Canada. The segment's products are sold mainly through:
- traditional grocery chains;
- mass merchandisers and supercenters;
- warehouse clubs;
- drug and dollar stores; and
- e-commerce retailers and the online platforms of traditional retailers.

The segment holds leading or strong positions in several shelf-stable categories, and its brands are among the most widely distributed in our portfolio.

The segment's results are influenced by several factors:
- the level of consumer spending on food consumed at home;
- the relative price gap between branded and private label products;
- the effectiveness of our trade promotion programs; and
- the cost of key inputs, including vegetables, tomatoes, grains, dairy ingredients, edible oils, proteins and steel and aluminum used in cans.

The segment operates a network of company-owned manufacturing facilities and uses third-party co-manufacturers for certain products. Distribution to customers is handled largely through company-operated and third-party warehouses.

Snacks and Bakery

The Snacks and Bakery segment manufactures and markets savory snacks, crackers, cookies, snack cakes and frozen bakery items, mainly in the United States. Its products reach consumers through grocery, mass merchandise, club, convenience, drug and dollar store channels, as well as e-commerce. Depending on the product line and geography, products are delivered either through customer warehouses or through a combination of direct store delivery arrangements and independent distributors.

Snacking categories are generally characterized by:
- frequent product innovation;
- a higher share of impulse purchases;
- greater sensitivity to in-store merchandising and display; and
- a broad range of competitors, including large multinational food companies, regional producers and smaller emerging brands.

Key inputs for the segment include flour and other grains, sugar, edible oils, cocoa, nuts, dairy ingredients, potatoes and flexible packaging materials. The segment operates dedicated bakeries and snack manufacturing facilities and uses co-manufacturers to supplement its internal capacity.

Foodservice and International

The Foodservice and International segment includes:
- our sales of bulk and portion-control products to foodservice distributors and operators in the United States;
- our ingredient sales to other food manufacturers; and
- our operations outside North America.

In foodservice, our products are sold primarily through broadline and specialty foodservice distributors. They are ultimately used by restaurants, hotels, schools, colleges and universities, hospitals, long-term care facilities, government institutions and contract feeders. Demand in this channel depends on trends in away-from-home dining, institutional food budgets and menu decisions by operators.

Outside North America, we sell selected brands and product lines in markets in Latin America, Europe and the Asia-Pacific region. We do so through local subsidiaries, licensees and third-party distributors. Our international business is smaller than our domestic operations and faces risks associated with foreign currency fluctuations, local economic conditions and differing regulatory requirements. During fiscal 2024, we continued to evaluate the composition of our international portfolio and took steps to simplify our presence in certain markets.

Customers

We sell our products primarily to retailers, foodservice distributors and other customers who resell them to consumers or to foodservice operators. Our retail customers include:
- national and regional grocery chains;
- mass merchandisers and supercenters;
- warehouse clubs;
- drug and dollar store chains;
- convenience store operators;
- military commissaries; and
- e-commerce retailers.

Our foodservice customers include broadline and specialty distributors and, in some cases, national restaurant chains and institutional operators with whom we contract directly.

As in the broader packaged food industry, our customer base has consolidated over time. A relatively small number of large retail and foodservice customers accounts for a significant share of our revenue. Our largest customer is a mass merchandiser that operates supercenters and warehouse clubs throughout the United States. The loss of, or a significant reduction in purchases by, this customer or any of our other largest customers could have a material adverse effect on our business, financial condition and results of operations.

Consolidation has also increased the purchasing power of large customers. Many of them place growing emphasis on their own private label programs, and several now compete directly with our branded products in many of the categories in which we operate. We seek to sustain strong relationships with our major customers through joint business planning, category management support, supply chain collaboration and data-driven promotional programs. We generally do not have long-term supply contracts with our retail customers. Sales are typically made on the basis of purchase orders placed under customary trade terms.

Sales and Marketing

We market our products through a combination of our own sales organization, independent food brokers and, for certain products and channels, independent distributors.

Our sales teams are organized by channel and by customer. Dedicated customer teams serve our largest retail accounts, while broker networks support coverage of smaller and regional customers. In foodservice, our sales personnel work with distributors and with operators to secure menu placements and to encourage distributors to stock our products.

Our marketing activities are designed to build brand awareness, encourage trial and support repeat purchases. They include:
- advertising across television, streaming video, digital, social media and audio channels;
- in-store displays and merchandising;
- consumer promotions such as coupons and digital offers; and
- sponsorships and public relations.

Over the past several years we have shifted a larger portion of our advertising toward digital and data-driven media. This shift allows us to target specific consumer segments and to measure the return on our investments more precisely.

We also spend significant amounts on trade promotion. Trade promotion includes temporary price reductions, feature advertising in retailer circulars, display fees, slotting fees for new products and other payments to customers to support the sale of our products. These programs are an important part of how we compete for shelf space and consumer purchases. In accordance with applicable accounting standards, we generally record them as reductions of revenue. We continually evaluate the effectiveness of our trade promotion spending and seek to reallocate resources toward programs that generate the greatest incremental volume and profitability.

E-commerce has become an increasingly important channel for the sale of our products. It includes sales through the online platforms of traditional retailers, pure-play online retailers and click-and-collect services. We work with our customers to optimize product content, digital shelf presence and pack formats suited to online purchasing.

Competition

The packaged food industry is highly competitive. We compete with:
- large multinational food and beverage companies with broad product portfolios and significant resources;
- regional and national food manufacturers that focus on particular categories;
- smaller emerging brands, many of which target specific consumer trends such as organic, plant-based or premium positioning; and
- retailers' private label products, which are typically sold at lower prices than branded products.

Competition is based primarily on brand recognition and loyalty, product quality and taste, price, value, product innovation, packaging, promotional activity, shelf space and in-store presence, customer service and supply reliability. Some of our competitors have greater financial, marketing or distribution resources than we do. Some are more diversified across categories or geographies, and some benefit from lower cost structures.

The growth of private label products, particularly during periods of elevated food price inflation, has intensified competitive pressure in several of our categories. Many of our retail customers, including several of our largest, offer private label products that compete directly with ours and that they may favor in shelf placement or promotion. In addition, low barriers to entry in some categories, together with the reach of digital marketing and online retail, have made it easier for smaller brands to win consumer attention and distribution.

We believe our established brands, broad distribution, scale in manufacturing and procurement, and long-standing customer relationships position us to compete effectively. Maintaining our competitive position will nonetheless require continued investment in our brands, product innovation and cost productivity.

- corn and corn-derived ingredients;
- sugar and sweeteners;
- edible oils;
- dairy products;

To manage input cost volatility, we use a combination of long-term supply agreements, fixed-price purchase commitments and, where appropriate, derivative instruments such as commodity futures, options and swaps. We may also pursue pricing actions, product reformulation, package size changes and productivity initiatives to mitigate cost increases. We may not be able to fully offset higher input costs, however, and pricing actions may reduce consumer demand for our products.

Manufacturing and Distribution

We manufacture most of our products at company-owned or leased facilities located primarily in the United States, with additional facilities in Canada and in selected international markets. We supplement our internal production with third-party co-manufacturers and co-packers, particularly for products that require specialized processes or that serve smaller-volume markets.

Our products are distributed to customers through a network of company-operated and third-party distribution centers. They move mainly by truck and, to a lesser extent, by rail and intermodal transportation.

We regularly assess the efficiency and capacity of our manufacturing and distribution network. In recent years we have consolidated production into fewer, higher-volume facilities, invested in automation and upgraded packaging lines. These actions have resulted in, and may in the future result in, plant closures, asset impairments and restructuring charges. We believe our facilities are generally well maintained and adequate for our current needs.

Seasonality

Demand for certain of our products is seasonal:
- Soups, broths, baking mixes, frostings and certain other meal and pantry products typically sell in higher volumes during the fall and winter months, particularly around the year-end holiday season.
- Certain condiments, dressings, dips and snack products tend to sell in higher volumes during the spring and summer months and around major sporting and holiday events.

As a result of these patterns, our revenue, working capital requirements and cash flows fluctuate from quarter to quarter. Inventory levels generally increase during the third quarter in anticipation of the higher-demand fall and winter season. Receivables typically peak in the fourth quarter. The timing of retailer promotional events, customer inventory management practices and the timing of holidays may also affect quarterly results. Because of the breadth of our portfolio, seasonal effects in one category are partially offset by opposite patterns in others.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Continued pressure on consumer spending for food at home could further reduce our sales volumes and revenue.

Our revenue declined in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023. A principal reason was lower volume in several categories, as consumers responded to several years of cumulative food price inflation by buying fewer items, switching to lower-priced alternatives or shifting purchases to value-oriented retail formats. Many of our products are everyday staples, but consumers can readily substitute among brands, pack sizes and price tiers. They may also defer purchases of discretionary items such as snacks and sweet baked goods. If household budgets remain constrained, or if inflation in housing, energy, health care or other essential costs continues to compete with food for consumer spending, volumes in our categories could decline further.

We may not be able to restore volume growth through pricing, promotion or innovation. Price increases that we implement to offset input cost inflation may accelerate volume losses. Increased promotional spending to defend volume may reduce our margins. Because a significant portion of our manufacturing, distribution and overhead costs are fixed in the near term, sustained volume declines reduce our ability to absorb those costs. Such declines could adversely affect our gross margin, operating income and cash flows.

Competition from branded manufacturers and private label products could reduce our market share, pricing and profitability.

The packaged food industry is highly competitive. In each of our categories we compete with large multinational food companies, regional and specialty manufacturers, and smaller emerging brands. Many smaller brands have gained distribution through e-commerce and natural and specialty retail channels with relatively low capital investment. Some competitors have greater financial resources, broader product portfolios, lower cost structures or more extensive marketing capabilities than we do. Competitors may introduce new products, reduce prices, increase promotional activity or obtain more favorable shelf placement in ways that reduce demand for our products.

We also compete with private label products offered by our retail customers. Many of these customers have invested in expanding and premiumizing their own brands, which they often sell at a meaningful discount to national brands and which can carry higher margins for the retailer. When the price gap between our products and private label alternatives widens, or when consumers come to regard private label quality as equivalent to ours, we may lose share. Although we manufacture a limited amount of private label product ourselves, that business is generally lower margin and does not offset share losses in our branded portfolio. Failure to compete effectively on price, quality, innovation and service could adversely affect our results of operations.

A significant portion of our sales is concentrated among a limited number of large customers, and the loss of, or a significant reduction in business with, any of them could adversely affect our results.

Our largest retail and foodservice customers account for a significant portion of our revenue. The retail grocery and foodservice distribution industries have consolidated over time, and the largest customers have substantial negotiating leverage. These customers may seek lower prices, increased trade promotion support, longer payment terms, more favorable delivery and service requirements, or contributions toward their own marketing and retail media programs. They may also reduce the number of SKUs they carry, allocate shelf space to private label or competing brands, or reduce inventory levels in their supply chains. Some customers impose fines or fees for failure to meet fill rate, on-time delivery or other service standards.

We generally do not have long-term supply contracts with our retail customers, and purchasing decisions are typically made on a category-by-category or promotional-period basis. The loss of a significant customer, a material reduction in its purchases, a change in its business model or a deterioration in its financial condition could adversely affect our revenue, profitability and accounts receivable collections. In addition, further consolidation among retailers or distributors could increase customer concentration and intensify these pressures.

Changes in consumer preferences, health and wellness trends and eating habits could reduce demand for our products.

Our success depends on our ability to anticipate and respond to changing consumer tastes and dietary habits. Consumer interest in reduced sodium and sugar, simpler ingredient statements, higher protein content, plant-based products and minimally processed foods continues to evolve. Several of our core categories, including condensed soups, shelf-stable entrees, cookies, snack cakes and savory snacks, may be perceived by some consumers as less consistent with these preferences. Public attention to ultra-processed foods, and the growing use of medications that reduce appetite or alter food preferences, could reduce overall consumption in some of our categories or shift purchases toward products we do not currently offer.

Our research and development spending is modest relative to the size of our business, and our product renovation efforts may not keep pace with consumer expectations or with the innovation of competitors. Reformulating products can also increase costs, affect taste and texture, and alienate existing loyal consumers. If we fail to identify and respond to these trends in a timely and cost-effective manner, demand for our products could decline and our results could be adversely affected.

Our results depend on the strength of our brands and on the effectiveness of our marketing and trade promotion spending.

Our brands and trademarks are among our most valuable assets. Many have been sold for several generations, and consumer familiarity and trust in those brands are central to our business. Brand value can be harmed by product quality failures, recalls, negative publicity, adverse social media commentary, labeling controversies or actions by our licensees, co-manufacturers or distributors that are outside of our direct control. Damage to the reputation of any of our major brands could be difficult and costly to repair.

We spend significant amounts on advertising, consumer promotions and trade promotions. Trade promotion spending is complex to plan and measure, and accruals for promotional obligations require significant estimates. Shifts in media consumption, the growth of retailer-operated advertising networks and changes in data privacy practices are making it harder to reach consumers efficiently and to measure the return on our marketing investment. If our spending does not generate sufficient incremental sales, or if we must increase spending to maintain our competitive position, our margins and profitability could be adversely affected.

Portfolio actions, including divestitures, product line exits and SKU rationalization, may not achieve their intended benefits and could disrupt our business.

As part of our strategy to improve profitability and focus on our core brands, we have exited or rationalized certain lower-margin product lines and completed other portfolio actions, which contributed to the decline in our revenue in fiscal 2024. We expect to continue to evaluate our portfolio, including the composition of our international business, and we may undertake additional divestitures, exits or acquisitions. These actions involve risks, including:
- stranded overhead and manufacturing costs that are not eliminated when the related revenue is removed;
- reduced scale in procurement, logistics and customer negotiations;
- disruption to relationships with customers, suppliers and employees;
- transition service and supply arrangements that divert management attention and resources;
- indemnification obligations and retained liabilities associated with divested businesses; and
- losses on disposal or impairment charges.

Smaller revenue resulting from portfolio actions may make it more difficult to leverage our fixed cost base. We may not realize the margin improvement or other benefits we expect from these actions, within the anticipated time frame or at all. Any acquisitions we pursue would carry integration risks and could increase our indebtedness or require the issuance of equity.

The continuing shift of consumer purchasing toward e-commerce, discount and other evolving retail channels could adversely affect our business.

Consumers increasingly purchase food through online platforms, retailer delivery and pickup services, warehouse clubs, dollar stores and limited-assortment discount formats. These channels often carry fewer SKUs and favor private label or value-priced products. They may also require specialized pack sizes, packaging designed for shipment or different supply chain capabilities. Online retailers and the digital platforms of traditional retailers give consumers easy price comparisons and access to a broader range of competing brands, including emerging brands that have not historically had wide physical distribution.

Adapting to these channels requires continued investment in packaging development, digital marketing, data analytics and supply chain capabilities, and these channels may be less profitable for us than traditional grocery. If we fail to compete effectively as purchasing patterns shift, or if growth in these channels comes at the expense of higher-margin sales elsewhere, our revenue and profitability could be adversely affected.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Volatility in the cost and availability of raw materials, packaging, energy and transportation could reduce our margins.

Our cost of revenue represents the large majority of our total costs, and our gross margin is sensitive to changes in input costs. We purchase large quantities of agricultural commodities and other inputs, including vegetables, tomatoes, flour and other grains, sugar, cocoa, nuts, potatoes, dairy ingredients, edible oils and proteins. We also purchase steel and aluminum for cans, flexible film, corrugate and other packaging materials, together with natural gas, electricity and diesel fuel. The prices of these inputs can fluctuate significantly as a result of weather, crop yields, plant and animal disease, geopolitical conflict, export restrictions, tariffs, energy prices, currency movements, speculative trading and changes in global supply and demand.

We use forward purchase contracts and, in some cases, derivative instruments to manage a portion of our exposure. These strategies do not cover all of our requirements or time horizons, and they may result in costs above prevailing market prices if commodity prices fall. Our ability to pass increased costs on to customers through pricing is limited by competitive conditions, customer resistance and the elasticity of consumer demand, and price increases typically take effect only after a lag. With an operating margin that remains narrow, even modest unrecovered increases in input costs could have a significant effect on our operating income and cash flows.

Disruptions in our supply chain, manufacturing network or reliance on third parties could impair our ability to meet customer demand.

We manufacture most of our products in company-owned facilities and rely on third-party co-manufacturers, warehouse operators, transportation carriers and independent distributors for important parts of our production and distribution. Some products are made at a single facility or by a single co-manufacturer. Some ingredients and packaging materials are available from only a limited number of suppliers. A disruption at any of these points could interrupt supply of affected products. Such disruptions may result from fire, flood, severe weather, equipment failure, contamination, labor disputes, cyber incidents, supplier insolvency, regulatory action or transportation constraints.

Our retail customers increasingly measure supplier performance by fill rates and on-time delivery and may reduce shelf space, impose penalties or shift promotional support if we fail to meet their expectations. Our network optimization efforts, including the consolidation of certain manufacturing and distribution operations, may increase our dependence on fewer facilities and reduce our ability to shift production in the event of a disruption. Our business interruption insurance may not fully compensate us for losses, including lost sales and damage to customer relationships.

Product contamination, recalls and food safety concerns could harm our reputation and result in significant costs.

We sell products that are consumed by people, and our products may be subject to contamination by foodborne pathogens, undeclared allergens, foreign materials or other adulterants. Such contamination may occur during the growing, manufacturing, packaging, storage, distribution or preparation of products. Some of these risks arise at suppliers, co-manufacturers or customers over whom we have limited control. Foodservice products, which may be further handled and prepared by operators, carry additional exposure. We may be required to recall products voluntarily or at the direction of regulators, to destroy inventory and to defend product liability claims.

Product recalls and food safety incidents can result in direct costs, lost sales, customer fines and loss of shelf space. They can also cause lasting damage to the reputation of affected brands, and concerns about one product may affect consumer perceptions of an entire category or of our company more broadly, even where the concern originates with another manufacturer or proves to be unfounded. Our insurance coverage may not be adequate to cover all costs and liabilities arising from a significant food safety event.

We may not realize the expected savings from our cost productivity and restructuring programs.

Our strategy relies on multi-year programs to reduce cost of goods sold and selling, general and administrative expenses through procurement savings, network optimization, automation, waste reduction, organizational simplification and the use of shared service centers. Our ability to maintain and improve our operating margin, particularly in a period of declining revenue, depends significantly on the success of these programs. They involve complex changes to our manufacturing footprint, systems and processes, and they may require significant upfront cash expenditures and charges, including severance, asset write-downs and accelerated depreciation.

The savings we achieve may be smaller, take longer to realize or cost more than we expect. Restructuring activities may also disrupt operations, impair service to customers, reduce employee morale and productivity, and result in the loss of employees with important institutional knowledge. Savings we realize may be offset by input cost inflation, wage increases, competitive reinvestment or volume declines. If we fail to deliver planned productivity, our profitability and cash flows could be adversely affected.

Our business depends on our ability to attract, retain and manage a large workforce at competitive cost.

We employ approximately 22,700 people, a large proportion of whom work in manufacturing, warehousing and distribution roles. Competition for hourly workers in many of the communities where we operate remains intense, and we have experienced periods of elevated turnover, absenteeism and wage inflation. Increases in wage rates, including those resulting from changes in minimum wage laws, and increases in health care and other benefit costs could raise our operating costs. A portion of our workforce is represented by labor unions under collective bargaining agreements. We may be unable to renew these agreements on satisfactory terms, and strikes, work stoppages or other labor disruptions could interrupt production at affected facilities.

Our success also depends on our ability to attract and retain skilled personnel in areas such as food science, engineering, supply chain management, data analytics and digital marketing, as well as on the continued service of our senior leadership. Workforce reductions associated with our productivity programs could make it more difficult to retain key employees. An inability to maintain an adequate and capable workforce could adversely affect our operations and financial results.

A failure or breach of our information technology systems could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.

We rely extensively on information technology systems, some of which are managed by third-party service providers, to manage procurement, production, inventory, order processing, distribution, trade promotion, financial reporting and communications with customers, suppliers and employees. These systems are vulnerable to damage or interruption from cyberattacks, including ransomware, phishing and social engineering, as well as from software defects, power outages, natural disasters and employee error. Attacks against companies in the food and agriculture sector have increased, and threat actors are using increasingly sophisticated techniques, including tools enabled by artificial intelligence.

A significant cyber incident could halt manufacturing or shipments, compromise confidential business information or personal data of employees and consumers, or impair our ability to report financial results accurately and on time. We have implemented and continue to upgrade our enterprise systems, and system transitions carry their own risks of disruption. Any such event could result in lost sales, remediation costs, regulatory penalties, litigation and reputational harm, and our cyber insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

Our international operations subject us to additional risks, including foreign currency fluctuations.

We sell selected brands and product lines in Latin America, Europe and the Asia-Pacific region through local subsidiaries, licensees and third-party distributors. These operations are exposed to risks that differ from those of our domestic business, including:
- fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates;
- political and economic instability;
- changes in import duties, tariffs and trade restrictions;
- differing food safety, labeling and ingredient regulations;
- limitations on the repatriation of earnings;
- difficulties in enforcing contracts and protecting intellectual property; and
- compliance with anti-corruption, sanctions and export control laws.

Because our international business is relatively small, we may lack the scale to operate efficiently in some markets. Actions we take to simplify our presence in certain markets may result in charges, disruption to distributor and licensee relationships, and the loss of revenue without a corresponding reduction in costs. Strengthening of the U.S. dollar reduces the dollar value of our foreign revenue and earnings.

Climate change, severe weather and natural resource constraints could adversely affect our supply chain and costs.

Many of the agricultural commodities we use are vulnerable to drought, flooding, extreme heat, changes in growing seasons and other weather patterns, which may become more frequent or severe as a result of climate change. Adverse weather in key growing regions for vegetables, tomatoes, grains, cocoa, potatoes and nuts could reduce supply, increase prices or lower quality. Our manufacturing facilities depend on adequate supplies of water and energy, and some are located in regions subject to water stress, hurricanes or other natural hazards.

Efforts to address climate change, including carbon pricing, energy efficiency requirements and changes in customer sustainability expectations, may increase our costs of energy, transportation and packaging. Customers and investors increasingly expect suppliers to set and meet emissions, packaging and sourcing goals. Failure to make progress on such goals, or to meet related reporting requirements, could affect customer relationships and our reputation.

Our level of indebtedness could limit our flexibility and adversely affect our financial condition.

We have a significant amount of long-term debt. Interest expense represents a substantial portion of our operating income, and our operating margin and net margin are narrow. As a result, a relatively small decline in operating income could significantly reduce our net income and our ability to service our debt. Our indebtedness could:
- require us to dedicate a substantial portion of our operating cash flow to interest and principal payments;
- reduce the funds available for capital expenditures, brand investment, dividends and share repurchases;

- increase our vulnerability to rising interest rates on any variable-rate or refinanced borrowings; and
- place us at a disadvantage compared with competitors that have less debt.

Although we reduced our long-term debt during fiscal 2024, we may incur additional indebtedness in the future, which would intensify these risks.

A downgrade in our credit ratings or adverse conditions in the credit markets could increase our borrowing costs and limit our access to financing.

We depend on access to the capital markets, our credit facilities and, from time to time, commercial paper and other short-term borrowings to fund our operations and to refinance maturing debt. Our current liabilities exceed our current assets, which increases our reliance on ongoing cash generation and on available liquidity sources to meet obligations as they come due. Credit rating agencies evaluate our leverage, profitability, cash flow and business profile. Continued revenue declines, weaker operating results, additional borrowings or more aggressive capital returns could lead to a downgrade.

A downgrade could increase our cost of borrowing, reduce our access to commercial paper markets, and cause suppliers or other counterparties to demand less favorable terms. Disruptions in credit markets, higher prevailing interest rates or changes in investor appetite for consumer staples issuers could make it more difficult or more expensive to refinance debt as it matures. Any of these developments could adversely affect our liquidity and financial condition.

Our ability to sustain dividends, share repurchases and debt reduction depends on cash flows that may not be sustained at recent levels.

During fiscal 2024, we paid dividends, repurchased shares of our common stock and reduced long-term debt. These uses of cash exceeded our net cash provided by operating activities after capital expenditures. A meaningful portion of the difference was funded by cash inflows from investing activities other than capital expenditures, including proceeds associated with portfolio actions, which may not recur. Our operating cash flow in fiscal 2024 also benefited from changes in working capital, which may not be repeatable.

If our operating cash flows decline or if we do not generate comparable proceeds from other sources, we may need to reduce or suspend share repurchases, reduce our dividend, slow the pace of debt reduction, defer capital investment or borrow additional funds. Our board of directors may change our capital allocation priorities at any time. A reduction in our dividend or share repurchases could adversely affect the market price of our common stock, and underinvestment in our manufacturing network or brands could weaken our competitive position over time.

We may be required to record impairment charges related to goodwill, trademarks and other intangible assets.

Goodwill and other non-current assets, which include indefinite-lived trademarks and other intangible assets arising from past acquisitions, represent a significant portion of our total assets. We test goodwill and indefinite-lived intangible assets for impairment at least annually and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying value may not be recoverable. These tests rely on estimates of future revenue, profitability, discount rates and royalty rates.

Continued revenue declines, sustained margin pressure in specific brands or reporting units, further portfolio actions, rising interest rates that increase discount rates, or a decline in our market capitalization could cause the estimated fair value of a reporting unit or trademark to fall below its carrying value. Impairment charges are non-cash, but they could be material to our reported results and stockholders' equity. Such charges could also affect our compliance with financial covenants and the perception of our business by investors and rating agencies.

Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Risk Management and Strategy

We depend on information technology systems to operate our manufacturing facilities, manage procurement and inventory, process customer orders and payments, coordinate distribution through company-operated and third-party warehouses, and support our shared service centers. We also depend on these systems to exchange data with our retail and foodservice customers. Because a disruption or compromise of these systems could affect our operations, financial reporting and reputation, we treat cybersecurity as a component of our enterprise risk management program.

Our enterprise risk management program is overseen by a committee of senior executives, including our Chief Executive Officer, Chief Financial Officer, General Counsel and Chief Information Officer. The committee evaluates current and emerging risks to the Company's strategic and operating objectives and has designated cybersecurity as a priority risk. The committee reviews this risk periodically and more often when circumstances warrant.

Day-to-day management of cybersecurity risk is the responsibility of our information security function. Our processes for identifying, assessing and managing risks from cybersecurity threats are informed by the cybersecurity framework published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology and by other recognized industry practices. Key elements of the program include:
- continuous monitoring of our networks, endpoints and cloud environments to detect suspicious activity;
- vulnerability management, including regular scanning, patching and configuration standards;
- identity and access controls, including multi-factor authentication for remote and privileged access;
- segmentation and specific safeguards for the operational technology that runs our manufacturing and distribution equipment;
- periodic penetration testing and security assessments performed by internal personnel and independent firms;
- mandatory security awareness training for employees with system access, supplemented by simulated phishing exercises; and
- participation in an industry information sharing organization to obtain timely threat intelligence relevant to food manufacturers and their supply chains.

Our information security function works with our Internal Audit function to evaluate cybersecurity-related controls as part of our broader internal control environment. We also maintain cybersecurity insurance and have arranged in advance for outside forensic and incident response support.

Third-Party Risk

We rely on third parties, including co-manufacturers, logistics providers, software and cloud service vendors, and independent distributors. Many of them have access to our systems or data. Before engaging a vendor that will have such access, we assess its security practices, and we tier vendors according to the sensitivity of the systems and information involved. Higher-risk vendors are subject to contractual security requirements and periodic reassessment.

Incident Response

We maintain a written cybersecurity incident response plan. The plan sets out the roles and responsibilities of the response team, procedures for containing, investigating and remediating incidents, and criteria for escalating matters to senior management and the Board of Directors. The plan also includes a process, involving our legal and financial reporting personnel, for evaluating whether an incident is material and whether disclosure is required. We test the plan through tabletop exercises and update it as our environment and the threat landscape change.

As of the date of this report, we have not identified any risks from cybersecurity threats, including as a result of any previous incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect the Company, including our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. However, we cannot provide assurance that we will not experience such an incident in the future. For further discussion, see Part I, Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

Governance

Board Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees our enterprise risk management program and has delegated primary oversight of cybersecurity risk to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives regular reports from our Chief Information Officer and Chief Information Security Officer. These reports cover the threat environment, the results of assessments and testing, the status of key security initiatives and any significant incidents. The Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full Board, which also receives periodic cybersecurity briefings directly.

Management's Role

Our cybersecurity program is led by our Chief Information Security Officer ("CISO"), who reports to our Chief Information Officer. The Chief Information Officer, in turn, reports to our Chief Financial Officer. Our CISO has extensive experience in information security, risk management and technology operations, including prior leadership roles in security programs at large consumer products and manufacturing organizations. The CISO also holds recognized professional certifications in information security. The CISO's team keeps senior management informed of cybersecurity threats and incidents through regular reporting, and it escalates significant matters promptly in accordance with our incident response plan.

PART II

Item 7. Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations

Overview

The following discussion covers our results of operations for the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024 ("fiscal 2024") compared with the fiscal year ended December 31, 2023 ("fiscal 2023"), as well as our financial condition at the end of each year. Read it together with our consolidated financial statements and the related notes in Part II, Item 8 of this Annual Report on Form 10-K. The discussion explains the principal changes in our financial statements between the two years, the factors behind those changes, and the measures management uses to evaluate the business.

This discussion contains forward-looking statements that involve risks and uncertainties. Actual results could differ materially from those we anticipate because of various factors, including those described in Part I, Item 1A, "Risk Factors." We do not undertake to update forward-looking statements except as required by law.

Zenaltal Foods, Inc. manufactures and markets branded and private label packaged foods. Our products are sold primarily in the United States and in selected international markets. We manage the business through three reportable segments: North America Retail, Snacks and Bakery, and Foodservice and International. Certain corporate expenses are not allocated to the segments and are reported as corporate and unallocated items. These include headquarters functions, certain restructuring activities and other items not attributable to the ongoing operations of a particular segment.

Fiscal 2024 was a year of deliberate repositioning. Revenue decreased 6.7% to $15,185 million from $16,282 million in fiscal 2023. The decline reflected three factors:
- lower volumes in several categories as consumers responded to cumulative food price inflation;
- our exit from or rationalization of certain lower-margin product lines; and
- portfolio actions completed during the year.

Despite the lower revenue, operating income increased 38.7% to $548 million from $395 million, and our operating margin improved to 3.6% from 2.4%. The improvement came mainly from two sources. First, we recorded no other operating expense in fiscal 2024, compared with $188 million in fiscal 2023. Second, cost productivity actions reduced selling, general and administrative expenses and research and development expenses. Net income increased 120.0% to $242 million from $110 million, and net margin improved to 1.6% from 0.7%.

Business Environment and Key Factors Affecting Our Results

We operate in a highly competitive packaged food industry. Our competitors include large multinational food companies, regional and specialty manufacturers, smaller emerging brands, and the private label programs of our retail customers.

Consumer behavior remained a central influence on our results in fiscal 2024. Several years of cumulative price increases across the food industry have strained household budgets. Many consumers have responded by:
- buying fewer items per trip;
- trading down to lower-priced brands, pack sizes or private label alternatives; and
- shifting purchases to warehouse clubs, dollar stores, limited-assortment discount formats and e-commerce platforms.

These trends were most pronounced in discretionary categories, such as sweet baked goods and certain snacks, and in categories where the price gap between branded and private label products widened.

Our results are also affected by the cost and availability of key inputs. These include vegetables, tomatoes, grains, flour, sugar, edible oils, cocoa, nuts, dairy ingredients, proteins, steel and aluminum for cans, and flexible packaging materials. Inflation in some inputs moderated during fiscal 2024 compared with the prior two years. Other inputs, notably cocoa and certain labor-intensive services, continued to rise. Freight and warehousing costs were generally more stable than in earlier periods, although wage rates across our manufacturing and distribution network continued to increase.

In this environment, we have prioritized profitability and resilience over volume. We have:
- reduced the number of SKUs in our portfolio;
- exited product lines that did not meet our margin or strategic criteria;
- reduced our presence in certain international markets; and
- continued multi-year programs to lower the cost of goods sold and manage overhead.

These actions reduce revenue in the near term. We believe they position the business for more consistent margins and cash generation. However, smaller revenue makes it more difficult to absorb fixed manufacturing, distribution and overhead costs, and we may not realize all of the benefits we expect.

We expect continued uncertainty in fiscal 2025. Sources of that uncertainty include consumer spending on food at home, competitive pricing and promotional activity, input cost volatility, changes in trade policy and tariffs, foreign currency fluctuations, and the evolving purchasing practices of our largest customers.

Results of Operations

The following discussion compares our consolidated results for fiscal 2024 with those for fiscal 2023. Financial information for our reportable segments is set forth in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Part II, Item 8.

Revenue

Revenue decreased 6.7% to $15,185 million in fiscal 2024 from $16,282 million in fiscal 2023. The principal drivers were as follows.

Volume declines in our continuing business. Volumes were lower in a number of categories, particularly in the North America Retail and Snacks and Bakery segments. Consumers reduced purchases and traded down in response to cumulative food price inflation. Retail customers also continued to expand their private label offerings and, in some cases, reduced inventory levels and the number of branded SKUs they carry.

Exits and rationalization of lower-margin product lines. As part of our strategy to concentrate resources on core brands, we discontinued or reduced distribution of selected product lines and SKUs. These lines generated revenue in fiscal 2023 but did not meet our expectations for growth, margin or strategic fit.

Portfolio actions. Revenue also declined because of portfolio actions completed during fiscal 2024, including steps to simplify our presence in certain international markets. Revenue associated with these businesses is not included in our results after the dates of the respective transactions.

Price realization was modestly favorable overall. It reflected pricing actions taken in prior periods to recover input cost inflation, partially offset by higher trade promotion investment in categories where competitive intensity increased. In the Foodservice and International segment, revenue was also affected by softer traffic in certain away-from-home dining channels and by changes in foreign currency exchange rates.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $10,552 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $11,321 million in fiscal 2023. Gross profit was $4,633 million, compared with $4,961 million. Gross margin was 30.5% in both years.

The lower cost of revenue mainly reflected lower volumes and the exit of product lines and businesses that are no longer part of our portfolio. Gross margin was unchanged because favorable and unfavorable factors largely offset each other.

Factors that supported gross margin:
- productivity savings from procurement initiatives, network optimization, automation and waste reduction programs;
- a favorable mix effect from exiting lower-margin product lines, which carried gross margins below our portfolio average; and
- moderation of inflation in certain commodities and packaging materials.

Factors that pressured gross margin:
- fixed cost deleverage, as lower volumes spread manufacturing overhead and depreciation over fewer units;
- continued inflation in certain inputs, including cocoa, as well as wage rates in our manufacturing and distribution operations;
- higher trade promotion investment, which is recorded as a reduction of revenue and therefore reduces gross margin; and
- transition costs associated with consolidating production as product lines were exited.

Research and Development

Research and development expenses were $91 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $128 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, they were 0.6% in fiscal 2024 and 0.8% in fiscal 2023.

The decrease reflected the reprioritization of our development pipeline toward core brands and the elimination of projects associated with exited product lines and divested businesses. We also benefited from consolidating certain technical activities at our New Jersey technical center. Our research and development activities remain focused on product formulation and renovation, process improvement, packaging development, food safety and quality assurance. Recent priorities included reduced sodium and sugar, simpler ingredient statements and more convenient or recyclable packaging.

Selling, General and Administrative Expenses

Selling, general and administrative expenses were $3,994 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $4,250 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, they were 26.3% in fiscal 2024 and 26.1% in fiscal 2023.

The decrease in absolute terms reflected several factors:
- lower advertising and consumer promotion spending for product lines we exited or deemphasized;
- reduced selling and distribution expenses associated with lower volumes and divested businesses;
- savings from simplifying our organizational structure and moving transactional processes to shared service centers; and
- lower incentive compensation and discretionary spending.

The slight increase in selling, general and administrative expenses as a share of revenue indicates that our overhead structure did not decline as quickly as revenue. Certain corporate and shared costs were not eliminated when the related revenue was removed. In addition, we continued to invest in marketing support for our core brands and in digital commerce, data analytics and customer-facing capabilities. Wage inflation also increased certain personnel costs. Reducing stranded overhead and improving the leverage of our cost structure remain priorities.

Other Operating Expense

We recorded no other operating expense in fiscal 2024, compared with $188 million in fiscal 2023. The fiscal 2023 amount consisted of charges that we do not consider part of our ongoing operating results. These primarily related to restructuring and network optimization activities, impairment of certain long-lived and intangible assets associated with product lines identified for exit, and related costs. No comparable charges were recognized in fiscal 2024. We may incur similar charges in future periods if we undertake further restructuring, portfolio actions or asset impairments.

Operating Income

Operating income increased 38.7% to $548 million in fiscal 2024 from $395 million in fiscal 2023. Operating margin improved to 3.6% from 2.4%.

The increase was primarily attributable to:
- the absence of the other operating expense recorded in fiscal 2023; and
- lower selling, general and administrative expenses and research and development expenses.

These benefits were partially offset by the lower gross profit that resulted from reduced revenue.

Interest Expense

Interest expense was $240 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $256 million in fiscal 2023. The decrease primarily reflected lower average borrowings, as we reduced our long-term debt during the year. This effect was partially offset by higher interest rates on borrowings that were refinanced or that carry variable rates. Our long-term debt was $5,341 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $5,690 million at December 31, 2023.

Provision for Income Taxes and Effective Tax Rate

Income before income taxes was $308 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $139 million in fiscal 2023. The provision for income taxes was $66 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $29 million in fiscal 2023.

Our effective tax rate was 21.4% in fiscal 2024, compared with 20.9% in fiscal 2023. The higher rate primarily reflected:
- changes in the geographic mix of earnings;
- the tax effects of portfolio actions completed during the year; and
- a smaller relative benefit from favorable discrete items, such as the resolution of uncertain tax positions and adjustments to prior-year estimates.

Our effective tax rate can vary from period to period because of changes in the jurisdictional mix of earnings, the tax treatment of divestitures and restructuring activities, changes in tax laws and the outcome of tax audits.

Net Income

Net income increased 120.0% to $242 million in fiscal 2024 from $110 million in fiscal 2023. Net margin improved to 1.6% from 0.7%.

The increase primarily reflected higher operating income, together with lower interest expense. These benefits were partially offset by a higher provision for income taxes.

Segment Discussion

Each of our reportable segments was affected by the trends described above, to differing degrees.

The North America Retail segment experienced volume declines in several shelf-stable categories as consumers traded down and retailers expanded private label assortments. SKU rationalization also reduced the segment's revenue. Productivity savings and the exit of lower-margin items supported the segment's profitability.

The Snacks and Bakery segment was affected by softer demand for discretionary sweet baked goods and by continued cocoa cost inflation. Competitive intensity in savory snacks increased, which led to higher trade promotion investment.

The Foodservice and International segment reflected two main influences. Demand in certain away-from-home dining channels was softer. Portfolio actions to simplify our international presence also reduced the segment's revenue.

Segment revenue and segment operating income for each period are presented in the notes to our consolidated financial statements.

Critical Accounting Estimates

Our consolidated financial statements are prepared in accordance with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. Applying these principles requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses, and the related disclosures. We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions and other assumptions we believe are reasonable. Actual results may differ from these estimates.

We consider the following accounting estimates critical because they require significant judgment and changes in them could have a material effect on our financial statements. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements.

Trade Promotions and Customer Incentives

We offer a variety of trade promotion and customer incentive programs to our retail and foodservice customers. These include temporary price reductions, feature and display allowances, volume-based rebates, slotting fees and cooperative advertising. We record the cost of these programs as a reduction of revenue in the period in which the related sales are recognized.

Many of these programs are settled after the end of the promotional period. Determining the related accruals therefore requires estimates of:
- customer performance and expected volumes;
- the terms of individual agreements; and
- the timing and amount of customer deductions and claims.

We base these estimates on program terms, historical redemption and settlement experience, and current trends. Differences between estimated and actual promotional costs are recognized in the period in which they become known. Because trade promotion spending is significant relative to our revenue, changes in these estimates could have a material effect on reported revenue and operating income.

Goodwill and Indefinite-Lived Intangible Assets

Goodwill and indefinite-lived intangible assets, consisting primarily of trademarks, are not amortized. Instead, we test them for impairment at least annually, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that an asset may be impaired. We may first perform a qualitative assessment. If a quantitative test is required, we compare the fair value of each reporting unit or indefinite-lived intangible asset with its carrying amount.

We generally estimate fair values using discounted cash flow models, supplemented by market-based approaches. Key assumptions include:
- projected revenue growth rates and operating margins;
- royalty rates for trademarks;
- terminal growth rates; and
- discount rates that reflect the risks associated with the projected cash flows.

These assumptions are inherently uncertain. Continued volume declines, adverse changes in consumer preferences, the effects of portfolio actions, increases in interest rates or a sustained decline in our market capitalization could cause the fair value of a reporting unit or trademark to fall below its carrying amount. Any of these could result in an impairment charge. Certain trademarks associated with categories experiencing volume pressure carry fair values that do not significantly exceed their carrying amounts, and they would be more susceptible to impairment if our assumptions are not achieved.

Long-Lived Assets and Portfolio Actions

We review property, plant and equipment and finite-lived intangible assets for impairment whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. When we decide to exit a product line, consolidate manufacturing operations or divest a business, we must make several judgments:
- whether the related assets meet the criteria for classification as held for sale;
- the estimated fair value less costs to sell of those assets; and
- the remaining useful lives of assets that will continue to be used until the exit is completed.

For divestitures, determining the gain or loss on disposal requires estimates of the fair value of consideration received, the allocation of goodwill to the business sold, and any retained obligations or indemnities. Changes in these judgments could affect the timing and amount of impairment charges, accelerated depreciation and gains or losses recognized in our results.

Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and in numerous foreign jurisdictions. Determining our provision for income taxes, deferred tax assets and liabilities, and liabilities for uncertain tax positions requires significant judgment.

We recognize the benefit of a tax position only if it is more likely than not to be sustained upon examination, based on its technical merits. Estimating the amount of benefit to recognize involves judgment about the outcome of audits and the interpretation of complex tax laws. We record valuation allowances against deferred tax assets when it is more likely than not that some portion will not be realized, considering projected future taxable income and available tax planning strategies. Portfolio actions also require judgment about the tax basis of divested assets and the character of resulting gains or losses.

Changes in tax laws, the geographic mix of earnings, or the resolution of audits could cause our effective tax rate to differ from our estimates.

Inventory Valuation

Inventories are stated at the lower of cost or net realizable value. We record provisions for excess, obsolete and slow-moving inventory based on estimates of future demand, product shelf life and expected selling prices. SKU rationalization and product line exits increase the judgment required in these estimates, because the remaining inventory of discontinued items may need to be sold through alternative channels or written off. If actual demand or market conditions are less favorable than we project, additional inventory write-downs may be required.
