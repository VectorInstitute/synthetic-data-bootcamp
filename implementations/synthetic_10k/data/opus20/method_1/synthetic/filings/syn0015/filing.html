PART I

Item 1. Business

Overview

Zennov Medical, Inc. is a medical technology company that designs, develops, manufactures and markets devices and related software used in the diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular and neurovascular disease. We focus on conditions in which earlier detection and less invasive intervention can improve patient outcomes and reduce the overall cost of care. Our portfolio includes catheter-based systems for acute ischemic stroke and cerebral aneurysm, implantable and wearable cardiac rhythm management and monitoring devices, and imaging and analytics software that helps clinicians identify, triage and follow patients across the care continuum.

Zennov Medical, Inc. was incorporated in Delaware and is headquartered in Denver, Colorado. Unless the context requires otherwise, references in this Annual Report on Form 10-K to "Zennov," the "Company," "we," "us" and "our" refer to Zennov Medical, Inc. and its consolidated subsidiaries. Our fiscal year ends on December 31. For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024 ("fiscal 2024"), our total revenue was $2,264 million, an increase of 8.2% over the fiscal year ended December 31, 2023 ("fiscal 2023").

Innovation is central to how we compete. During fiscal 2024, research and development expense was 40.8% of revenue. We sustained this level of investment because we believe our long-term growth depends on a steady flow of new and improved products, clinical evidence that supports physician adoption and favorable reimbursement, and software capabilities that connect our devices to the clinical workflow. Our engineering, clinical and regulatory teams work closely with physicians, hospital systems and academic research centers to identify unmet clinical needs and to turn them into products that can be adopted efficiently in a procedural setting.

Our Strategy

Our strategy rests on four priorities:

  • Leading in the disease areas we serve. We focus our resources on a defined set of cardiovascular and neurovascular conditions in which we believe we can build differentiated positions through product performance, clinical evidence and physician relationships.
  • Serial innovation. We follow an iterative development model. Successive product generations build on established platforms, which lets us bring improvements to market efficiently while managing technical and regulatory risk. Alongside these programs, we pursue a smaller number of longer-horizon programs aimed at new therapeutic categories.
  • Connecting devices to data. We are integrating our devices with software that collects, interprets and communicates clinical information. We believe this will improve patient management, strengthen customer relationships and create recurring service revenue over time.
  • Disciplined operations and capital allocation. We seek to maintain an efficient commercial and administrative structure, invest in manufacturing quality and capacity, and return capital to stockholders, while preserving the flexibility to pursue targeted acquisitions and technology licenses that complement our portfolio.

Principal Products and Services

Our products are generally used by interventional neuroradiologists, neurosurgeons, interventional cardiologists, electrophysiologists, emergency physicians and the hospital staff who support them. We market our products under proprietary trademarks. Our principal product families are described below.

Neurovascular products. Our neurovascular portfolio includes aspiration catheters, stent retrievers, guide and access catheters, microcatheters and guidewires used in mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke. We also offer embolization coils, flow-diverting stents and intrasaccular devices used to treat cerebral aneurysms. Our ZenFlow aspiration platform and Clearpath retrieval systems are designed to restore blood flow quickly and with few device passes, and our access products are engineered for trackability and support in tortuous cerebral anatomy. Ongoing development in this product family includes next-generation aspiration technology, devices designed for distal and medium vessel occlusions, and aneurysm treatment options intended to expand the range of anatomies physicians can treat endovascularly.

Cardiac rhythm management and monitoring products. Our cardiac rhythm portfolio includes implantable pacemakers, leadless pacing systems, implantable cardioverter defibrillators and insertable cardiac monitors, together with the programmers and remote monitoring infrastructure that support them. Under the Cadence brand we also market wearable and patch-based ambulatory electrocardiogram monitors used to diagnose arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation. Our remote monitoring service transmits device data to clinicians, who can identify clinically significant events between office visits and adjust therapy as needed. Development activities in this area focus on extending device longevity, reducing device size, improving arrhythmia detection algorithms and expanding the indications for our leadless pacing technology.

Imaging, analytics and digital health products. Our Sentinel software platform applies validated image analysis algorithms to computed tomography and other imaging studies. It helps care teams identify suspected large vessel occlusions, intracranial hemorrhage and other time-sensitive findings, and it alerts the relevant specialists through secure mobile and desktop applications. The platform also supports care coordination, case review and quality reporting for stroke and cardiovascular programs. We sell these offerings primarily on a subscription basis, and they are increasingly integrated with our neurovascular and cardiac product lines. We also provide services that complement our hardware and software, including clinical education, procedural support, device programming and data management.

Reportable Segments

We manage our business through three reportable segments, which reflect how our chief operating decision maker reviews operating results, allocates resources and assesses performance. Additional information about our segments is included in the notes to our consolidated financial statements included elsewhere in this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

Neurovascular Therapies. This segment develops, manufactures and markets devices used in the endovascular treatment of acute ischemic stroke, cerebral aneurysms and related neurovascular conditions. Its customers are primarily comprehensive and thrombectomy-capable stroke centers and the interventional physicians who practice there. Growth in this segment is driven by broader recognition of mechanical thrombectomy as a standard of care, expanded treatment windows supported by clinical research, the development of regional stroke systems of care and continued innovation in device design. We believe our position in this segment reflects the clinical performance of our aspiration and access products, our relationships with leading stroke programs and our investment in physician training.

Cardiac Rhythm and Monitoring. This segment develops, manufactures and markets implantable and wearable devices for the diagnosis and management of cardiac arrhythmias and conduction disorders, together with associated remote monitoring services. Its customers include hospitals, ambulatory surgery centers, cardiology practices and independent diagnostic testing facilities. The segment benefits from an aging population, the rising prevalence of atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias, and the shift toward less invasive implant procedures and long-term remote patient management. Revenue in this segment includes a combination of device sales and recurring monitoring service fees.

Imaging and Digital Health. This segment develops and markets software, analytics and connected-care solutions that support the detection, triage and management of patients with stroke and cardiovascular disease. Its customers are primarily hospitals and integrated delivery networks, which typically enter into multi-year subscription agreements. The segment is our newest and smallest. We view it as strategically important because it connects our device businesses to the clinical workflow, deepens our relationships with health system decision makers and supports adoption of our therapeutic products.

Customers

Our customers are principally hospitals, integrated delivery networks, academic medical centers, ambulatory surgery centers, physician practices and independent diagnostic testing facilities. In many cases purchasing decisions are influenced by the physicians who use our products and made through hospital value analysis committees, group purchasing organizations ("GPOs") and integrated delivery networks that negotiate pricing and contract terms on behalf of their members. Outside the United States, our customers include public and private hospitals, national and regional health authorities that buy through tender processes, and independent distributors that resell our products in their territories.

Our customer base is broad, and no single end-user customer accounted for a material portion of our revenue in fiscal 2024. However, a significant share of our sales in the United States is made under agreements with a limited number of GPOs and integrated delivery networks. The loss of, or a material change in the terms of, any of these agreements could adversely affect our business. In certain international markets we depend on a limited number of independent distributors.

Most of our products are ultimately paid for, directly or indirectly, by third-party payors, including government health care programs such as Medicare and Medicaid in the United States, national health systems outside the United States, and private insurers. Hospital and physician demand for our products therefore depends in part on the availability and adequacy of coverage and reimbursement for the procedures in which our products are used.

Sales and Marketing

In the United States, we market and sell our products primarily through a direct sales organization made up of territory managers, clinical specialists and account executives with expertise in neurovascular intervention, cardiac rhythm management and hospital software. Our clinical specialists provide technical support in the procedure room, device programming and follow-up support, and in-service training for hospital staff. Our health system account teams work with hospital executives, GPOs and integrated delivery networks to establish enterprise agreements that span multiple product families.

Outside the United States, we sell through a combination of direct sales organizations in selected markets and independent distributors in others. We use distribution partners where they provide established market access, regulatory and logistics capabilities, and local customer relationships at a cost structure we believe is appropriate for the size of the opportunity. We regularly evaluate our route to market in each geography and may move from a distributor model to a direct model, or the reverse, as market conditions change.

Our marketing activities emphasize clinical evidence and physician education. We sponsor and support clinical trials and registries, present data at medical congresses, and run training programs at our facilities and at customer sites, including hands-on simulation for new and experienced users. We also support fellowship and residency education programs consistent with applicable laws and industry codes of conduct. Our digital health offerings are marketed jointly with our device businesses, and we seek to show health systems how our integrated solutions can improve time to treatment, operational efficiency and quality reporting.

Competition

The medical technology industry is highly competitive and subject to rapid technological change, evolving clinical practice and significant pricing pressure. In each of our segments we compete with large, diversified medical device companies that have substantially greater financial, manufacturing, marketing and research resources than we do, broader product portfolios and more extensive relationships with customers and GPOs. We also compete with smaller, specialized companies, including venture-backed companies developing novel technologies, and with software and imaging companies that offer clinical decision support and care coordination tools.

In Neurovascular Therapies, we compete primarily on clinical performance, ease of use, breadth of product offering, physician training and support, and price. In Cardiac Rhythm and Monitoring, we compete on device longevity and size, diagnostic accuracy, remote monitoring capabilities, magnetic resonance imaging compatibility, service quality and price, and many competitors offer bundled portfolios that span multiple cardiovascular categories. In Imaging and Digital Health, we compete on algorithm performance, regulatory clearances, integration with hospital information systems and imaging equipment, cybersecurity, customer service and total cost of ownership.

Competitors may develop products or therapies, including pharmaceutical, biologic or other non-device treatments, that are safer, more effective, less costly or more convenient than ours, or that make our products obsolete. Consolidation among health care providers and the growing influence of GPOs and integrated delivery networks have increased pressure on pricing and contract terms. We believe our continued investment in research and development, our clinical evidence, our focus on specific disease areas and the quality of our customer support position us to compete effectively. There can be no assurance, however, that we will maintain or improve our competitive position.

Seasonality

Our business is subject to modest seasonal fluctuations. Demand for elective and scheduled procedures, including many cardiac rhythm device implants and diagnostic monitoring studies, tends to be lower during summer months, particularly in Europe, and during year-end holiday periods. Demand is often stronger in the fourth quarter, when patients in the United States who have met annual insurance deductibles may schedule procedures and hospitals may complete capital and software purchases before their budget years close. Acute procedures such as mechanical thrombectomy for stroke are less affected by seasonal patterns. Quarterly results may also be affected by product launches, the timing of large customer agreements and tender awards, and distributor ordering patterns.

Government Regulation

Our products and operations are subject to extensive regulation by the FDA and other federal, state and local authorities in the United States, and by comparable authorities in other countries.

United States device regulation. Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, the FDA regulates the design, development, testing, manufacturing, labeling, storage, advertising, promotion, distribution, import, export and post-market surveillance of medical devices, including software that meets the definition of a medical device. Depending on its risk classification, a device generally requires either premarket notification under Section 510(k), de novo classification or premarket approval ("PMA") before it can be marketed in the United States. Our implantable cardiac rhythm devices and certain of our neurovascular implants are Class III devices that require PMA approval, which typically involves extensive clinical data and manufacturing inspections. Many of our catheters, accessories, monitors and software products are cleared through the 510(k) or de novo pathways. Modifications to marketed devices may require new clearances or approvals. Clinical studies conducted to support marketing applications are subject to the FDA's investigational device exemption regulations and to oversight by institutional review boards.

After a device is marketed, we are subject to ongoing requirements. These include the FDA's quality system regulation, establishment registration and device listing, labeling and promotional restrictions, unique device identification, medical device reporting of adverse events and malfunctions, reporting of corrections and removals, and post-approval study obligations. The FDA has amended its quality system regulation to align more closely with the international quality management system standard for medical devices, with the amended requirements taking effect in 2026, and we are preparing our quality system accordingly. The FDA inspects our facilities periodically. Failure to comply with applicable requirements can result in warning letters, product recalls or seizures, injunctions, civil penalties, suspension or withdrawal of marketing authorizations, or criminal prosecution.

Cybersecurity of medical devices. Our connected devices and software are subject to FDA requirements and guidance on medical device cybersecurity, including premarket submission requirements for cyber devices. These requirements cover software bills of materials, vulnerability management and post-market monitoring. We maintain a product security program designed to address these obligations.

International regulation. Outside the United States, our products are subject to regulatory requirements that vary by country. In the European Union, medical devices must comply with the Medical Device Regulation, which imposes stringent requirements for clinical evidence, technical documentation, post-market surveillance and oversight by notified bodies. Other markets, including the United Kingdom, Japan, China, Canada and Australia, maintain their own registration and quality system requirements, and approval timelines and data requirements differ significantly among them.

Health care fraud and abuse and transparency laws. We are subject to federal and state health care laws designed to prevent fraud and abuse. These include the federal Anti-Kickback Statute, the civil False Claims Act, the civil monetary penalties law and comparable state laws, which restrict the remuneration we may offer to health care professionals and institutions and the claims that may be submitted to government payors. The federal Physician Payments Sunshine Act and similar state laws require us to report certain payments and other transfers of value to physicians and teaching hospitals and certain other practitioners. We maintain a compliance program designed to meet these requirements, including policies, training, monitoring and auditing.

Coverage and reimbursement. Sales of our products depend significantly on coverage and payment for the associated procedures by government and private payors. In the United States, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services establishes coverage policies and payment rates for hospital inpatient, hospital outpatient, ambulatory surgery center and physician services. Changes in these policies or rates, or in the policies of private payors, may affect demand for our products. Outside the United States, reimbursement systems vary widely, and many countries use health technology assessments, reference pricing and public tenders that may limit prices or delay market access.

Data privacy and security. In the course of providing remote monitoring services and software, we collect, process and store health information and other personal data. We are subject to the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and its implementing regulations, state privacy and consumer health data laws, and international data protection laws, including the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation. These laws impose obligations on the collection, use, disclosure, security and cross-border transfer of personal data.

Other regulation. We are also subject to anti-corruption laws, including the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and comparable laws in other countries; export control and economic sanctions laws; customs and trade laws; environmental, health and safety laws governing the handling and disposal of hazardous materials and the operation of our facilities; and laws governing labor and employment. Compliance with these laws has not had, and is not currently expected to have, a material effect on our capital expenditures, earnings or competitive position, although future changes in law could increase our compliance costs.

Item 1A. Risk Factors

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

We face intense competition, and many of our competitors have substantially greater resources than we do.

The markets for neurovascular devices, cardiac rhythm management products, ambulatory cardiac monitoring and clinical imaging software are highly competitive and are characterized by rapid technological change. In each of our segments we compete with large, diversified medical technology companies. Many of these companies have broader product portfolios, larger sales forces, greater financial resources, established relationships with hospital systems and group purchasing organizations, and greater experience obtaining regulatory approvals and reimbursement. Several of them can bundle products across multiple therapeutic areas, offer volume-based discounts or absorb pricing concessions in ways that we may not be able to match. We also compete with smaller, venture-backed companies that may introduce disruptive technologies, particularly in aspiration thrombectomy, leadless pacing, patch-based monitoring and artificial intelligence-enabled image analysis.

Competitors may develop products that are safer, more effective, easier to use or less expensive than ours, or that obtain regulatory approval or favorable clinical data before ours do. In cardiac rhythm management in particular, physician loyalty to an installed base of programmers and remote monitoring systems can make it difficult to win new accounts. If we fail to compete effectively, we may lose market share, experience pricing pressure or be required to increase our commercial spending, any of which could adversely affect our revenue and margins.

Our growth depends on our substantial investment in research and development producing commercially successful products, and that investment may not generate an adequate return.

Research and development represents our largest operating expense and consumes a significant portion of our revenue each year, a level that is high relative to many companies in the medical technology industry. We maintain this level of investment because our strategy depends on serial innovation across our neurovascular, cardiac rhythm and digital health platforms and on longer-horizon programs aimed at new therapeutic categories. Product development is inherently uncertain. Programs may fail to achieve their technical objectives, may require more time or money than anticipated, may not produce clinical results sufficient to support regulatory approval or physician adoption, or may be overtaken by competing technologies before launch.

Because a large share of our cost base is committed to research and development, our operating results are sensitive to the productivity of that spending. If revenue growth slows, we may face a difficult choice between reducing development investment, which could impair our long-term competitive position, and maintaining it, which would pressure our operating margins and cash flows. Delays or failures in significant programs, such as our next-generation aspiration technology, devices for distal and medium vessel occlusions, or expanded indications for leadless pacing, could have a material adverse effect on our future growth.

Changes in coverage and reimbursement by third-party payors could reduce demand for our products and the prices we can obtain.

Our customers generally rely on Medicare, Medicaid, other government programs, national health systems and private insurers to pay for the procedures in which our products are used and for the remote monitoring and software services we provide. Payors continue to pursue cost containment through lower payment rates, bundled or episode-based payments, prior authorization requirements, coverage restrictions and value-based purchasing arrangements. Changes to hospital inpatient and outpatient payment systems, physician fee schedules or payment for remote physiologic and cardiac monitoring could reduce the attractiveness of our products to hospitals and physicians. Reimbursement for software-based triage and analytics remains limited and evolving, and the commercial prospects of our Imaging and Digital Health segment depend in part on whether hospitals perceive sufficient clinical and economic value to fund subscriptions in the absence of specific reimbursement.

Outside the United States, reimbursement and pricing are frequently controlled by government authorities that may require health technology assessments, impose reference pricing or award contracts through competitive tenders. Unfavorable coverage or reimbursement decisions in any of our significant markets could reduce procedure volumes, delay adoption of new products and adversely affect our results of operations.

Consolidation among health care providers and the purchasing power of group purchasing organizations exert continuing pressure on our prices.

A significant share of our United States sales is made under agreements with a limited number of group purchasing organizations and integrated delivery networks. These organizations negotiate aggressively on price, frequently award contracts to a limited number of suppliers in a product category and may favor competitors with broader portfolios. Continued consolidation among hospitals and health systems is concentrating purchasing decisions further and strengthening the negotiating position of our customers. Hospital value analysis committees increasingly require clinical and economic evidence before approving new technologies, which can lengthen sales cycles. The loss of, or adverse changes to, any significant purchasing agreement, or a failure to be included on new agreements, could materially reduce our revenue, and pricing concessions required to retain business could reduce our gross margin.

Clinical research and evolving treatment guidelines could reduce the use of our products.

Demand for our neurovascular products has benefited from clinical studies that established mechanical thrombectomy as a standard of care for many patients with large vessel occlusion stroke and that extended treatment windows. Demand for our cardiac rhythm and monitoring products depends similarly on clinical evidence supporting device therapy and long-term monitoring. Future studies, including studies sponsored by competitors or independent investigators, could produce unfavorable or ambiguous results for our products or for the procedures in which they are used, could show that competing therapies such as pharmacological treatments or alternative device approaches are equally or more effective, or could identify previously unrecognized safety risks. Professional society guidelines could change in ways that reduce the number of patients treated with our devices. Any of these developments could reduce physician adoption, impair reimbursement and adversely affect our sales.

Our Imaging and Digital Health segment and our broader connected-care strategy may not succeed.

Our strategy includes integrating our devices with software that collects, interprets and communicates clinical information and building recurring subscription revenue. Imaging and Digital Health is our newest and smallest segment. It faces competition from established imaging vendors, electronic health record providers and numerous emerging software companies. Hospital technology budgets are constrained, procurement and information security reviews can be lengthy, and integrating with diverse hospital information systems requires significant ongoing investment. Customers on multi-year subscriptions may decline to renew or may renegotiate pricing. If we cannot demonstrate clinical and economic value, keep our algorithms current, or achieve sufficient scale, the segment may not reach profitability and we may not realize the strategic benefits we expect for our device businesses.

Adverse economic conditions, inflation and constraints on hospital budgets could reduce demand for our products and increase our costs.

Hospital procedure volumes, capital budgets and purchasing behavior are affected by general economic conditions, labor shortages among clinical staff, changes in patient insurance coverage and the financial health of health systems. Economic downturns, high unemployment or loss of insurance coverage could cause patients to defer elective procedures and follow-up care, and financially stressed hospitals may delay technology purchases or seek further price concessions. At the same time, inflation in wages, components, raw materials, freight and energy has increased, and may continue to increase, our operating costs. Our ability to pass these increases on to customers is limited by long-term contracts, group purchasing arrangements and competitive dynamics, which could compress our margins.

Our international operations expose us to political, economic, trade and currency risks.

We sell our products in numerous countries through direct sales organizations and independent distributors, and we source certain materials and components from suppliers outside the United States. Our international business is subject to risks that include fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates, changes in tariffs and trade policy, export controls and sanctions, political instability and armed conflict, differing and changing regulatory and reimbursement requirements, longer payment cycles, difficulty enforcing contracts and intellectual property rights, and the challenges of staffing and managing foreign operations. In certain markets we depend on a small number of independent distributors whose performance, financial condition and compliance practices we do not control. The termination of a distributor relationship or a transition from a distributor model to a direct model could disrupt sales in the affected territory.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Disruptions in our manufacturing operations or failures in our quality systems could impair our ability to supply products.

We manufacture most of our devices in a limited number of facilities, and many products are produced at a single site. Our catheters, implantable devices and monitors require precise processes, specialized equipment, controlled environments and highly trained personnel. Equipment failures, contamination, natural disasters, extreme weather, labor disruptions, cyberattacks or regulatory actions affecting a facility could interrupt production for an extended period. Qualifying a replacement facility or manufacturing line is time-consuming and generally requires regulatory notification or approval. In addition, our facilities and processes must comply with the quality system regulations of the United States Food and Drug Administration ("FDA") and comparable foreign requirements. A failure to maintain compliance could result in warning letters, import holds, production stoppages or delays in approval of new products. Supply interruptions could cause us to lose customers to competitors, particularly in procedural markets where hospitals expect reliable access to devices.

We depend on a limited number of suppliers, including single-source suppliers, for certain components, materials and services.

We obtain certain critical components, including batteries, specialized electronic components, polymers, nitinol and other metals, as well as sterilization services, from a limited number of suppliers, and in some cases from a single supplier. Several of these suppliers serve the broader electronics and medical technology industries and may give priority to larger customers during periods of constrained supply. Changes in suppliers generally require qualification, validation and, in some cases, regulatory approval, which can take considerable time. In addition, regulatory and community scrutiny of ethylene oxide sterilization has led to facility closures and capacity constraints across the industry, and further restrictions could limit our access to sterilization capacity or increase its cost. A supply disruption, quality problem at a supplier or significant price increase could delay shipments, increase our cost of revenue and adversely affect our results of operations.

Cybersecurity incidents involving our systems, our connected devices or our software platforms could harm patients, our customers and our business.

Our business relies on information technology systems to manage manufacturing, supply chain, financial reporting and customer relationships. Our cardiac rhythm products communicate with programmers and remote monitoring infrastructure, and our Sentinel platform processes medical images and patient information from hospital systems and transmits alerts to clinicians' mobile and desktop devices. These systems and products are potential targets for ransomware, unauthorized access, malware and other attacks, including attacks by sophisticated and state-sponsored actors, and they are also vulnerable to employee error, vendor compromise and software defects. A successful attack could compromise patient safety, interrupt clinical workflows at our customers, expose protected health information, disrupt our operations or require costly remediation, product updates or field actions.

Security vulnerabilities in medical devices and health software attract heightened regulatory attention, and regulators increasingly require cybersecurity documentation as part of premarket submissions and post-market surveillance. A significant incident could result in regulatory enforcement, litigation, contractual liability to hospital customers, loss of customer confidence and harm to our reputation, and our insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

We may not successfully complete or integrate acquisitions or licensing transactions, and we could be required to record impairment charges.

Our strategy includes targeted acquisitions and technology licenses that complement our portfolio. Goodwill increased during fiscal 2024, and goodwill and other non-current assets together represent the majority of our total assets. Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity Risk Management and Strategy

We maintain an information security program designed to protect the confidentiality, integrity and availability of our information systems, our connected medical devices and software, and the data entrusted to us. This includes patient health information transmitted through our remote monitoring infrastructure and our Sentinel software platform. Cybersecurity risk is one of the risk categories in our enterprise risk management process. It is evaluated alongside operational, regulatory, financial and strategic risks so that it informs decisions about priorities and resource allocation.

Our program follows a layered approach to prevention, detection and response. Its principal elements include:

  • Identity and privileged access controls, including multifactor authentication.
  • Endpoint and network protection.
  • Email filtering.
  • Encryption of sensitive data.
  • Continuous security monitoring through a security operations function that operates around the clock.
  • Vulnerability scanning and patch management.
  • Secure configuration standards.
  • Data backup and recovery capabilities supporting our manufacturing and business continuity plans.

All employees and contractors with network access complete mandatory security awareness training. Simulated phishing exercises supplement the training, and personnel in higher-risk roles receive additional role-specific training.

Because our products increasingly connect to hospital networks and clinical workflows, we operate a dedicated product security function within our engineering organization. This team embeds threat modeling, secure coding practices, software composition analysis and penetration testing into the design and development of our implantable and wearable cardiac devices, programmers, remote monitoring systems and imaging software. It also monitors fielded products for emerging vulnerabilities and coordinates disclosure and remediation with customers and regulators. It supports the cybersecurity documentation required in our premarket submissions to the United States Food and Drug Administration and to regulatory authorities in other jurisdictions, as well as the security reviews that hospital customers conduct during procurement.

We assess cybersecurity risks through periodic risk assessments, threat intelligence, and the results of internal testing and audits. We benchmark our controls against recognized frameworks, including the National Institute of Standards and Technology Cybersecurity Framework. Independent assessors, consultants and penetration testers evaluate elements of our program from time to time, and our internal audit function includes information technology controls in its audit plan. We maintain a written incident response plan that defines roles, escalation criteria and communication procedures, including the process for evaluating whether an incident is material and requires public disclosure. We test this plan through simulated scenarios involving information technology, product security, legal, communications and operations leaders.

We rely on third parties for cloud hosting, software, manufacturing inputs, logistics and distribution. Before engaging vendors that will access our systems or handle sensitive data, we evaluate their security practices and contractually require them to maintain appropriate safeguards and to notify us of security incidents. We reassess higher-risk vendors periodically.

As of the date of this report, we are not aware of any cybersecurity threats or incidents, including any previous incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. However, threats continue to grow in frequency and sophistication, and our measures may not prevent or detect every attack. For additional information, see Part I, Item 1A, "Risk Factors."

Cybersecurity Governance

Our Board of Directors oversees risk management, including cybersecurity risk, and has delegated primary oversight of cybersecurity and data privacy to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives reports from management on cybersecurity matters at each regularly scheduled meeting. These reports cover the threat environment, program maturity, product security, the results of assessments and testing, and significant incidents. The Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full Board, which also reviews cybersecurity as part of its annual review of enterprise risks. Management promptly escalates significant incidents to the Audit Committee chair and, as appropriate, to the full Board in accordance with our incident response plan.

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads our cybersecurity program and reports to our Chief Information Officer, who reports to our Chief Executive Officer. Our Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience leading information security, risk management and incident response functions at global health care and technology organizations, including programs addressing regulated medical products. This individual also holds recognized industry certifications in information security. Our Chief Information Security Officer chairs a cross-functional cybersecurity steering committee that includes senior leaders from legal, compliance, privacy, quality and regulatory affairs, engineering, operations and finance. This committee reviews risks, approves program priorities and oversees remediation activities.

Executive Overview

General

Zennov Medical, Inc. is a Delaware corporation headquartered in Denver, Colorado. We design, develop, manufacture and market devices and related software used to diagnose and treat cardiovascular and neurovascular disease. Our portfolio includes catheter-based systems for acute ischemic stroke and cerebral aneurysm, implantable and wearable cardiac rhythm management and monitoring devices, and imaging and analytics software. Clinicians use this software to identify, triage and follow patients across the care continuum. At the end of fiscal 2024 we employed about 2,700 people.

We operate through three reportable segments: Neurovascular Therapies, Cardiac Rhythm and Monitoring, and Imaging and Digital Health. Our chief operating decision maker uses these segments to evaluate performance and allocate resources. Segment financial information is presented in the notes to our consolidated financial statements.

Our business model depends on sustained investment in innovation. We compete through product performance, clinical evidence and physician relationships, and we develop products iteratively on established platforms. As a result, research and development is our largest operating expense. We aim to fund that investment while keeping our commercial and administrative structure efficient, investing in manufacturing quality and capacity, and returning capital to stockholders.

Key Factors Affecting Our Results

Several factors influence our results and are relevant to comparisons between periods.

Procedure volumes and clinical adoption. Demand for our neurovascular products depends on the number of mechanical thrombectomy and aneurysm treatment procedures. Those volumes are driven by broader recognition of thrombectomy as a standard of care, expanded treatment windows and the growth of regional stroke systems. Demand for our cardiac rhythm and monitoring products reflects an aging population, the rising prevalence of atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias, and a shift toward less invasive implants and long-term remote patient management.

Product mix and new product introductions. Margins vary across our product families. Newer generations of our aspiration, access and leadless pacing products and our subscription software generally carry different cost structures than older products. The timing of launches and the rate at which customers convert to new platforms therefore affect revenue growth and gross margin.

Pricing and purchasing dynamics. A significant share of our United States sales is made under agreements with a limited number of group purchasing organizations and integrated delivery networks. Outside the United States, many sales are made through tenders. These arrangements, together with continued consolidation among health systems, put ongoing pressure on our prices.

Reimbursement. Coverage and payment decisions by government and private payors affect demand for the procedures in which our products are used. They also affect demand for our remote monitoring and software services.

Cost inflation and supply chain. Wages, components, raw materials, freight and energy costs affect our cost of revenue and operating expenses. Our ability to pass these increases on to customers is limited.

Results of Operations

The following discussion compares our consolidated results for fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023. All amounts are in millions unless otherwise stated.

Revenue

Revenue was $2,264 million in fiscal 2024 and $2,093 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 8.2%. Growth came mainly from higher unit volumes across our device portfolio and a growing contribution from recurring service and subscription revenue.

In our Neurovascular Therapies segment, growth reflected continued procedure growth in mechanical thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke. It also reflected adoption of our ZenFlow aspiration platform and related access products at comprehensive and thrombectomy-capable stroke centers. Our investment in physician training and our relationships with leading stroke programs supported account expansion during the year. Demand for our aneurysm treatment products, including flow-diverting stents and intrasaccular devices, also contributed.

In our Cardiac Rhythm and Monitoring segment, growth was driven by continued adoption of our leadless pacing systems and insertable cardiac monitors. Demand for our Cadence wearable and patch-based ambulatory electrocardiogram monitors also increased. Recurring remote monitoring service fees grew as the installed base of patients followed through our monitoring infrastructure expanded. Competition in traditional implantable devices remained intense, and installed-base dynamics continued to affect our ability to win new accounts.

In our Imaging and Digital Health segment, revenue growth reflected new multi-year subscription agreements for our Sentinel software platform and expanded deployments within existing hospital and integrated delivery network customers. This segment remains our smallest. We continue to integrate it more closely with our neurovascular and cardiac product lines as part of enterprise agreements with health systems.

Pricing pressure from group purchasing organizations, integrated delivery networks and international tenders partly offset volume growth across our segments. We expect pricing pressure to continue.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $606 million in fiscal 2024 and $584 million in fiscal 2023. Gross profit was $1,658 million in fiscal 2024 and $1,509 million in fiscal 2023. Gross margin increased to 73.2% from 72.1%.

Three factors drove the improvement. First, the product mix shifted toward newer-generation devices and subscription software, which carry higher margins. Second, higher production volumes spread fixed manufacturing overhead over more units. Third, manufacturing yield and process efficiency improved at our principal facilities. Continued inflation in labor, components and freight, together with pricing concessions under purchasing agreements, partly offset these benefits.

Because many of our products are produced at a single site, gross margin can be affected by production scheduling, the timing of new product transfers into manufacturing, and costs associated with maintaining our quality systems. We continue to invest in manufacturing quality and capacity to support anticipated volume growth and new product introductions.

Research and Development

Research and development expense was $923 million in fiscal 2024 and $854 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, it was 40.8% in both years.

Higher spending supported our serial innovation programs and our longer-horizon development efforts. In neurovascular, spending focused on next-generation aspiration technology, devices for distal and medium vessel occlusions, and aneurysm treatment options intended to address a broader range of anatomies. In cardiac rhythm management, spending focused on device longevity, miniaturization, improved arrhythmia detection algorithms and expanded indications for our leadless pacing technology. Higher expense also reflected clinical studies to generate evidence supporting physician adoption and reimbursement, regulatory activities, and continued investment in software engineering for our Sentinel platform and the integration of device data with the clinical workflow. Personnel costs increased as we added engineering, clinical and regulatory staff.

Our research and development spending is high relative to many companies in the medical technology industry. We maintained this level because we believe our long-term growth depends on new and improved products and on the clinical evidence and software capabilities that support them. Because a large share of our cost base is committed to development, our operating margin is sensitive to revenue growth and to the productivity of this spending.

Selling, General and Administrative

Selling, general and administrative expense was $124 million in fiscal 2024 and $87 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, it was 5.5% in fiscal 2024 and 4.2% in fiscal 2023.

The increase reflected investment in our commercial organization. In particular, we added clinical specialists and health system account resources to support procedure growth, new product launches and enterprise agreements spanning multiple product families. It also reflected higher general and administrative costs, including information technology and cybersecurity, compliance, and costs of integrating acquired businesses and technologies. Even with this increase, our selling, general and administrative expense remains low relative to revenue. This reflects our emphasis on an efficient commercial and administrative structure.

Other Operating Expense

Operating Income

Operating income grew because revenue increased and gross margin expanded. Operating margin was broadly stable but decreased slightly. The gross margin improvement was more than offset by selling, general and administrative expense rising as a share of revenue, while research and development held at a constant share of revenue.

We expect to fund these requirements from operating cash flows, existing cash and cash equivalents, and available borrowing capacity.

Critical Accounting Estimates

We prepare our consolidated financial statements in conformity with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. This requires management to make estimates, judgments and assumptions that affect reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses, and related disclosures.

We consider an accounting estimate critical if it requires significant judgment about matters that are highly uncertain at the time the estimate is made, and if different reasonable estimates, or changes in estimates that are reasonably likely, could materially affect our financial statements. We base our estimates on historical experience and on other assumptions we believe are reasonable under the circumstances. Actual results may differ. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements.

Revenue Recognition and Variable Consideration

We recognize revenue when control of promised goods or services transfers to customers, in an amount that reflects the consideration we expect to receive. For most device sales, control transfers at shipment or, for consigned inventory held at customer sites, when the product is used in a procedure. Revenue from remote monitoring services and Sentinel software subscriptions is generally recognized ratably over the service or subscription period.

Many contracts include multiple performance obligations, such as devices, programmers, monitoring services, software and support. We allocate the transaction price based on relative standalone selling prices. When observable prices are not available, we estimate standalone selling prices using expected cost plus margin or other methods. This requires judgment.

Our transaction prices include variable consideration. Examples are rebates and administrative fees payable to group purchasing organizations and integrated delivery networks, chargebacks, volume and tiered pricing discounts, distributor price adjustments and product returns. We estimate variable consideration using the expected value or most likely amount method, based on contract terms, historical experience, expected purchase volumes and current trends. Revenue is recognized only to the extent that a significant reversal is not probable. If actual rebates, returns or price adjustments differ from our estimates, or if customer purchasing patterns change, we may need to adjust revenue in future periods.

Inventory Valuation

We state inventories at the lower of cost or net realizable value. We record adjustments for excess, obsolete or expiring inventory based on forecasted demand, product life cycles, shelf-life limits, planned product transitions and expected selling prices. Our products have defined shelf lives, and new product generations can make existing inventory obsolete quickly. Estimating these adjustments therefore requires judgment. If actual demand or the timing of product transitions differs from our forecasts, we may record additional inventory charges, which would increase cost of revenue.

Business Combinations

We account for acquisitions using the acquisition method. Under this method, we record assets acquired and liabilities assumed at their estimated fair values at the acquisition date, and record the excess of the purchase price as goodwill. Estimating the fair values of acquired intangible assets, such as developed technology, in-process research and development and customer relationships, requires significant judgment. Key assumptions include projected revenue and cash flows, technology obsolescence rates, the probability and timing of regulatory approvals, and discount rates. When contingent consideration is part of an acquisition, we estimate its fair value using probability-weighted assumptions about the achievement of milestones. We remeasure contingent consideration each reporting period, and changes in fair value are recognized in earnings.

Goodwill and Intangible Asset Impairment

Goodwill was $490 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment annually and whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. Our reporting units are aligned with our reportable segments. We may first perform a qualitative assessment. If we perform a quantitative test, we estimate reporting unit fair value using discounted cash flow analyses, market-based approaches or both.

These estimates require significant assumptions, including revenue growth, gross margin, operating expense levels, terminal growth rates and discount rates. The assumptions for our Imaging and Digital Health reporting unit carry more uncertainty than those for our device businesses. This is because the segment is newer and smaller, and its prospects depend on subscription growth and hospital adoption.

We review finite-lived intangible assets for impairment when indicators of impairment exist. We test indefinite-lived intangible assets, including in-process research and development, at least annually. Unfavorable clinical results, regulatory setbacks, competitive developments or changes in reimbursement could reduce expected cash flows and cause impairment charges.

Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and in numerous foreign jurisdictions. Determining our provision for income taxes, deferred tax assets and liabilities, and reserves for uncertain tax positions requires significant judgment. Key judgments include interpreting complex tax laws, allocating income among jurisdictions under transfer pricing arrangements, evaluating the realizability of deferred tax assets, and assessing the likely outcome of tax examinations. We recognize a tax benefit only when it is more likely than not to be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. Changes in tax law, the geographic mix of earnings, audit outcomes and changes in our assessment of uncertain positions could materially affect our effective tax rate in future periods.

Product Liability, Litigation and Contingencies

We are subject to product liability claims, intellectual property disputes and other legal proceedings arising in the ordinary course of business. We record a liability when a loss is probable and the amount can be reasonably estimated. Assessing the probability of loss and estimating the amount requires significant judgment about the facts of each matter, applicable law, and the potential outcome of litigation or settlement negotiations. These matters are inherently uncertain, and actual outcomes could differ materially from our estimates. We also accrue estimated costs of product warranties and field actions based on historical experience and specific known issues. If the frequency or cost of product failures or field actions differs from our expectations, we may need to revise these accruals.
