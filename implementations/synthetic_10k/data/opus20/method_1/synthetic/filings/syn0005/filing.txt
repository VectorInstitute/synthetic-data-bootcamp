PART I


Item 1. Business

Overview

Merqua Land, Inc. ("Merqua," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") is a diversified real estate company that owns, operates, develops and manages commercial and residential properties in metropolitan markets across the United States. Our portfolio includes office buildings, industrial and logistics facilities, neighborhood and community retail centers, and multifamily residential communities. We complement our owned portfolio with a real estate services platform that provides property management, leasing, development management and related services to our consolidated properties and to third-party owners. We are a Delaware corporation headquartered in Chicago, Illinois, and our common stock trades under the ticker symbol "MERQ."

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, our total revenue was $9,373 million. Our revenue consists primarily of rental income under leases with commercial and residential tenants, tenant reimbursements for property operating expenses, fees earned for real estate services, and proceeds from the sale of developed or repositioned assets.

We are organized and taxed as a corporation under Subchapter C of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986, as amended (the "Code"). We have not elected to be taxed as a real estate investment trust. This structure gives us flexibility to retain operating cash flow for reinvestment, to return capital to stockholders through share repurchases or other means, and to conduct services and development businesses without the asset, income and distribution tests that apply to real estate investment trusts. As a taxable corporation, we are subject to U.S. federal, state and local income taxes on our earnings.

We manage our business through three reportable segments: Commercial Properties, Residential Communities, and Real Estate Services and Development. Our chief operating decision maker reviews segment performance and allocates resources primarily on the basis of segment operating results, which reflect property-level revenue less property-level operating expenses, together with fee revenue less the direct costs of providing services. Additional information about our segments appears in the notes to our Consolidated Financial Statements included in Part II, Item 8 of this Annual Report on Form 10-K (this "Annual Report").

Business Strategy

Our strategy is to generate durable, growing cash flow per share by owning well-located real estate in markets with favorable long-term demographic and economic fundamentals, operating that real estate efficiently, and allocating capital with discipline. The principal elements of our strategy are the following:

- Concentrating our portfolio in markets with diversified employment bases, constrained new supply and population growth, and favoring asset types and submarkets where we believe tenant demand will remain resilient across economic cycles.

- Using the scale of our integrated operating platform to drive occupancy, rental rate growth and tenant retention while containing property operating costs through centralized procurement, energy management and shared service functions.

- Recycling capital by selectively disposing of mature, non-core or lower-growth assets and redeploying the proceeds into acquisitions, development and redevelopment projects, debt reduction or the repurchase of our common stock, depending on our assessment of relative risk-adjusted returns.

- Maintaining a conservative balance sheet with access to multiple sources of capital, a manageable debt maturity schedule and sufficient liquidity to pursue opportunities and withstand periods of market dislocation.

- Expanding our third-party services business, which generates fee income that requires limited capital investment and deepens our relationships with property owners, investors and tenants in our markets.

During fiscal 2024, operating conditions in several of our markets remained challenging, particularly in the office sector, where tenants continued to evaluate their space needs in light of hybrid work arrangements. In response, we continued to emphasize leasing and retention across the portfolio, reduced discretionary corporate spending, sold selected non-core assets and continued to repurchase shares of our common stock. We expect to continue evaluating our portfolio composition and capital allocation priorities in light of market conditions.

Principal Products and Services

Our principal offering is the provision of space to commercial and residential tenants under leases of varying duration. Commercial leases generally have terms of several years and may provide for fixed rental escalations, recovery of a tenant's share of real estate taxes, insurance and common area maintenance costs, and, in the case of certain retail tenants, additional rent based on tenant sales. Residential leases generally have terms of approximately one year or less and are priced in response to local market conditions.

In addition to leasing space, we offer a range of services, including:

- Property management, including building operations, maintenance, engineering, security, tenant services and financial reporting for owned and third-party properties.

- Leasing and tenant representation services for office, industrial and retail space.

- Development and construction management services, including site selection, entitlement, design coordination, budgeting and oversight of general contractors.

- Asset management and advisory services for institutional investors and other property owners.

- Ancillary resident and tenant services, including parking, storage, telecommunications access, amenity programs and other building-related services.

We also develop and redevelop properties for our own portfolio, and from time to time we sell completed projects, land parcels and stabilized assets when we believe a sale will produce a more attractive return than continued ownership.

Reportable Segments

Commercial Properties

Our Commercial Properties segment includes office buildings, industrial and logistics facilities, and retail centers that we own and operate, either wholly or through consolidated ventures.

Our office properties consist primarily of multi-tenant buildings located in central business districts and established suburban office markets. We focus on buildings that offer quality amenities, efficient floor plates, convenient access to public transportation and highways, and strong building systems. Over the past several years we have invested in amenity upgrades, building system modernization and spec-suite programs designed to attract tenants seeking high-quality, move-in-ready space. Office tenants include professional services firms, financial institutions, technology companies, healthcare organizations, government agencies and other businesses of various sizes.

Our industrial and logistics properties include bulk distribution warehouses, regional distribution centers, light industrial buildings and flexible-use facilities located near major transportation corridors, ports, airports and population centers. Tenants in these properties include third-party logistics providers, retailers, wholesalers, manufacturers and e-commerce businesses. Demand for well-located industrial space is driven by supply chain reconfiguration, inventory management strategies and the continued growth of online commerce.

Our retail properties consist primarily of grocery-anchored neighborhood centers and community shopping centers that serve the daily needs of surrounding residential populations. Tenants include grocery stores, pharmacies, restaurants, personal and medical services providers, fitness operators and specialty retailers. We seek to maintain a tenant mix weighted toward necessity-based and service-oriented uses, which we believe are less susceptible to competition from online retail.

Revenue in the Commercial Properties segment is driven by occupancy, rental rates, the timing of lease commencements and expirations, the cost of tenant improvements and leasing commissions, and the collectibility of rent.

Residential Communities

Our Residential Communities segment includes multifamily rental communities that we own and operate in urban, urban-adjacent and suburban locations. Our communities include mid-rise and high-rise apartment buildings and garden-style communities, and they offer a variety of unit types and amenity packages tailored to the demographic profile of each submarket.

We operate our residential communities through an integrated platform that handles marketing, leasing, resident services, maintenance, revenue management and capital planning. We use data-driven pricing and leasing tools to set rents and manage lease expirations, and we offer online leasing, payment and service request capabilities. Residents generally sign leases with terms of approximately one year, which allows us to adjust rents relatively quickly in response to changes in market conditions but also exposes us to changes in demand and new supply in our markets.

Revenue in the Residential Communities segment consists primarily of rental income, together with fees and income from parking, storage, utility reimbursement and other resident services. Segment performance depends principally on occupancy, rental rate trends, resident turnover, operating expense management and the level of competing new apartment supply in our submarkets.

Real Estate Services and Development

Our Real Estate Services and Development segment provides property management, leasing, development management, construction management and asset management services to third-party property owners and investors, and conducts our development and redevelopment activities.

Our services clients include institutional investors, pension funds, insurance companies, private equity real estate funds, corporations that own their facilities, public agencies and private owners. Services are generally provided under management, leasing or development agreements that compensate us through fees based on a percentage of property revenue, a fixed amount, transaction value or project cost, and in some cases include incentive fees tied to performance. Many of our management agreements are cancelable by either party on relatively short notice, and we rely on service quality, reporting and client relationships to sustain and grow this business.

Our development activities include ground-up development of multifamily and industrial properties, the redevelopment and repositioning of existing commercial assets, and the entitlement of land for future use or sale. Completed development projects are typically transferred to the Commercial Properties or Residential Communities segment upon stabilization, or are marketed for sale. Development activities expose us to risks relating to construction costs, entitlement timing, financing availability and lease-up, which we seek to manage through pre-leasing, fixed-price or guaranteed maximum price construction contracts, phased development and selective use of joint venture partners.

Customers

Our customers include commercial tenants, residential tenants and third-party clients of our services business. Our commercial tenant base is diversified across industries, tenant sizes and geographic markets. Our residential tenant base consists of a large number of individual residents and households. Our services clients range from large institutional investors to individual property owners.

Where appropriate, we require security deposits, letters of credit or guarantees. Sales and Marketing

We market space in our commercial properties through our in-house leasing professionals and through relationships with third-party brokerage firms that represent tenants. Our leasing teams are organized by market and property type and are responsible for prospecting, responding to tenant requirements, negotiating lease terms and coordinating tenant improvement work. We participate in industry events, maintain relationships with corporate real estate executives and site-selection consultants, and use digital listing platforms and property-specific marketing materials to promote available space.

For our residential communities, we rely primarily on digital marketing, including our own websites, internet listing services, search engine marketing and social media. We offer self-guided and virtual tours, online applications and electronic lease execution, and we use resident referral programs and community events to encourage retention and word-of-mouth referrals. Our centralized marketing and revenue management functions support on-site teams with pricing recommendations, advertising campaigns and lead management tools.

Our services business develops new client relationships through direct business development efforts by our senior professionals, responses to requests for proposals, and referrals from existing clients, lenders, brokers and investors. We emphasize our operating track record, reporting capabilities, market knowledge and breadth of services when competing for new assignments.

Competition

The real estate industry is highly competitive and fragmented. We compete with numerous owners, operators and developers of commercial and residential real estate, including publicly traded real estate investment trusts, other public real estate operating companies, private equity real estate funds, pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds, institutional investors, local and regional developers and individual owners. Many of these competitors own properties similar to ours in the same markets and submarkets.

In leasing, we compete for tenants on the basis of location, rental rates, building quality and amenities, the availability and configuration of space, tenant improvement allowances and other concessions, operating expenses, sustainability features and the quality of property management. Competition for commercial tenants has intensified in the office sector, where reduced demand has led many owners to offer significant concessions. In the residential sector, we compete not only with other apartment owners but also with single-family rental homes, condominiums and for-sale housing, and new apartment supply in certain of our markets has increased competitive pressure on rents and occupancy.

In acquisitions and development, we compete for attractive properties and development sites with buyers that may have greater financial resources, a lower cost of capital or different return requirements. In our services business, we compete with national, regional and local property management, brokerage and development management firms, as well as with owners that manage their properties internally.

We believe our competitive strengths include the quality and location of our portfolio, the scale and integration of our operating platform, our experienced management and leasing teams, our long-standing relationships in our markets and our access to capital.

Seasonality

Certain aspects of our business are affected by seasonal factors. In our Residential Communities segment, leasing activity and resident turnover are typically highest in the late spring and summer months, when households are more likely to relocate, and lowest in the late fall and winter months. As a result, we generally have greater ability to increase rents during peak leasing periods, and we seek to manage lease expirations to align with periods of stronger demand.

Property operating expenses vary seasonally as well. Utility costs are generally higher during the summer cooling season and the winter heating season, and properties in northern markets incur snow removal and related costs during the winter. Repair, maintenance and capital project activity is often concentrated in warmer months.

In our retail properties, tenant sales and percentage rent are generally higher during the year-end holiday season. In our services business, transaction-based leasing and development fees may fluctuate from quarter to quarter based on the timing of lease executions and project milestones. Because of these factors, results for any quarter are not necessarily indicative of results for a full fiscal year.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Continued weakness in office demand resulting from hybrid and remote work could reduce occupancy, rental rates and the value of our office properties.

Office buildings represent a meaningful portion of our Commercial Properties segment. Many employers have adopted hybrid or remote work policies, and tenants are reassessing the amount, configuration and location of the space they lease. As leases expire, tenants may renew for less space, relocate to smaller or newer buildings, sublease excess space or vacate entirely. Sublease space marketed by other tenants competes directly with our vacant space and can depress effective rents in a submarket. During fiscal 2024, office market conditions in several of our markets remained challenging. If they do not improve, or deteriorate further, we may experience lower occupancy, reduced rental rates, larger tenant concession packages and longer downtime between leases.

Sustained weakness in office demand could also reduce the values of our office assets, impair our ability to sell them at acceptable prices, and lead to impairment charges. In addition, lenders and investors have reduced their exposure to the office sector, which could limit the availability and increase the cost of financing for office properties and depress the prices buyers are willing to pay.

Our results depend on economic and real estate conditions in the metropolitan markets where our properties are concentrated.

Our properties are located in metropolitan markets across the United States, and our revenue depends on local employment levels, population trends, business formation and the supply of competing space in those markets. A regional economic slowdown, the relocation or downsizing of major employers, changes in local tax or regulatory policy, rising crime or perceptions of reduced public safety, or declining use of public transportation in urban cores could reduce demand for our properties. To the extent a significant portion of our revenue is derived from a limited number of markets or submarkets, adverse developments in those areas could affect us disproportionately. A broader recession, prolonged high interest rates or reduced consumer spending could reduce demand across all of our property types and our services business at the same time.

We face significant competition for tenants, residents, services clients and investment opportunities.

We compete with numerous owners, operators and developers of office, industrial, retail and multifamily properties, many of which own properties near ours. Some of these competitors have greater financial resources, lower costs of capital or newer buildings. Competitors that own buildings with lower occupancy or that face financial distress may offer rental rates, free rent or tenant improvement allowances that we are unwilling or unable to match. In our services business, we compete with national, regional and local property management, leasing and development firms, some of which have broader geographic coverage or more established relationships with institutional owners. In acquiring and developing properties, we compete with public and private real estate companies, institutional investors and private equity funds, which may increase prices and reduce our expected returns. Any of these competitive pressures could reduce our occupancy, revenue, fee income and growth.

The short-term nature of our residential leases and increases in new apartment supply could pressure rental rates and occupancy in our Residential Communities segment.

Our residential leases generally have terms of approximately one year or less. As a result, our residential rental revenue is exposed to declines in market rents relatively quickly. Several of our markets have experienced elevated levels of new multifamily construction, and newly delivered communities frequently offer significant concessions during lease-up. That competing supply could limit our ability to raise rents, increase resident turnover, and require us to offer concessions or accept lower occupancy. Demand for our apartments could also decline if job growth slows, if residents shift toward single-family rentals or homeownership, or if household formation weakens. Higher turnover increases our costs to prepare, market and lease units.

The financial difficulties, bankruptcy or insolvency of commercial tenants could reduce our revenue and increase our costs.

We depend on commercial tenants to pay rent and to reimburse us for their share of property operating expenses. A tenant experiencing financial difficulty may delay or stop paying rent, seek rent concessions, decline to renew, or file for bankruptcy protection. In a bankruptcy proceeding, a tenant may reject its lease, and our claim for unpaid future rent may be limited and paid only in part, if at all. The loss of an anchor tenant at a retail center could reduce traffic to the center and allow other tenants to terminate their leases or reduce their rent under co-tenancy provisions. Retail tenants also continue to face competition from online commerce, and some industrial tenants are exposed to changes in supply chain strategy, trade policy and consumer demand. Re-leasing vacated space may take considerable time and require significant capital.

Our third-party services business depends on management and service agreements that clients can often terminate on short notice.

Our Real Estate Services and Development segment earns fees under property management, leasing, development and asset management agreements, many of which are cancelable by either party on relatively short notice. Clients may terminate or decline to renew these agreements if they sell the underlying properties, consolidate service providers, bring functions in-house or become dissatisfied with our performance. Fees based on property revenue or transaction value decline when rents, occupancy or transaction volumes fall. Our services business also exposes us to potential liability for errors in property operations, construction oversight or financial reporting performed on behalf of clients, and to conflicts of interest when we serve clients whose properties compete with our own.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Our cost structure is largely fixed, our margins are narrow, and increases in operating costs could significantly reduce our profitability.

A substantial majority of our revenue is consumed by property operating costs, the direct costs of providing services, and depreciation and amortization. Many of these costs, including real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs and maintenance, on-site labor and depreciation, do not decline proportionately when occupancy or revenue falls. As a result, a relatively small decline in revenue can produce a disproportionately larger decline in operating income. During fiscal 2024, our revenue declined modestly while our operating income declined at a significantly faster rate, and our operating margin remained narrow.

Operating costs have risen in recent years because of wage inflation, higher property insurance premiums, increased real estate tax assessments, and higher energy and materials prices. Our ability to recover these increases from tenants is limited. Residential leases generally do not provide for expense recovery. Some commercial leases cap recoverable expenses or use base years. Vacant space generates costs without corresponding reimbursements. Our selling, general and administrative expenses also make up a significant share of our revenue, and our efforts to reduce discretionary corporate spending may not offset increases elsewhere. If we cannot control costs or pass them through to tenants, our results of operations and cash flows could be materially and adversely affected.

Re-leasing space and keeping our properties competitive require substantial capital expenditures.

When leases expire or tenants vacate, we typically incur significant costs for tenant improvements, leasing commissions, free rent and other concessions, and these costs have increased in competitive markets, particularly for office space. Our properties also require ongoing capital investment to replace building systems, update amenities, improve energy efficiency and comply with evolving codes and tenant expectations. Older properties may become functionally obsolete or require substantial renovation to compete with newer buildings. If we do not have sufficient capital, or choose to defer these investments, our properties could become less attractive to tenants and residents, leading to lower occupancy and rental rates. Our capital expenditure requirements also reduce the cash available for debt reduction, share repurchases and new investment.

Our development and redevelopment activities expose us to construction, entitlement, financing and lease-up risks.

We develop multifamily and industrial properties, redevelop and reposition commercial assets, and entitle land for future use or sale. These activities involve risks that include construction cost overruns caused by inflation in labor and materials, contractor defaults, supply chain delays, failure to obtain zoning, permits or other governmental approvals on a timely basis or at all, and community opposition. Financing may not be available on acceptable terms, and completed projects may not lease up at expected rents or within expected time frames. Guaranteed maximum price contracts and pre-leasing do not eliminate these risks. We may abandon projects after incurring significant predevelopment costs, which we would then be required to expense. Development projects generally produce little or no revenue while under construction, and capitalized interest and other carrying costs may exceed our estimates.

Real estate investments are relatively illiquid, and our strategy of recycling capital through dispositions may not succeed.

Our strategy contemplates selling mature, non-core or lower-growth assets and redeploying the proceeds. Real estate is relatively illiquid, and our ability to sell properties quickly, or at prices we consider acceptable, depends on buyer demand, financing availability and conditions in the transaction markets, all of which are outside our control. Transaction volumes for certain property types, particularly office, have been depressed, and buyers have demanded significant price reductions. Contractual restrictions in leases, joint venture agreements, financing agreements or ground leases may limit our ability to sell certain properties. Because we are a taxable corporation, gains on sales may be subject to income tax, which reduces the net proceeds available for redeployment. If we cannot complete dispositions on favorable terms, we may be unable to fund our capital allocation priorities, reduce our exposure to weaker sectors or improve our portfolio quality.

We may be required to record impairment charges on our real estate, goodwill or other long-lived assets.

Property and equipment, goodwill and other non-current assets represent substantially all of our total assets. We evaluate real estate and other long-lived assets for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually. Declines in occupancy, rental rates or market values, increases in capitalization or discount rates, changes in our expected holding periods, or decisions to sell assets could require us to record impairment charges. Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity Risk Management and Strategy

Our business depends on information systems that support leasing, rent collection, property accounting, payroll, resident and tenant services, and the reporting we provide to third-party clients of our services business. We also rely on building technology that controls access, heating, ventilation, air conditioning, elevators, lighting, energy management and life safety functions. We maintain a cybersecurity program intended to identify, assess and manage material risks from cybersecurity threats to these systems and to the data they hold. That data includes personal information of residents, tenants, employees and job applicants, payment information, and confidential information of our clients and business partners.

Framework and Integration with Enterprise Risk Management

We design and evaluate our cybersecurity program with reference to recognized industry frameworks and guidance. This reference helps us organize our activities and measure progress. It does not mean that we satisfy any specific technical standard or certification requirement.

Cybersecurity risk is one of the risk categories in our enterprise risk management program. Cybersecurity risks are identified, rated and reported using the same methodologies, risk registers and escalation channels that we apply to operational, financial, legal and compliance risks. This alignment allows senior management and the Board of Directors to weigh cybersecurity risks against other risks to our business when setting priorities and allocating resources.

Key Elements of Our Program

The principal components of our cybersecurity program include:

- Periodic risk assessments of our corporate information technology environment, property-level systems and cloud-based applications, designed to identify vulnerabilities and evaluate the potential effect of threats on our operations.
- Technical safeguards, including identity and access management, multifactor authentication, endpoint detection and response, network segmentation, encryption of sensitive data, email filtering, data backup and vulnerability management.
- Continuous security monitoring, supported by a managed detection and response provider that reviews alerts and escalates suspicious activity to our internal security team.
- Security awareness training for employees upon hire and on a recurring basis. The training includes simulated phishing exercises and focused instruction for personnel who handle payments, wire transfers and transaction closings, where fraudulent payment instructions are a recurring threat in the real estate industry.
- A written incident response plan that defines roles, escalation procedures, communication protocols and recovery steps, together with periodic tabletop exercises involving members of senior management.
- Policies governing acceptable use, data classification and retention, and the protection of resident and tenant information.

Third-Party Service Providers

We engage external firms to support our program. These firms perform penetration testing, independent assessments of program maturity, threat intelligence services and incident response retainer services. We also rely on vendors for property management software, payment processing, resident and tenant portals, cloud hosting and building technology maintenance. Before engaging vendors that will access our systems or sensitive data, we conduct security due diligence that is scaled to the risk they present. Our contracts with such vendors generally include security and breach notification obligations, and we reassess higher-risk vendors periodically. Because we cannot directly control the security practices of these third parties, a compromise at a vendor could affect us despite these measures.

Building and Operational Technology

Many of our properties use networked building management, access control and surveillance systems, some of which are installed or serviced by outside contractors. We seek to reduce the risk that a compromise of these systems could affect building operations, tenant safety or our corporate network. Our measures include segmenting these systems from corporate networks where practicable, restricting and monitoring remote vendor access, and incorporating security requirements into capital projects and system upgrades.

Assessment of Risks from Cybersecurity Threats

As of the date of this Annual Report, we have not identified any cybersecurity incident or threat, including any resulting from prior incidents, that has materially affected or is reasonably likely to materially affect us, including our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. Like other owners and operators of real estate, we are regularly targeted by phishing, credential theft, ransomware, payment fraud and other malicious activity. A future incident could disrupt property operations, delay rent collection or financial reporting, expose personal information, damage relationships with tenants, residents and services clients, and result in litigation, regulatory penalties and remediation costs. Our cybersecurity program, including its policies, controls and procedures, may not be fully implemented, complied with or effective in preventing or mitigating every incident. For additional information, see "Risk Factors—Cybersecurity incidents or failures of our information or building technology systems could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability" in Part I, Item 1A of this Annual Report.

Cybersecurity Governance

Board of Directors and Audit Committee Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees risk management as a whole and has delegated primary oversight of cybersecurity and information technology risk to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives regular reports from our Chief Information Security Officer and Chief Information Officer. These reports cover the cybersecurity threat environment, the results of risk assessments and third-party testing, the status of program initiatives, and significant incidents, including those that did not have a material effect. The chair of the Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full Board, which also receives periodic cybersecurity briefings from management and outside advisers as part of its ongoing education.

Management's Role

Management is responsible for assessing and managing material risks from cybersecurity threats and for operating our cybersecurity program day to day. A management-level information security steering group oversees program priorities, policy changes and the remediation of identified risks. The group includes our Chief Information Officer, Chief Information Security Officer, General Counsel, Chief Financial Officer and leaders from our property operations and services businesses.

Chief Information Security Officer

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads our information security team and reports to our Chief Information Officer, who reports to our Chief Financial Officer. The Chief Information Security Officer also has direct access to the Audit Committee. Our current Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience in information security leadership roles at large multi-site organizations, including responsibility for security operations, incident response and third-party risk programs. He holds industry-recognized certifications, including Certified Information Systems Security Professional. Members of the information security team hold additional professional certifications in security operations, cloud security and risk management.

Reporting and Escalation

The Chief Information Security Officer and the security team monitor efforts to prevent, detect, mitigate and remediate cybersecurity incidents. They use internal security tools, alerts from our managed detection and response provider, threat intelligence sources and information from government and industry groups. Under our incident response plan, potentially significant incidents are escalated promptly to the Chief Information Officer, the General Counsel and other senior executives. These executives evaluate operational, legal and disclosure implications, including whether an incident is material. The Audit Committee and, where appropriate, the full Board are informed of significant incidents and the actions taken in response.

Overview

Merqua Land, Inc. is a diversified real estate company. We own, operate, develop and manage office, industrial and logistics, retail and multifamily residential properties in metropolitan markets across the United States. We also operate a real estate services platform that serves our consolidated properties and third-party owners. We report results through three segments: Commercial Properties, Residential Communities, and Real Estate Services and Development. Our revenue consists principally of the following:

- Rental income under commercial and residential leases.
- Tenant reimbursements of property operating expenses.
- Fees for property management, leasing, development management and asset management services.
- Proceeds from sales of developed or repositioned assets.

We are a taxable C corporation and have not elected to be taxed as a real estate investment trust. As a result, we are not subject to minimum distribution requirements. We can retain operating cash flow for reinvestment, debt reduction or share repurchases, and our earnings are subject to federal, state and local income taxes.

Our business is capital intensive. Depreciation and amortization of our real estate, real estate-related intangible assets and other long-lived assets is significant, and a substantial portion of our costs is fixed in the short term. These costs include real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, on-site personnel and depreciation. Because of this cost structure, our margins are narrow, and modest changes in revenue can have a proportionately larger effect on operating income and net income. Cash flow from operations, by contrast, has historically been substantially greater than net income because of large non-cash depreciation and amortization charges.

Business Environment

Operating conditions in fiscal 2024 were mixed across our property types and markets.

Office remained the most challenging sector. Many tenants continued to reassess their space requirements in light of hybrid work arrangements. Renewal decisions often involved space reductions, and sublease availability in several submarkets competed with our vacant space. Tenants favored high-quality, well-amenitized buildings, which benefited our renovated assets, but leasing concessions and tenant improvement costs remained elevated.

Industrial and logistics demand moderated from the very strong levels of prior years. New deliveries in several markets increased available supply, although well-located facilities near population centers and transportation corridors continued to lease steadily.

Our grocery-anchored and necessity-oriented retail centers performed relatively consistently.

In our Residential Communities segment, elevated deliveries of new apartment supply in a number of submarkets limited rent growth and led to increased use of concessions, particularly where competing properties were in lease-up.

In the capital markets, transaction volumes for commercial real estate remained below historical norms for much of the year. Elevated borrowing costs and uncertainty about the direction of interest rates and property values continued to affect investor activity and lender appetite, particularly for office assets. This environment affected the volume and timing of our asset dispositions and the fee activity in our services business tied to leasing and transactions.

In response, we focused on the following during fiscal 2024:

- Leasing and tenant retention.
- Disciplined control of corporate and property operating costs.
- Selective disposition of non-core assets.
- Reductions in debt.
- Continued repurchases of our common stock.

Fiscal 2024 Compared with Fiscal 2023

Revenue

Revenue decreased 2.2% to $9,373 million in fiscal 2024 from $9,587 million in fiscal 2023. Several factors contributed to the decrease.

The most significant factor was lower rental income in our office portfolio. Occupancy declined at certain buildings where tenants vacated or renewed for less space at lease expiration. In addition, free rent and other concessions granted on new and renewal leases reduced recognized revenue in the period.

Revenue was also reduced by the sale of selected non-core properties during fiscal 2024 and late fiscal 2023. These sales removed the rental income of those properties from our results for all or part of the current year. They were consistent with our capital recycling strategy, and their proceeds were applied to debt reduction, share repurchases and reinvestment in our portfolio.

Within our Residential Communities segment, rental revenue was broadly stable. Renewal rate increases at many communities were largely offset by greater use of concessions and modestly lower occupancy in submarkets with significant new apartment supply.

Our industrial and logistics properties benefited from rent increases on leases that rolled to current market rates during the year. Our grocery-anchored retail centers generated relatively consistent rental income. Together, these partially offset the declines described above.

In our Real Estate Services and Development segment, fee revenue tied to leasing activity and transaction volume was affected by subdued capital markets activity. Revenue from property management agreements was comparatively steady. Proceeds from sales of developed assets and land parcels vary from year to year depending on project completion and market conditions, and they did not offset the declines in rental revenue.

Tenant reimbursement revenue generally moves with recoverable property operating expenses and with occupancy. It declined modestly as a result of lower occupancy in portions of our office portfolio and the disposition of properties.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue decreased to $7,118 million in fiscal 2024 from $7,273 million in fiscal 2023. It consists primarily of:

- Property operating expenses, including real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs and maintenance, and on-site personnel.
- Direct costs of providing services to third-party clients.
- The cost basis of developed assets and land sold.
- Depreciation and amortization of our real estate and related intangible assets.

The decrease primarily reflected the properties we sold, which removed their operating expenses and depreciation from our results. It also reflected cost containment measures across our operating platform, including centralized procurement, energy management initiatives and staffing efficiencies. These reductions were partially offset by higher real estate taxes and property insurance premiums in a number of our markets. Labor costs for building operations and maintenance personnel also continued to increase, as did depreciation on recently completed capital projects, tenant improvements and building upgrades placed in service.

Gross profit was $2,255 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $2,314 million in fiscal 2023. Gross margin was 24.1% in both years. Because cost of revenue declined broadly in line with revenue, gross margin was unchanged despite the decline in office rental income. Many components of property operating expense do not decline proportionately with occupancy, so further declines in office occupancy could pressure gross margin in future periods.

Critical Accounting Estimates

We prepare our Consolidated Financial Statements in conformity with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. Doing so requires us to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses. We consider an accounting estimate critical if it involves a significant level of estimation uncertainty and has had, or is reasonably likely to have, a material effect on our financial condition or results of operations. We base our estimates on historical experience, current market conditions and other assumptions we believe are reasonable, and actual results may differ. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to our Consolidated Financial Statements.

Impairment of Real Estate and Other Long-Lived Assets

We review our real estate and other long-lived assets for impairment whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying amount may not be recoverable. Indicators include:

- Sustained declines in occupancy or cash flow.
- Significant adverse changes in market conditions.
- A change in our expected holding period.
- A decision to sell an asset.

When indicators are present, we compare the asset's carrying amount with the estimated undiscounted future cash flows expected from its use and eventual disposition. If the carrying amount is not recoverable, we record an impairment charge equal to the excess of the carrying amount over estimated fair value. Estimating future cash flows and fair value requires significant judgment about market rents, occupancy, leasing costs, operating expenses, capital expenditures, holding periods, terminal capitalization rates and discount rates.

Our office properties are the most sensitive to these estimates in the current environment. A shortening of expected holding periods, as can occur when we identify assets for potential disposition under our capital recycling strategy, can significantly increase the likelihood of impairment. Changes in these assumptions could result in material impairment charges in future periods.

Goodwill

Goodwill was $6,767 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level at least annually, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the fair value of a reporting unit may be below its carrying amount. We estimate the fair value of reporting units using discounted cash flow analyses and market-based approaches. These require assumptions about future revenue growth, operating margins, capital requirements, discount rates and market multiples. When we dispose of a portion of a reporting unit, we allocate goodwill to the disposed business based on relative fair values, which also requires judgment.

Our annual test did not result in an impairment for fiscal 2024. However, adverse changes in the assumptions above could result in impairment charges in future periods. Such changes could include sustained weakness in office demand, lower fee revenue in our services business or higher discount rates.

Allocation of Purchase Price of Acquired Real Estate

When we acquire properties, we allocate the purchase price among land, buildings, improvements and identifiable intangible assets and liabilities based on their relative fair values. Intangibles include in-place leases and above- and below-market leases. These allocations require estimates of market rents, lease-up periods, carrying costs, replacement costs and discount rates. They affect the amount and timing of depreciation and amortization expense and of rental revenue recognized from the amortization of above- and below-market lease intangibles.

Depreciation and Capitalization

We depreciate buildings, improvements and other long-lived assets on a straight-line basis over their estimated useful lives. Depreciation and amortization was $1,619 million in fiscal 2024, so changes in estimated useful lives could significantly affect our results of operations.

We also capitalize certain costs during the development, redevelopment and leasing of properties. These include interest, real estate taxes, insurance and internal personnel costs directly attributable to these activities. Determining when capitalization begins and ends, and which costs qualify, requires judgment.

Revenue Recognition and Collectibility of Rent

We recognize rental revenue from leases with fixed escalations on a straight-line basis over the lease term. For each lease, we assess whether collection of substantially all remaining lease payments is probable, considering the tenant's payment history, financial condition, industry and current economic conditions. If collection is not probable, we limit revenue recognized to cash received and write off related receivables, including straight-line rent receivables.

These assessments require significant judgment, particularly for tenants experiencing financial difficulty. Changes in our assessments could materially affect our rental revenue. Estimates are also required for tenant reimbursement revenue that is billed on an estimated basis and reconciled after year end.

Income Taxes

As a taxable corporation, we recognize deferred tax assets and liabilities for the expected future tax consequences of temporary differences between the financial reporting and tax bases of our assets and liabilities. We evaluate the need for valuation allowances on deferred tax assets based on our projections of future taxable income, reversals of temporary differences and available tax planning strategies. We recognize the benefit of uncertain tax positions only when it is more likely than not that the position will be sustained on examination.

Because our pre-tax income is small relative to our operations, changes in these estimates can have a significant effect on our effective tax rate, which was 27.3% in fiscal 2024 compared with 22.5% in fiscal 2023.
