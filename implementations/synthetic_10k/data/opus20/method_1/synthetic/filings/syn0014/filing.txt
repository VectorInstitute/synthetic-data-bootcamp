Item 1. Business

Overview

Kator Minerals, Inc. ("Kator," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") extracts, processes and distributes mineral-based materials. Our products are used in construction, infrastructure, manufacturing, agriculture and environmental applications. We operate a network of quarries, pits, mines, processing plants, distribution yards, rail terminals and port facilities. Through this network we supply crushed stone, sand and gravel, lime and limestone products, industrial and specialty minerals, and related processing and logistics services. Our customers range from public agencies and construction contractors to steel producers, glass and chemical manufacturers, utilities and agricultural distributors.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, we generated total revenue of $11,823 million. Our business is built on long-lived mineral deposits located near the markets they serve. Our materials are heavy relative to their value, so transportation costs often determine how far a product can travel economically. We therefore manage our operations as a series of local and regional markets. Each market is supported by shared corporate functions, centralized procurement, common safety and environmental standards, and an integrated logistics organization.

Kator was incorporated in Delaware and has its principal executive offices in Reston, Virginia. Our common stock trades under the ticker symbol "KRAT." Unless the context requires otherwise, references to Kator include its consolidated subsidiaries.

Our Strategy

Our strategy is to serve as a reliable, long-term source of essential minerals in the markets where we operate. We pursue this strategy in four main ways.

Developing a durable reserve base. We seek to control mineral reserves of sufficient quality and quantity to support decades of production at our principal sites. We periodically acquire adjacent land or mineral rights, obtain permits for expansions, and reassess reserve estimates as geological information improves. Information about our mineral properties, including summary disclosure of mineral reserves and resources prepared in accordance with the applicable rules of the Securities and Exchange Commission ("SEC"), is included in Item 2, Properties.

Integrating extraction, processing and delivery. We believe that combining resource ownership, processing and logistics improves our cost position, the consistency of our product quality, and the dependability of supply to our customers. Where economically justified, we own or contract for the rail cars, barges, trucks and terminal capacity needed to move products from our production sites to regional distribution points.

Growing through disciplined acquisitions. Acquisitions have been an important part of our development. We evaluate businesses that extend our geographic footprint, add complementary product lines or deepen our reserve position in existing markets. During fiscal 2024, we completed acquisitions that broadened our operations in several regional markets. We are integrating these businesses into our operating, safety and reporting systems. We intend to keep evaluating acquisition opportunities and to apply consistent criteria for strategic fit, reserve quality, integration risk and expected returns.

Improving operating efficiency. Our cost structure is heavily influenced by energy, labor, maintenance, freight and equipment utilization. We are pursuing initiatives to reduce these costs and to improve margins over time, including plant automation, preventive maintenance programs, fleet optimization, and consolidation of redundant facilities following acquisitions.

Principal Products and Services

Our principal products and services fall into the following categories.

- Construction aggregates. These include crushed stone, sand and gravel, and recycled concrete and asphalt. They are used as base materials in highways, streets, bridges, airports, railroads, residential and commercial foundations, and drainage and erosion control projects. Aggregates are also the primary raw material in ready-mixed concrete, asphalt and precast concrete products.

- Downstream construction materials. In certain markets we produce and sell ready-mixed concrete, asphalt mix and concrete block. These operations consume aggregates from our own quarries and let us participate in more of the construction value chain.

- Lime and limestone products. These include high-calcium and dolomitic quicklime, hydrated lime, and ground and pulverized limestone. Customers use them in steelmaking, flue gas desulfurization at power plants, water and wastewater treatment, pulp and paper production, soil stabilization, chemical manufacturing and agriculture.

- Industrial and specialty minerals. These include industrial sand, ground calcium carbonate, clays, feldspar and other processed minerals. They are sold to manufacturers of glass, ceramics, building products, coatings, plastics, filtration media and foundry products, and to the energy sector.

- Processing, transportation and terminal services. These include rail and barge transportation, transloading, storage, blending and toll processing. We provide them both for our own products and, in certain locations, for third parties.

Construction Materials

The Construction Materials segment is our largest segment by revenue. It consists of our aggregates operations and our downstream ready-mixed concrete, asphalt and concrete products businesses. The segment operates quarries, sand and gravel pits, and processing plants, along with associated distribution yards. Its locations are concentrated in metropolitan and fast-growing regional markets.

Demand for this segment's products depends on public infrastructure spending, private non-residential construction and residential construction in each local market. Public infrastructure demand is supported by federal surface transportation programs and by state and local transportation funding. Those programs finance construction and maintenance of highways, bridges, transit systems and airports. Non-residential demand comes from projects such as warehouses, data centers, manufacturing facilities, energy projects and commercial buildings. Residential demand follows single-family and multi-family housing starts as well as land development activity.

Aggregates are produced by drilling and blasting rock, then crushing, screening and washing it into graded products that meet customer and governmental specifications. Sand and gravel are typically extracted from unconsolidated deposits and then washed and screened. Because aggregates are costly to transport relative to their selling price, our quarries generally serve customers within a limited radius. Where rail, barge or ship transport is available, we can reach more distant markets. We believe the location, permitted status and remaining life of our reserves are significant competitive advantages, given the difficulty of obtaining permits for new quarries near population centers.

Our downstream operations use aggregates produced by the segment. They supply finished materials directly to contractors on paving, site development and building projects. In certain markets the segment also performs paving and related construction services, primarily under public contracts.

Industrial Minerals

The Industrial Minerals segment produces lime, limestone products and specialty minerals. It operates lime kilns, grinding and milling facilities, hydrating plants and mineral processing plants. These facilities are generally located near high-purity mineral deposits or near major industrial customers.

Lime is made by heating high-purity limestone in kilns to drive off carbon dioxide, producing quicklime. Some quicklime is then combined with water to produce hydrated lime. Our lime products serve as fluxing agents in steelmaking, reagents for removing sulfur dioxide and other pollutants from combustion emissions, pH control and softening agents in water treatment, and stabilizing agents in road construction. Our ground and pulverized limestone products are used as fillers and extenders in manufactured goods, as agricultural soil amendments, and in animal feed.

Our specialty minerals are processed to tight chemical and physical specifications. Typical specifications cover particle size distribution, brightness, purity and moisture content. These products are often qualified by customers through extended testing. Once qualified, they tend to be supplied under multi-year agreements. Customers include manufacturers of glass, ceramics, roofing, coatings, plastics, rubber and filtration products, as well as foundries and certain energy sector users.

The Industrial Minerals segment is more capital and energy intensive than our aggregates operations. Its results depend more heavily on the operating rates of the industrial customers it serves, particularly in steel, power generation and chemicals. Many of the segment's sales are made under supply contracts with volume commitments and price adjustment provisions linked to energy and other input costs.

Logistics and Services

The Logistics and Services segment manages the transportation, terminal and processing assets that link our production sites with customers. These assets include rail car fleets, rail-served terminals, barge loading facilities, port terminals and distribution yards. The segment moves products from both of our other segments to regional markets. It also provides transportation, transloading, storage and toll processing services to third parties on a contractual basis.

We believe this integrated logistics capability lets us serve customers beyond the reach of truck-only delivery and keep supply steady during periods of peak demand. Through the same capability we can respond to shifts in regional demand by redirecting product among our facilities. Segment results depend on shipment volumes, terminal utilization, contract pricing, fuel costs and the performance of third-party rail and marine carriers.

Sales and Marketing

We sell our products mainly through our own sales organization, which is aligned by segment and geographic market. In Construction Materials, local sales representatives and plant managers maintain relationships with contractors, producers and public agencies. They also prepare quotations and bids on specific projects. Pricing generally reflects local market conditions, product specifications, delivery requirements and project scope. Sales are typically made on an FOB plant basis or a delivered basis.

In Industrial Minerals, our sales organization includes technical sales personnel and applications engineers. They work with customers to qualify products, optimize product performance in customer processes and develop customized blends. Sales are frequently made under annual or multi-year agreements, which may include provisions to adjust prices in response to changes in energy, freight and other costs. We also sell certain products through distributors, particularly in agricultural and smaller industrial markets.

Our Logistics and Services segment markets its capacity directly to producers and shippers of bulk commodities. Arrangements are typically governed by contracts covering volumes, rates, service levels and term.

Our marketing activities are focused on customer service, product quality, delivery dependability and technical support, rather than on broad advertising. We use digital ordering, ticketing and delivery tracking tools in many of our Construction Materials markets to improve the customer experience and the efficiency of our operations.

Competition

We face competition in each of our segments and markets. The construction materials industry in the United States is fragmented. Participants range from large, diversified building materials companies with national or multinational operations to regional producers and small, independently owned single-quarry operators. Because transportation costs limit the economic shipping range of most aggregates, competition in Construction Materials is mainly local. The number and size of competitors in a given market vary widely. Competitive factors include the location of reserves relative to customers, price, product quality and consistency, delivery capability and reliability, and customer service. In certain markets, vertically integrated competitors that operate their own aggregates, concrete and asphalt businesses compete with us across several product lines. We also compete, in some applications, with recycled materials and alternative products.

In Industrial Minerals, we compete with other lime producers, specialty mineral processors and, in some applications, producers of substitute materials. Some of these competitors are units of larger, diversified chemical or materials companies, and some have greater financial resources than we do. Competitive factors include proximity to customers, product purity and performance, technical service, reliability of supply and price. Lime and certain specialty minerals are costly to transport, so competition in these products is also largely regional. However, some higher-value specialty products are marketed nationally and face competition from imports.

In Logistics and Services, we compete with independent terminal operators, third-party logistics providers, and the logistics capabilities of other producers. Competitive factors include terminal location, rail and water access, service quality and price.

We believe that our reserve base, the permitted status of our operations, the breadth of our product offering and our integrated logistics network position us well against competitors in our core markets. These factors are discussed further in Item 1A, Risk Factors.

Seasonality

Our business is seasonal, and our operating results are affected by weather. Construction activity, and with it demand for aggregates, ready-mixed concrete and asphalt, generally declines in winter months in many of our markets, particularly in northern regions where freezing temperatures and snow restrict paving and other outdoor work. Heavy rainfall, flooding, hurricanes, droughts and extreme heat can also disrupt production, shipments and customer construction schedules in any season. As a result, revenue and operating results in our Construction Materials segment are typically lower in the first and fourth quarters than in the second and third quarters.

Demand in our Industrial Minerals segment is generally less seasonal because many of its customers operate continuously throughout the year. Agricultural lime and limestone products have seasonal demand patterns tied to planting and harvesting cycles. Demand for flue gas treatment reagents can vary with electricity demand, which tends to peak in summer and winter. Volumes in our Logistics and Services segment generally follow those of our other segments and are subject to weather-related disruptions in rail and marine transportation.

We typically schedule major maintenance at our plants and kilns during periods of lower seasonal demand. Working capital needs generally build in advance of the peak construction season.

Permitting, Land Use and Reclamation

Opening a new quarry or mine, or expanding an existing one, generally requires permits and approvals from multiple governmental authorities. These may include zoning and land use approvals, mining permits, air and water permits, and, in some cases, environmental impact reviews. The permitting process can be lengthy and costly, and it often draws opposition from local residents or community groups. Permits we obtain may contain conditions that limit hours of operation, production volumes, truck traffic or the depth and extent of extraction.

State laws generally require us to reclaim mined land when mining is completed. Reclamation may involve regrading, revegetation and stabilization of slopes and water features. Many jurisdictions also require financial assurance for these obligations, such as surety bonds or letters of credit. We record asset retirement obligations for the estimated cost of reclaiming our properties. We regularly update reclamation plans in consultation with the relevant authorities.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Our results depend heavily on construction activity in the local and regional markets we serve, and a decline in that activity could reduce demand for our products.

Our Construction Materials segment, our largest segment by revenue, sells aggregates, ready-mixed concrete, asphalt and concrete products. Demand for these products depends on public infrastructure, private non-residential and residential construction in each market where we operate. These sectors respond to interest rates, credit availability, population and employment trends, business investment decisions, housing affordability and general economic conditions. Our materials cannot be transported economically over long distances, so we cannot readily move volume from a weak market to a strong one. A downturn concentrated in one or more of our principal metropolitan or regional markets could therefore reduce our shipments, pricing and plant utilization even if national construction activity remains stable.

Non-residential construction activity has recently been supported by projects such as warehouses, data centers, manufacturing facilities and energy installations. A slowdown in any of these project categories, or the cancellation or deferral of large projects in markets where we have concentrated operations, could reduce demand faster than we can adjust our cost structure. Weakness in residential construction would also reduce demand for aggregates and downstream products used in site development, foundations and paving.

Reductions or delays in governmental funding for highways, bridges and other public infrastructure could adversely affect our sales.

A substantial part of our construction-related demand comes from projects funded by federal surface transportation programs and by state and local transportation budgets. These programs depend on legislative authorization, annual appropriations, fuel tax and other dedicated revenue sources, and the fiscal condition of state and local governments. Federal programs may not be reauthorized when current authorizations expire, or may be reauthorized at lower funding levels or with different priorities. Delays in appropriations, budget shortfalls, competing spending priorities or changes in political leadership could defer or cancel projects in our markets. Public agencies may also face higher construction costs that reduce the amount of work their budgets support. Any of these developments could reduce volumes in our Construction Materials segment and the related transportation volumes handled by our Logistics and Services segment.

Our Industrial Minerals segment depends on the operating rates of cyclical industrial customers, and structural changes in those industries could permanently reduce demand.

Our lime, limestone and specialty mineral products are sold mainly to steel producers, electric utilities, chemical manufacturers, glass and ceramics producers, pulp and paper mills, and other industrial users. Many of these industries are cyclical. Reduced operating rates at customer facilities directly reduce the volumes they purchase under our supply agreements, many of which adjust with customer requirements. In addition, some demand rests on technologies and fuel sources that are changing. Retirements of coal-fired generating units reduce demand for lime and limestone used in flue gas desulfurization. Shifts in steelmaking technology, the closure or relocation of customer plants, and customers' substitution of alternative materials could permanently reduce demand for our products in particular regions. Because kilns and processing plants are capital intensive and located near specific deposits or customers, we may not be able to redeploy idled capacity, and we could be required to record impairment charges or incur closure costs.

We face significant competition, and pricing pressure could further compress our already narrow margins.

We compete with national, regional and local producers of aggregates, construction materials, lime and industrial minerals, as well as with transportation and terminal operators. In many local markets, competition depends on proximity to customers, product quality, service, reliability of supply and price. New entrants, the expansion of permitted reserves by competitors, imported materials delivered by water, and recycled or substitute materials could increase competitive pressure. Public construction work is generally awarded through competitive bidding, which can limit our ability to pass through cost increases on fixed-price contracts. Our gross margin is narrow relative to our revenue, so even modest declines in pricing or modest increases in costs that we cannot recover could have a disproportionate effect on our operating results.

Weather conditions and seasonality cause our results to fluctuate and can reduce our profitability in any given period.

Construction and many industrial activities slow during periods of cold, snow, heavy rainfall, flooding or extreme heat, and our sales volumes are typically lower in the winter months in many of our markets. Prolonged adverse weather during the peak construction season can defer or cancel shipments and projects. Because a large portion of our production costs, including depreciation, labor and maintenance, is fixed or semi-fixed, lower volumes reduce our margins. Drought conditions can restrict the water available for washing aggregates and for certain processing operations. Low water levels on inland waterways can limit barge loading capacity. Results for any quarter are therefore not necessarily indicative of results for a full year.

Our acquisition strategy exposes us to integration, valuation and execution risks.

Acquisitions are an important part of our strategy, and during fiscal 2024 we completed acquisitions that broadened our presence in several regional markets. Acquisitions involve risks that could prevent us from realizing the anticipated benefits, including:

- difficulties integrating operations, safety programs, information systems, internal controls and personnel;
- the diversion of management attention from existing operations;
- the loss of key employees or customers of acquired businesses;
- reserve quantities, permit status or asset condition that differ from our expectations;
- environmental, reclamation, tax or other liabilities that were not identified during due diligence or are not covered by indemnities; and
- conditions imposed by antitrust authorities, including required divestitures.

We have funded recent acquisitions with a combination of cash on hand, borrowings and equity issuances. Future acquisitions may require us to incur additional debt or issue additional shares. If acquired businesses do not perform as expected, we may not generate returns sufficient to justify the price paid or to service the related financing, and we could be required to record impairment charges against the goodwill and other assets we recorded.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Increases in the cost of energy, fuel and other inputs could reduce our profitability, particularly given our narrow margins.

Our operations consume large quantities of diesel fuel, electricity, natural gas, coal, petroleum coke, explosives, liquid asphalt, cement, steel, tires and replacement parts. Lime kilns in particular are energy intensive, and fuel represents a major component of lime production cost. Transportation by truck, rail and barge also depends on fuel prices. Prices for these inputs can be volatile and are affected by global commodity markets, geopolitical events, supply disruptions, regulatory changes and inflation. Many of our industrial supply contracts include price adjustment provisions linked to energy and other input costs, but these provisions may operate with a lag, may not cover all cost categories and may not fully offset cost increases. In our construction businesses, fixed-price bids and contracts limit our ability to recover cost increases during the term of a project. Our cost of revenue is high relative to our revenue, so unrecovered input cost inflation could quickly erode our gross profit.

Our business depends on our ability to maintain and replenish permitted mineral reserves, and our reserve estimates may prove inaccurate.

Our long-term viability depends on access to economically recoverable reserves of suitable quality at locations near our markets. Obtaining zoning approvals and permits for new quarries, mines and expansions has become increasingly difficult, time consuming and costly, especially near population centers. Local opposition, environmental review requirements, water management restrictions and land use changes may delay or prevent the development of new reserves or the expansion of existing sites. Some of our reserves are held under leases that may expire, may not be renewed on acceptable terms or may require increasing royalty payments.

Our reserve estimates rest on geological data, engineering assumptions and economic factors, including prices, costs and expected recovery rates. These estimates involve inherent uncertainty. Actual mineral quality, quantity, recoverability or economic viability may differ materially from our estimates. Downward revisions could shorten the useful lives of our properties, increase depletion expense, trigger impairment charges and require us to acquire replacement reserves sooner and at greater cost than planned.

We rely on third-party rail and marine carriers and on our own logistics assets, and disruptions to transportation could impair our ability to serve customers.

Our integrated logistics network, managed largely through our Logistics and Services segment, depends on third-party railroads and marine carriers to move products from our production sites to terminals and customers. Rail service disruptions, labor disputes at carriers, congestion, car shortages, rate increases, infrastructure failures, changes in carrier operating practices and adverse waterway conditions could delay shipments, increase our costs and cause us to lose sales. In many locations we rely on a single rail carrier, which limits our negotiating leverage. We also own or lease rail car fleets, terminals and port facilities that require ongoing maintenance and capital investment. Any of these assets may become underutilized if volumes decline. Shortages of qualified commercial truck drivers could limit our delivery capacity and increase our costs.

Unplanned outages, equipment failures and catastrophic events could interrupt our operations and adversely affect our results.

Our operations depend on the continued performance of crushing and screening plants, kilns, mills, hydrating plants, mobile mining equipment, terminals and related infrastructure. Mechanical failures, fires, explosions, slope or pit wall failures, flooding, dam or impoundment failures, power outages and similar events could halt production, damage assets and injure people. Hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, wildfires, earthquakes, pandemics, acts of terrorism or war, and other catastrophic events could disrupt our facilities, those of our customers and suppliers, and the transportation networks we use. Some of our products are produced at a limited number of facilities, and an extended outage at one of those facilities could prevent us from meeting contractual supply commitments. Our insurance may not cover all losses, may be subject to substantial deductibles and may become more expensive or unavailable on acceptable terms.

Accidents and health and safety incidents could result in injuries, fatalities, liabilities and reputational harm.

Mining, blasting, processing, heavy equipment operation, kiln operations and transportation are inherently hazardous. Despite our safety programs, serious injuries or fatalities may occur among our employees, contractors or members of the public. Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Risk Management and Strategy

We maintain processes to identify, assess and manage material risks from cybersecurity threats to our information technology systems and to the operational technology at our quarries, lime kilns, processing plants, terminals and logistics operations. Our cybersecurity program is part of our enterprise risk management process. Through that process, management from operations, finance, legal, procurement and information technology assess risks that could affect our ability to produce and deliver materials, protect data and meet our obligations to customers and regulators. We draw on widely recognized cybersecurity frameworks and industry practices in designing our controls. We do not certify compliance with any particular standard.

Our controls combine preventive, detective and responsive measures. They include access management and multifactor authentication, network segmentation between business and plant control systems, endpoint protection, vulnerability management, data backup and recovery capabilities, and continuous security monitoring. Employees with access to our systems complete periodic security awareness training, including simulated phishing exercises. Personnel who operate automated plant and logistics equipment receive additional guidance on operational technology risks. We maintain a written incident response plan. It sets out how we classify, escalate, contain, investigate and recover from cybersecurity incidents, and how we evaluate potential legal, contractual and disclosure obligations. We test the plan periodically through simulation exercises involving members of management.

We use outside firms to supplement our internal capabilities. These firms provide managed monitoring services, penetration testing, forensic support and independent assessments of our program's maturity. Our internal audit function also reviews selected cybersecurity controls as part of its risk-based audit plan.

We rely on third-party vendors for software, cloud hosting, equipment control systems and other services. We assess the security practices of vendors that have access to our systems or sensitive data, considering the nature of the service and the data involved. Where appropriate, our contracts include security, confidentiality and incident notification requirements. We monitor significant vendors over the course of the relationship. When we acquire businesses, we assess their information and operational technology environments and plan their migration onto our security standards as part of integration. Until that work is complete, acquired systems may present elevated risk.

We have experienced cybersecurity events in the ordinary course of business. To date, none has materially affected us, including our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. However, threats continue to evolve, and we cannot be certain that our processes will prevent or detect every incident or limit its effects. For further discussion, see Item 1A, Risk Factors.

Governance

Our Board of Directors oversees enterprise risk and has delegated primary oversight of cybersecurity risk to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives reports from management on cybersecurity at least annually and more often as circumstances warrant. These reports cover the threat environment, the results of assessments and testing, the status of program initiatives, the integration of acquired operations and significant incidents. The Audit Committee reports to the full Board on these matters, and the full Board periodically receives cybersecurity updates directly from management.

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads the day-to-day management of our cybersecurity program, supported by a dedicated security team and outside service providers. The Chief Information Security Officer reports to our Chief Information Officer, who reports to our Chief Financial Officer. Our current Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience leading security programs at industrial and asset-intensive companies, including responsibility for protecting operational technology environments, and holds professional security certifications. The Chief Information Officer has broad experience managing enterprise technology, infrastructure and related risk.

Under our incident response plan, significant incidents are escalated promptly to the Chief Information Officer and to an executive response team that includes our General Counsel and Chief Financial Officer. This team determines whether further escalation to the Audit Committee, the Board or external parties is required.

Overview

Business Overview

Kator Minerals, Inc. extracts, processes and distributes mineral-based materials used in construction, infrastructure, manufacturing, agriculture and environmental applications. We operate a network of quarries, pits, mines, processing plants, lime kilns, distribution yards, rail terminals and port facilities. We manage the business through three reportable segments:

- Construction Materials. Aggregates operations and downstream ready-mixed concrete, asphalt and concrete products businesses.
- Industrial Minerals. Lime, limestone products and specialty minerals.
- Logistics and Services. Transportation, terminal, storage and toll processing services.

Our products are heavy relative to their selling price, and freight costs largely determine how far they can travel economically. We therefore run our operations as a portfolio of local and regional markets, supported by centralized procurement, shared safety and environmental standards, and an integrated logistics organization. Segment revenue and segment profitability measures appear in Note 18, Segment Information, of the Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements.

Fiscal 2024 Highlights

Fiscal 2024 brought substantial revenue growth, driven mainly by acquisitions and supported by pricing. Gross margin improved modestly. Profitability nevertheless remained below the level needed to cover our operating expenses and financing costs. Key results compared with fiscal 2023:

- Revenue increased 13.9% to $11,823 million, from $10,379 million.
- Gross profit increased to $726 million from $556 million. Gross margin rose to 6.1% from 5.4%.
- Operating loss narrowed 25.3% to $210 million, from $281 million. Operating margin improved to negative 1.8% from negative 2.7%.

Business Environment and Trends

Demand in our Construction Materials segment depends on public infrastructure, private non-residential and residential construction in our local markets. In fiscal 2024, publicly funded highway, bridge and transit work, supported by federal surface transportation programs and state and local funding, gave a steady base of demand in many of our markets. Non-residential activity was mixed. Projects such as data centers, manufacturing facilities and energy installations supported shipments in certain regions, while other commercial construction categories were softer. Residential activity remained constrained in several markets by elevated mortgage rates and housing affordability pressures.

In our Industrial Minerals segment, volumes depend on the operating rates of steel producers, electric utilities, chemical manufacturers and other industrial customers. Demand in fiscal 2024 was generally steady in water treatment, chemical and specialty applications. Demand tied to coal-fired power generation continued to face long-term pressure from unit retirements. Contractual price adjustment provisions tied to energy and other input costs partially offset cost changes, typically with a lag.

Our cost structure remains heavily influenced by energy, labor, maintenance, freight and equipment utilization. Inflation in labor, repair parts and contracted services continued during fiscal 2024. Some energy inputs were less volatile than in prior periods. Because our cost of revenue is high relative to revenue, small changes in pricing or unit costs have a large effect on gross profit. Improving margins through pricing discipline, operating efficiency and the integration of acquired operations remains management's central financial priority.

During fiscal 2024 we completed acquisitions that broadened our operations in several regional markets. These acquisitions added revenue and production capacity. They also increased depreciation, depletion and amortization, integration costs and interest expense. Integrating these businesses into our operating, safety, information and control systems continues into fiscal 2025.

Capital expenditures in fiscal 2024 were well below depreciation and amortization of $1,062 million. This gap partly reflects the step-up of acquired assets to fair value and the amortization of acquired intangible assets, which raise depreciation and amortization without a matching current cash outlay. It also reflects our decision to prioritize spending while integrating acquisitions and managing leverage. Sustained spending at levels well below the consumption of our asset base is not consistent with maintaining productive capacity over the long term. We expect future capital spending to reflect the maintenance needs of our enlarged asset base, safety and environmental requirements, and projects with attractive expected returns. We evaluate capital projects individually and may adjust spending in response to market conditions and liquidity.

Business Combinations

We account for acquisitions using the acquisition method. Identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed are recorded at fair value as of the acquisition date, and any excess of consideration over those fair values is recorded as goodwill. Valuing mineral reserves, property, plant and equipment, customer relationships, supply contracts and permits, and asset retirement obligations requires significant judgment. Key assumptions include:

- future volumes, selling prices and production costs;
- the quantity, quality and extraction timing of mineral reserves;
- replacement costs and remaining useful lives of plant and equipment;
- customer attrition rates; and
- discount rates.

Given the scale of our fiscal 2024 acquisitions, these estimates materially affected the amounts recorded for goodwill, property, plant and equipment, and intangible assets. They also affect future depreciation, depletion and amortization. Preliminary allocations may be adjusted during the measurement period, not to exceed one year from the acquisition date, as we obtain additional information.

Goodwill

Goodwill was $6,496 million at December 31, 2024, a significant portion of our total assets. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level annually in the fourth quarter, and more often if events or circumstances indicate that the fair value of a reporting unit may have fallen below its carrying amount. We may first perform a qualitative assessment. When we perform a quantitative test, we estimate reporting unit fair value using discounted cash flow analyses, sometimes corroborated by market-based approaches. Significant assumptions include projected revenue growth, pricing, volume, margin improvement, capital expenditures, terminal growth rates and discount rates.

Our consolidated results show operating losses, and recently acquired reporting units are carried at amounts close to their acquisition-date fair values. A reporting unit's fair value could fall below its carrying amount if, for example:

- margin improvement falls short of our projections;
- demand weakens in the markets served by acquired businesses;
- discount rates rise; or
- our market capitalization declines.

Any of these could result in a material non-cash impairment charge. No goodwill impairment was recorded in fiscal 2024.

Long-Lived Assets, Depletion and Mineral Reserves

Property, plant and equipment, net was $7,613 million at December 31, 2024. We depreciate plant and equipment over estimated useful lives. We deplete mineral reserves and related development costs on a units-of-production basis, using estimates of proven and probable reserves prepared or reviewed by qualified persons. Reserve estimates depend on geological data, engineering assumptions, expected recovery rates, and estimated prices and costs. Revisions to reserve estimates or useful lives change future depletion and depreciation expense.

We review long-lived assets and finite-lived intangible assets for impairment when events or circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. Examples include sustained operating losses at a site, a significant decline in demand from industrial customers, permitting setbacks, or plans to idle or close a facility. Recoverability tests and fair value measurements require estimates of future cash flows, remaining reserve life and discount rates. Structural changes in industries we serve, such as retirements of coal-fired generating units, could lead to impairments of capital-intensive assets that cannot readily be redeployed.

Asset Retirement and Reclamation Obligations

We recognize liabilities for legal obligations to reclaim land disturbed by mining and quarrying and to retire certain long-lived assets. Each liability is recorded at fair value when incurred, and the related cost is capitalized as part of the asset. Estimating these obligations requires judgments about:

- the scope of reclamation required under permits and regulations;
- the cost of the work, including third-party rates and inflation;
- the timing of reclamation, which depends on reserve life and extraction plans; and
- credit-adjusted risk-free discount rates.

Changes in regulations, permit conditions, cost estimates or timing could materially change these liabilities and related accretion and depreciation expense. Fiscal 2024 acquisitions increased our reclamation obligations.

Income Taxes

We account for income taxes under the asset and liability method. We record a valuation allowance when it is more likely than not that some or all of a deferred tax asset will not be realized. Assessing realizability requires weighing all available positive and negative evidence. This includes cumulative results in recent years, the reversal of existing taxable temporary differences, projected future taxable income, and available tax planning strategies.

Because of our cumulative pre-tax losses, we maintain a valuation allowance against most of our net deferred tax assets, which resulted in an effective tax rate of 0.0% in fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023. If our profitability improves sufficiently to provide objective evidence of future taxable income, we could release some or all of the valuation allowance and recognize a significant income tax benefit in that period. Higher effective tax rates would follow in later periods. We also exercise judgment in evaluating uncertain tax positions and in applying tax laws across the jurisdictions where we operate.
