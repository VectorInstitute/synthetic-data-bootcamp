PART I

Item 1. Business

Overview

Draor Midstream, Inc. ("Draor," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") is an integrated midstream energy company headquartered in Seattle, Washington. We own and operate a network of infrastructure assets that connect producers of crude oil, natural gas and natural gas liquids ("NGLs") with refiners, petrochemical manufacturers, local distribution companies, power generators, marketers and export facilities. Our business includes gathering, compressing, treating, processing, fractionating, transporting, storing, terminaling and marketing hydrocarbons and refined petroleum products. Our assets are located in the principal producing basins and demand centers of the United States, and we provide services under a mix of fee-based, cost-of-service, percentage-of-proceeds and commodity-sensitive arrangements.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, we reported total revenue of $134,874 million, an increase of 7.2% compared with the prior fiscal year. At December 31, 2024, we employed about 202,000 people.

Draor Midstream, Inc. is incorporated in the State of Delaware. Our common stock trades under the ticker symbol "DRAR." In this report, references to fiscal 2024 are to the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, and references to fiscal 2023 are to the fiscal year ended December 31, 2023.

Our Strategy

Our strategy is to own and operate a large, interconnected and diversified portfolio of midstream assets that generates durable cash flows across commodity price cycles. We pursue this strategy by:

  • Prioritizing safe, reliable and environmentally responsible operations across all of our facilities and pipeline systems.
  • Expanding the reach of our systems through capital-efficient, demand-driven projects that are generally supported by long-term customer commitments.
  • Integrating our services along the hydrocarbon value chain so that a molecule entering our gathering systems can be processed, transported, stored and delivered to market on Draor-owned infrastructure.
  • Increasing the share of our revenue earned under fee-based and contracted arrangements, while managing the commodity price exposure that remains in certain processing and marketing activities.
  • Maintaining financial discipline in capital allocation, balance sheet management and operating cost control.
  • Advancing lower-emission operating practices and evaluating infrastructure opportunities associated with the evolving energy mix, including carbon capture and transportation, renewable natural gas and hydrogen blending.

Principal Products and Services

We provide midstream services and market energy commodities. Our principal products and services include:

Natural gas gathering and processing. We gather natural gas from wellheads and central delivery points through low- and high-pressure gathering lines, compress the gas, remove impurities such as water, carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide, and process it to extract NGLs. Residue gas is delivered to intrastate and interstate pipelines, and extracted NGLs move to fractionation facilities.

Crude oil gathering and transportation. We gather crude oil and condensate from producing leases by pipeline and truck and deliver these volumes to our long-haul pipelines, storage terminals and third-party connections that serve refining and export markets.

NGL fractionation, transportation and storage. We transport raw NGL mix to fractionation facilities, where we separate it into purity products, including ethane, propane, normal butane, isobutane and natural gasoline. We store these products in underground caverns and above-ground facilities and deliver them to petrochemical, refining, heating and export customers.

Natural gas transmission and storage. We transport natural gas over long distances through interstate and intrastate pipeline systems and provide firm and interruptible storage services in depleted reservoirs and salt caverns. These services allow customers to balance supply and demand and to manage price volatility.

Refined products and terminaling. We operate pipelines, marine docks, rail facilities and truck racks that receive, store, blend and deliver gasoline, diesel, jet fuel and other refined products.

Commodity marketing. We purchase and sell natural gas, NGLs, crude oil and refined products, mainly to optimize the use of our asset base, capture location, quality and time spreads, and provide supply and logistics solutions to our customers.

Because a significant portion of our revenue reflects the gross value of commodities that we purchase and resell, our reported revenue and cost of revenue move with commodity prices, and changes in reported revenue do not necessarily correspond to changes in the volumes we handle or the margins we earn.

Reportable Segments

We manage our business through three reportable segments: Gathering and Processing; Transmission and Storage; and Liquids Logistics and Marketing. Our chief operating decision maker evaluates segment performance and allocates resources primarily on the basis of segment operating results. For additional segment information, see the Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements included in Item 8 of this report.

Gathering and Processing

The Gathering and Processing segment is the point at which most hydrocarbons enter our system. This segment owns and operates natural gas and crude oil gathering systems, compressor stations, treating facilities and cryogenic and refrigeration processing plants in several of the most active onshore producing regions of the United States, including oil-weighted shale basins and gas-weighted shale and tight-sand plays.

The segment's results depend largely on the volumes our producer customers deliver, which in turn depend on their drilling and completion activity, the productivity of their wells and the commodity price environment. We seek to reduce volume risk through acreage dedications, minimum volume commitments and demand charges. A portion of our processing revenue is earned under percentage-of-proceeds and keep-whole arrangements, which expose the segment to changes in natural gas and NGL prices. We manage this exposure through contract structure and, in some cases, through hedging activities.

During fiscal 2024, the segment focused on connecting new well pads, debottlenecking existing processing capacity and improving plant reliability and recovery rates. We also continued to reduce emissions by electrifying compression where feasible, replacing high-bleed pneumatic devices and expanding our leak detection and repair programs.

Transmission and Storage

The Transmission and Storage segment owns and operates interstate and intrastate natural gas pipeline systems and associated storage facilities. These systems connect supply basins with consuming markets, including local distribution companies in large metropolitan areas, gas-fired power generation plants, industrial facilities and liquefied natural gas ("LNG") export terminals along the coast.

Most of this segment's revenue comes from firm transportation and storage service agreements under which customers pay reservation charges for reserved capacity, whether or not they use it, together with usage charges for volumes actually moved or stored. Rates for our interstate systems are subject to regulation by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission ("FERC"), and many of our contracts have multi-year terms. As a result, this segment's cash flows are generally less sensitive to commodity prices and short-term changes in volumes than those of our other segments.

The segment's growth opportunities include expansions and laterals that serve new power generation load, industrial demand and export capacity, as well as modernization projects that improve system integrity, efficiency and environmental performance. We also evaluate opportunities to blend hydrogen and to transport renewable natural gas on portions of our network, subject to technical, commercial and regulatory considerations.

Liquids Logistics and Marketing

The Liquids Logistics and Marketing segment includes our crude oil, NGL and refined products pipelines, our fractionation complexes, our underground and above-ground storage, our marine, rail and truck terminals, and our commodity marketing activities.

This segment provides transportation, fractionation, storage and terminaling services under fee-based agreements, including long-term take-or-pay and throughput-and-deficiency contracts, as well as tariff-based services on common carrier pipelines. Our marketing activities purchase commodities from producers, processors and other market participants and resell them to refiners, petrochemical companies, wholesalers, retailers and export buyers. Marketing results depend on regional price differentials, product quality spreads, storage economics and the availability of transportation capacity, and they can vary significantly from period to period.

Our integrated position allows us to offer customers a single counterparty for gathering, processing, fractionation, storage, transportation and export logistics. We believe this integration improves asset utilization across our system and supports customer retention.

Customers

Our customer base is broad and includes:

  • Independent and integrated crude oil and natural gas producers, which use our gathering, processing and transportation services.
  • Local distribution companies and municipal utilities, which contract for firm transportation and storage to serve residential and commercial demand.
  • Electric generators, including operators of gas-fired combined cycle and peaking plants.
  • Refiners, which purchase crude oil and use our pipelines and terminals to receive feedstocks and distribute finished products.
  • Petrochemical manufacturers, which purchase ethane, propane and other NGLs as feedstocks.
  • Wholesalers, retailers and distributors of propane and refined products.
  • LNG exporters, international commodity buyers and trading companies.

Many of our customer relationships are long-standing and are governed by multi-year contracts. No single customer accounted for a level of revenue in fiscal 2024 that we believe would have a material adverse effect on our consolidated results of operations if that customer were lost. We monitor counterparty credit risk closely and, where appropriate, require letters of credit, parental guarantees, prepayments or other forms of credit support.

Sales and Marketing

We market our services through commercial teams organized by segment, basin and product line. These teams work directly with producers, end users and marketers to structure gathering and processing agreements, transportation and storage contracts, and commodity purchase and sale arrangements. Our commercial staff is supported by engineering, scheduling, risk management and regulatory personnel who evaluate project economics, system capacity and contract terms.

New capacity on our regulated pipelines is generally offered through open seasons conducted in accordance with applicable FERC requirements, and capacity is allocated among shippers on a non-discriminatory basis. On our common carrier liquids pipelines, we publish tariffs that set out rates and terms of service. For gathering and processing services, we negotiate bilateral agreements that often include acreage dedications, minimum volume commitments or both.

Our marketing business uses our physical assets together with contracted third-party capacity to buy, sell, transport and store commodities. We maintain a risk management policy, overseen by senior management and the Board of Directors, that sets limits on open positions, value-at-risk and counterparty exposure. We use derivative instruments principally to hedge commodity price, interest rate and other market risks associated with our physical business, not for speculative purposes.

Competition

The midstream industry is highly competitive. In each of our segments we compete with other midstream companies, integrated energy companies, producer-owned gathering and processing systems, private equity-backed infrastructure developers and, in some cases, alternative modes of transportation such as truck, rail and barge.

In the Gathering and Processing segment, competition centers on proximity to producer acreage, available capacity, processing efficiency and recoveries, fees, reliability and the ability to deliver to multiple downstream markets. Producers may also choose to build and operate their own gathering infrastructure.

In the Transmission and Storage segment, we compete with other interstate and intrastate pipelines that serve the same supply basins and market areas. The principal competitive factors are delivered cost of gas, access to supply, connectivity to markets, flexibility of service and reliability. New pipeline construction and expansions by competitors can affect the value of our capacity when existing contracts are renewed.

In the Liquids Logistics and Marketing segment, we compete with other pipeline, fractionation, storage and terminal operators, as well as with marketers and trading companies. Competitive factors include tariffs and fees, location, connectivity to refining, petrochemical and export markets, storage capacity and the ability to provide integrated, multi-product services.

We believe our scale, the geographic breadth and connectivity of our asset base, our integrated service offering and our operating track record allow us to compete effectively. However, some competitors may have greater access to capital, lower cost structures in particular regions or more favorable positions in specific markets.

Seasonality

Demand for many of the products we handle is seasonal. Natural gas demand for space heating peaks in the winter months, which increases throughput on our transmission systems that serve residential and commercial markets and increases withdrawals from our storage facilities. Summer demand for natural gas used in power generation, driven by air conditioning load, has become an increasingly significant second peak in many of our market areas. Storage injection activity generally occurs during the spring, summer and fall.

Propane demand is typically highest during the winter heating season, while demand for gasoline and certain other refined products is generally higher during the summer driving season. Weather conditions, including hurricanes along the Gulf Coast and severe winter storms, can disrupt production, processing and transportation, affecting both volumes and commodity prices.

Because a substantial portion of our revenue is derived from reservation charges and fixed-fee arrangements, the effect of seasonality on our consolidated results is partially mitigated. Our commodity-sensitive processing and marketing activities, however, may show greater quarterly variability.

Rate and Service Regulation

FERC regulates the rates, terms and conditions of service on our interstate natural gas pipelines and storage facilities under the Natural Gas Act and the Natural Gas Policy Act. FERC also has authority over the certification, construction, acquisition, expansion and abandonment of interstate natural gas facilities. Our interstate natural gas systems operate under tariffs approved by FERC, and their rates are generally determined on a cost-of-service basis or through negotiated rate agreements permitted by FERC policy.

Our interstate crude oil, NGL and refined products pipelines are regulated by FERC under the Interstate Commerce Act. These pipelines are required to publish tariffs, provide service on a non-discriminatory basis and charge rates that are just and reasonable. Most of our liquids pipeline rates are adjusted through an indexing methodology established by FERC, while others are set through cost-of-service filings, settlements or market-based rate authority.

Intrastate pipelines and gathering systems are generally exempt from FERC rate jurisdiction but may be subject to regulation by state public utility commissions or other state agencies, including requirements relating to ratable take, non-discriminatory service, rate filings and complaint procedures.

Pipeline Safety

Our pipelines and certain related facilities are subject to regulation by the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration ("PHMSA") of the U.S. Department of Transportation, and by state agencies that act under agreements with PHMSA. These regulations govern the design, construction, testing, operation, maintenance and inspection of pipeline facilities, and they require integrity management programs for pipeline segments located in high consequence areas. PHMSA has expanded its regulations in recent years to cover additional gathering lines, impose new requirements on transmission pipeline integrity verification, valve installation and rupture mitigation, and strengthen leak detection and repair standards. We expect to continue investing in inspection, testing and replacement to meet these requirements.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Changes in the prices of crude oil, natural gas, NGLs and refined products can significantly affect our revenue, margins and cash flows.

A substantial portion of our reported revenue reflects the gross value of commodities that we purchase and resell through our marketing activities. As a result, our revenue and cost of revenue move closely with commodity prices, and the spread between them is narrow relative to the size of either line. In fiscal 2024, cost of revenue grew faster than revenue, and gross profit and gross margin declined compared with fiscal 2023 despite higher reported revenue. Because the spread is narrow, small changes in purchase costs, sales prices, transportation costs or product quality differentials can have a disproportionate effect on our gross profit and operating income.

Commodity prices are volatile and are determined by factors outside our control. These factors include domestic and global supply and demand, the production decisions of major oil-producing nations, the level of domestic drilling and completion activity, weather, storage inventories, the availability of pipeline and export capacity, government policies affecting production or trade, armed conflicts and political instability, the pace of adoption of alternative energy sources, and general economic conditions. Sustained low or volatile prices could reduce the volumes delivered to our systems, compress our marketing spreads, lower the value of commodities we retain under certain processing contracts and increase the risk that our customers fail to perform.

Our results depend on the drilling and production activity of producers in the basins we serve, which we do not control.

Our gathering, processing, fractionation and liquids transportation businesses rely on producers continuing to drill and complete wells connected to our systems. Production from any individual well declines over time, so maintaining or increasing throughput requires a continuing program of new well connections. Producers' decisions to drill depend on commodity prices, their access to capital, their cost structures, the productivity of their acreage, regulatory requirements and their own strategic priorities, including pressure from investors to return capital rather than reinvest in growth.

Acreage dedications and minimum volume commitments mitigate, but do not eliminate, this exposure. Dedications do not obligate producers to drill, and minimum volume commitments expire, may be renegotiated, or may be uncollectible if the counterparty experiences financial distress. A sustained reduction in drilling activity in one or more of our core basins could reduce throughput across several of our segments at the same time, because volumes that enter our gathering systems also feed our processing plants, fractionation complexes and downstream pipelines.

Our percentage-of-proceeds, keep-whole and marketing arrangements expose us to direct commodity price and spread risk.

A portion of our Gathering and Processing segment revenue is earned under percentage-of-proceeds and keep-whole arrangements, under which our compensation varies with the value of natural gas and NGLs. Under keep-whole arrangements, we may bear the cost of replacing the heating value of NGLs extracted from the gas stream, which can be unprofitable when NGL prices are low relative to natural gas prices. Our Liquids Logistics and Marketing segment captures location, quality and time spreads that can narrow or disappear because of new pipeline capacity, changes in regional supply and demand, or market dislocations.

Our hedging activities may not be effective in offsetting these exposures. Hedges may cover only a portion of our volumes, may not correlate precisely with the prices we realize, may require us to post collateral that reduces our liquidity, and may prevent us from benefiting fully from favorable price movements. Our risk management policies may not detect or prevent unauthorized trading or positions that exceed approved limits, which could result in significant losses.

We may be unable to renew or replace expiring contracts on favorable terms, or at all.

Many of our transportation, storage, gathering and processing agreements have fixed terms. When these contracts expire, customers may elect not to renew, may seek to reduce contracted capacity, or may demand lower rates, shorter terms or fewer volume commitments. Our ability to recontract depends on the availability of competing infrastructure, the supply and demand outlook in the relevant markets, the creditworthiness and strategic plans of our customers, and regulatory constraints on our rates. If we recontract at lower rates or with less firm capacity, or if capacity remains unsubscribed, our revenue and cash flows could decline even if our operating costs do not.

We face significant competition, which could reduce our volumes, rates and margins.

We compete with other midstream companies, integrated energy companies, producer-owned gathering and processing systems, private equity-backed developers and marketing and trading firms. Some competitors have greater financial resources, lower costs of capital, newer assets or access to supply sources that we lack. Competition is based on rates, service quality, system reliability, interconnectivity, available capacity and the ability to offer integrated services. New pipelines and processing plants built by competitors in our core basins, or alternative transportation methods such as rail and truck, could divert volumes from our systems and pressure our rates and marketing margins.

We are exposed to the credit risk of our customers and other counterparties.

Our customers include producers, marketers, refiners, petrochemical manufacturers, utilities, power generators and exporters, many of whom are themselves exposed to commodity price volatility. Some of our customers are highly leveraged or have below-investment-grade credit ratings. During periods of low prices or tight credit markets, customers may be unable to pay amounts owed, may seek to reject or renegotiate contracts in bankruptcy proceedings, or may be unable to provide adequate credit support. Our accounts receivable and other current assets are significant, and losses from counterparty nonperformance, including counterparties to our hedging and marketing transactions, could adversely affect our results and liquidity.

Long-term changes in energy demand, including the transition to lower-carbon energy sources, could reduce demand for our services and the useful lives of our assets.

Government policies, technological developments, investor preferences and consumer behavior are encouraging greater energy efficiency and the use of renewable energy, electrification and other alternatives to hydrocarbons. Over time, these trends could reduce demand for crude oil, natural gas, NGLs and refined products, lower the volumes moving through our systems, shorten the economic lives of our pipelines, plants and terminals, and increase the risk of asset impairments. Our property, plant and equipment represents the large majority of our total assets, and much of it has long expected service lives. Our efforts to develop infrastructure related to carbon capture, renewable natural gas and hydrogen are at an early stage. They may not generate returns comparable to our existing businesses or offset declines in our traditional services.

Our growth and expansion projects may not be completed on schedule or within budget, and may not generate expected returns.

We invest significant capital in new pipelines, processing plants, fractionators, storage and export facilities and in expansions of existing systems. In fiscal 2024, our capital expenditures and other investing outflows significantly exceeded our cash provided by operating activities. These projects are subject to risks that include permitting delays, legal challenges, landowner opposition, cost inflation for steel, equipment and labor, contractor performance, supply chain disruptions, adverse weather and changes in customer demand after we commit capital. Projects often require years to complete, and revenue generally does not begin until they are placed in service. If we experience cost overruns or delays, or if contracted volumes fail to materialize, we may not recover our investment, and our leverage could increase without a corresponding increase in cash flow.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Our operations are subject to hazards that could result in personal injury, loss of life, environmental damage and significant financial loss, and our insurance may not be adequate.

Our pipelines, processing plants, fractionators, storage caverns, terminals and marine facilities handle flammable, explosive and toxic substances under high pressure. These operations involve risks of fires, explosions, ruptures, leaks, releases of hydrogen sulfide and other hazardous materials, cavern failures, equipment malfunctions, vehicle and vessel accidents and other incidents. Such events could result in injury or death, damage to property and natural resources, evacuation of nearby communities, regulatory penalties, litigation, suspension of operations and lasting damage to our reputation and relationships with regulators and communities.

We do not insure against every risk, and our insurance is subject to deductibles, retentions, sublimits and exclusions. Insurance coverage for certain risks, including pollution, named windstorms and cyber incidents, has become more costly and more limited, and we may choose to self-insure a larger portion of these risks. An uninsured or underinsured event could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition and results of operations.

The age and condition of portions of our infrastructure may require substantial unplanned maintenance and integrity expenditures.

Our asset base is extensive and includes pipeline segments, compressor stations and processing facilities that have been in service for many years. Aging assets may be more susceptible to corrosion, mechanical failure and other integrity problems. Our depreciation and amortization expense is large relative to our operating income, reflecting the capital intensity of our business, and maintaining and replacing assets requires continuing investment. Integrity assessments, new regulatory requirements or incidents on our systems or those of other operators could require us to repair, replace or reduce the operating pressure of facilities. Such actions could raise costs, reduce throughput and lower revenue.

Cyberattacks and other disruptions of our information and operational technology systems could disrupt operations and harm our business.

We rely on information technology and industrial control systems, including supervisory control and data acquisition systems, to operate our pipelines and facilities, schedule and measure volumes, process transactions and maintain financial records. These systems are targets of increasingly sophisticated attacks by criminal organizations, nation-state actors and others, including ransomware and attacks directed at critical energy infrastructure. A successful attack, or a failure of our systems or those of our vendors and interconnecting parties, could disrupt the flow of commodities, compromise safety systems, expose confidential data, result in regulatory penalties under federal security directives and cause significant remediation costs and liability. Our safeguards may not prevent or detect every intrusion.

Severe weather, natural disasters and the physical effects of climate change could damage our facilities and disrupt operations.

Our assets are located in regions exposed to hurricanes, flooding, extreme cold, extreme heat, wildfires, drought and earthquakes. Severe weather can damage facilities, interrupt power supplies needed for compression and processing, curtail producer volumes, disrupt marine and rail logistics and impair customers' ability to receive deliveries. Extreme cold events have in the past caused widespread freeze-offs of production and failures of power and gas infrastructure across the industry. Changes in the frequency and severity of such events could increase our operating and capital costs, raise insurance premiums and reduce the reliability of our systems.

Our large workforce exposes us to labor cost pressures, labor relations risks and difficulties in attracting and retaining qualified personnel.

We employ about 202,000 people, and labor is a significant component of both our cost of revenue and our selling, general and administrative expense. Wage inflation, rising benefit costs, competition for skilled technicians, engineers and operators, and changes in labor laws could increase our costs. Portions of our workforce may be represented by labor unions, and work stoppages, strikes or difficult negotiations could disrupt operations. The loss of key management or experienced operating personnel, or our inability to transfer knowledge as experienced employees retire, could impair our ability to operate safely and execute our strategy.

We depend on third-party pipelines, facilities, suppliers and service providers.

Our systems interconnect with pipelines, processing plants, fractionators, refineries, export terminals, railroads and power suppliers that we do not own or control. Outages, capacity constraints, quality specification changes or curtailments at these third-party facilities could limit our ability to receive or deliver volumes. We also rely on a limited number of suppliers for specialized equipment such as compressors, turbines, line pipe and processing components. Supply chain disruptions, tariffs and trade restrictions could increase costs or delay maintenance and growth projects.

Risks Related to Our Financing and Capital Structure

Our substantial indebtedness and the level of our interest expense have resulted in net losses and could limit our flexibility and our ability to meet our obligations.

We have a substantial amount of long-term debt, and our total liabilities represent the large majority of our total assets. In each of fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023, our interest expense exceeded our operating income, and as a result we reported a loss before income taxes and a net loss in both years. Although operating income improved in fiscal 2024 and our net loss narrowed, we cannot assure you that we will achieve or sustain profitability. If operating income does not grow faster than our interest expense, we will continue to report net losses, and our stockholders' equity could erode over time.

Our level of indebtedness could, among other things:

  • require us to dedicate a substantial portion of our cash flow from operations to interest and principal payments, reducing funds available for capital expenditures, acquisitions and other purposes;
  • increase our vulnerability to adverse economic, industry and commodity price conditions;
  • limit our flexibility to plan for or react to changes in our business and competitive environment;
  • place us at a disadvantage relative to competitors that are less leveraged; and
  • limit our ability to obtain additional financing on acceptable terms.

Rising interest rates and refinancing risk could increase our borrowing costs.

Our interest expense increased in fiscal 2024 compared with fiscal 2023, and our long-term debt also increased. Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Overview

Draor depends on information technology ("IT") and operational technology ("OT") systems to operate its business. These include the supervisory control and data acquisition ("SCADA") systems, pipeline control centers, measurement equipment and plant control systems that monitor and direct the movement of hydrocarbons across our gathering, processing, transmission, storage and terminaling assets. We also rely on enterprise systems for commodity scheduling, nominations, marketing and trading, billing, financial reporting and communications with customers and regulators.

A compromise of these systems could disrupt deliveries to the utilities, power generators, refiners, petrochemical manufacturers and export facilities we serve. It could also create safety and environmental hazards, cause loss of sensitive commercial or personal data, or expose us to regulatory penalties and litigation. For these reasons, we treat cybersecurity as an enterprise-level operational and safety risk rather than solely a technology matter.

Risk Management and Strategy

Our cybersecurity program is designed to identify, assess, prioritize and manage material risks from cybersecurity threats to our IT and OT environments. It is part of our enterprise risk management process, through which management identifies and evaluates the principal risks facing the Company and the controls that address them. Cybersecurity risks are evaluated alongside operational, financial, regulatory and strategic risks, which allows the threats most likely to affect pipeline integrity, plant operations or commercial activities to receive appropriate attention and resources.

The program is structured using the cybersecurity framework published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology. It also reflects the pipeline security requirements issued by the Transportation Security Administration and other industry standards applicable to control systems in the energy sector. Key elements include:

  • Network segmentation that separates our OT environments from corporate IT networks and from external connections, with controlled and monitored pathways between them.
  • Identity and access management controls, including multifactor authentication and privileged access restrictions for systems that can affect physical operations.
  • Continuous security monitoring through a security operations function that collects and analyzes activity from across our networks, endpoints and field facilities and escalates suspicious activity for investigation.
  • Vulnerability management processes that identify weaknesses in hardware, software and configurations and prioritize remediation based on exploitability and potential operational consequence.
  • Threat intelligence obtained from commercial providers, government agencies and industry information-sharing organizations, which helps us understand tactics directed at energy infrastructure.
  • Security requirements built into the design of new facilities, capital projects and digital solutions, including those associated with assets placed in service or acquired during the year.

We assess the program's effectiveness through internal testing, penetration exercises, tabletop simulations and periodic reviews by independent cybersecurity firms. Our internal audit function includes IT and cybersecurity controls within its risk-based audit plan and reports its findings to management and the Audit Committee.

All employees and contractors with network access must complete cybersecurity awareness training. Personnel in control rooms, field operations, engineering and finance receive additional role-specific training. We conduct simulated phishing campaigns to reinforce secure behavior and to identify areas where further training is needed.

Third-Party Risk

We rely on third parties for equipment, software, cloud services, telecommunications, engineering and construction, and managed services, some of which connect to our systems or handle our data. Our third-party risk management process evaluates the security practices of vendors before engagement and periodically afterward, with the depth of review scaled to the sensitivity of the data or systems involved and the potential effect on operations. Contracts with higher-risk vendors generally include security obligations, incident notification requirements and rights to request evidence of compliance. We also monitor threat intelligence for reported compromises affecting our suppliers and take protective measures when a supplier is affected.

Incident Response and Business Continuity

We maintain a cybersecurity incident response plan that defines roles, escalation criteria and communication protocols. When an incident is detected, a cross-functional response team assesses its scope and severity, contains and remediates it, and evaluates any legal, regulatory and disclosure obligations. The team includes representatives from information security, operations, legal, compliance, communications and finance, and may draw on outside forensic and legal advisors as needed.

Our pipeline control centers and major facilities maintain manual operating procedures and backup control capabilities so that critical operations can continue safely if automated systems become unavailable. Business continuity and disaster recovery plans are tested through regular exercises that involve operations and corporate personnel. We also carry cyber insurance, although, as described in Item 1A, Risk Factors, this coverage has become more costly and more limited and may not cover all losses.

Governance

Board Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees the Company's risk management, including cybersecurity, and has delegated primary oversight of cybersecurity risk to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives regular reports from the Chief Information Security Officer and the Chief Information Officer. These reports cover the threat environment, the results of assessments and audits, the status of significant security initiatives, regulatory developments and any significant incidents. The Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full Board, and the Board also considers cybersecurity as part of its annual review of enterprise risks. Directors receive periodic education on cybersecurity topics relevant to energy infrastructure.

Management's Role

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads the enterprise cybersecurity organization and is responsible for assessing and managing material risks from cybersecurity threats across our IT and OT environments. The Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience in information security and industrial control system security, including leadership roles in energy and critical infrastructure organizations, and holds recognized professional security certifications.

The Chief Information Security Officer reports to the Chief Information Officer, who oversees our broader technology organization and reports to our Chief Executive Officer. The cybersecurity organization works closely with our operations leadership, our pipeline safety and integrity teams and our legal and compliance functions, so that security decisions reflect operational and regulatory considerations. A management risk committee that includes senior operations, legal and financial executives reviews significant cybersecurity risks and incidents and determines when matters should be escalated to the Chief Executive Officer and the Board.

Material Effects of Cybersecurity Threats

As of the date of this report, we are not aware of any cybersecurity threats, including as a result of any previous incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect the Company, including our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. Like other operators of energy infrastructure, we are regularly targeted by attempted intrusions and other malicious activity. Our security measures cannot provide complete assurance that a future incident will be prevented or will not have a material adverse effect. For further discussion of cybersecurity risks, see Item 1A, Risk Factors.

Overview

Draor Midstream, Inc. is an integrated midstream energy company headquartered in Seattle, Washington. Our infrastructure links producers of crude oil, natural gas and NGLs with refiners, petrochemical manufacturers, utilities, power generators, marketers and export facilities. We report our operations in three segments:

  • Gathering and Processing.
  • Transmission and Storage.
  • Liquids Logistics and Marketing.

We earn revenue under several types of arrangements. These include fee-based contracts, cost-of-service contracts, percentage-of-proceeds contracts and commodity-sensitive contracts. We also earn revenue from the purchase and resale of commodities through our marketing activities.

A significant portion of our reported revenue reflects the gross value of commodities we buy and sell. As a result, revenue and cost of revenue tend to move together with commodity prices, and the spread between them is narrow relative to the size of either line. For this reason, management focuses on the following measures in evaluating our performance:

  • Gross profit.
  • Operating income.
  • Cash flows from operations.
  • Asset utilization and throughput.

Reported revenue alone is not a reliable guide to the volumes we handle or the margins we earn.

Our business is capital intensive. Property, plant and equipment, net, was $625,949 million at December 31, 2024, which represents the large majority of our total assets of $837,727 million. We finance our asset base with a combination of internally generated cash, long-term debt and equity. Interest expense is therefore a significant component of our cost structure.

Business Environment

Fiscal 2024 was marked by continued volatility in commodity prices and regional price differentials. Producer activity varied across the basins we serve. In our oil-weighted basins, sustained drilling and completion programs supported throughput on our gathering and processing systems and on our downstream liquids pipelines and fractionation facilities. In our gas-weighted regions, some producers moderated activity in response to lower natural gas prices during parts of the year, which tempered volume growth in those areas.

Demand for firm natural gas transportation and storage remained solid. This demand was supported by several sources:

  • Power generation load.
  • Industrial consumption.
  • Feed gas requirements of coastal LNG export terminals.

Our marketing results were affected by narrower location and quality spreads in certain markets. These narrower spreads resulted from new third-party pipeline capacity and changes in regional supply and demand balances.

Cost pressures continued in several areas, including labor, equipment, materials and contracted services. We responded with procurement initiatives, operating efficiency programs and reductions in overhead. Higher interest rates also increased the cost of the floating-rate portion of our borrowings and of new debt we issued during the year.

Results of Operations

The following discussion compares our consolidated results for fiscal 2024 with those for fiscal 2023.

Revenue

Revenue was $134,874 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $125,839 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 7.2%. The increase reflected several factors:

  • Higher volumes of NGLs, crude oil and natural gas sold through our marketing activities, as greater throughput on our systems allowed us to purchase and resell more product.
  • Higher average realized prices for certain NGL purity products and refined products during portions of the year.
  • Increased fee-based revenue from gathering, processing and fractionation, driven by new well connections and the full-year contribution of capacity placed in service during fiscal 2023.
  • Contractual escalations and recontracting activity on our transmission and storage systems.

These increases were partially offset by lower average realized natural gas prices in certain regions, which reduced the gross value of natural gas sold.

Because much of our revenue reflects the gross value of commodities, the increase in revenue does not by itself indicate a proportional increase in profitability. Changes in commodity prices generally affect revenue and cost of revenue in similar amounts. They have a smaller net effect on gross profit, except where we retain commodity exposure under percentage-of-proceeds, keep-whole or marketing arrangements.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $123,572 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $114,074 million in fiscal 2023. Cost of revenue includes the following:

  • The cost of commodities purchased for resale.
  • Operating and maintenance expenses for our pipelines, plants and terminals.
  • Fuel and power costs.
  • Property taxes.
  • Depreciation and amortization, which was $22,780 million in fiscal 2024.

Cost of revenue increased primarily because of higher volumes of commodities purchased through our marketing activities. Additional contributors were:

  • Higher operating and maintenance costs associated with an expanded asset base.
  • Inflation in labor, materials and contracted services.
  • Higher depreciation resulting from capital projects placed in service and assets added during the year.

Gross profit was $11,302 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $11,765 million in fiscal 2023. Gross margin declined to 8.4% from 9.3%. Gross profit declined despite higher revenue because cost of revenue grew faster than revenue. The principal reasons were:

  • Narrower marketing spreads. New pipeline capacity reduced price differentials between certain supply basins and demand centers, which compressed the margins we earned on purchase-and-resale transactions.
  • Lower commodity-sensitive processing margins. Lower natural gas and NGL prices in parts of the year reduced the value of the commodities we retain under percentage-of-proceeds and keep-whole arrangements.
  • Higher operating costs and depreciation. Expenses associated with newly constructed and acquired assets increased ahead of the full ramp-up of volumes on some of those assets.

These factors were partially offset by higher fee-based gathering, processing, fractionation and transportation revenue, which carries a higher margin than our marketing activities.

Operating Income

We expect to continue investing in growth and maintenance projects in fiscal 2025. We will prioritize projects that are supported by long-term customer commitments and that meet our return thresholds. Our capital program remains subject to change based on the following:

  • Customer demand.

  • Permitting outcomes.
  • Cost trends.
  • Our financing capacity.

Cash Flows from Financing Activities

Net cash provided by financing activities was $14,528 million in fiscal 2024. This amount consisted of net proceeds from issuances of common stock, net of share repurchases, of $10,378 million and a net increase in long-term debt of $4,150 million. We used these proceeds, together with cash provided by operating activities, to fund our capital expenditures, acquisitions and investments in unconsolidated affiliates.

Debt

Long-term debt was $217,306 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $213,156 million at December 31, 2023. The net increase of $4,150 million reflected new issuances of senior notes and term borrowings, partially offset by repayments and redemptions of maturing obligations. Our debt consists primarily of fixed-rate senior unsecured notes with staggered maturities. We supplement these notes with borrowings under our revolving credit facilities, term loans and commercial paper program.

Our credit agreements and indentures contain customary covenants, including:

  • Limitations on liens.
  • Limitations on sale-leaseback transactions.
  • Limitations on mergers and asset sales.
  • Under our revolving credit facilities, a maximum leverage ratio.

As of December 31, 2024, we were in compliance with all of our debt covenants.

We actively manage our maturity profile. We may refinance upcoming maturities, repurchase outstanding notes in the open market or through tender offers, or repay borrowings with available cash, depending on market conditions.

Our interest expense exceeds our operating income. Our long-term debt and other non-current liabilities are also large relative to our stockholders' equity. As a result, we are focused on improving our credit profile. Other non-current liabilities were $403,594 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $374,448 million at December 31, 2023. Total liabilities were $682,580 million, compared with $644,493 million. Our approach to improving our credit profile includes:

  • Growing fee-based cash flows.
  • Exercising capital discipline.
  • Directing a portion of excess cash flow toward debt reduction over time.

A downgrade of our credit ratings could increase our borrowing costs, require us to post additional collateral under certain commercial and hedging agreements, and limit our access to the commercial paper market.

Equity, Dividends and Share Repurchases

Total stockholders' equity was $155,147 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $147,292 million at December 31, 2023. The increase reflected net equity issuances, net of share repurchases, of $10,378 million, partially offset by our net loss of $2,523 million.

During fiscal 2024, our issuances of common stock exceeded our repurchases. These issuances included shares sold through registered offerings and shares issued under our employee equity compensation plans. Share repurchases during the year were limited and primarily related to shares withheld to satisfy tax obligations on vesting equity awards. We intend to evaluate share repurchases in the context of our leverage objectives and capital investment needs.

We did not pay dividends on our common stock in fiscal 2024. We currently retain cash to fund capital investment and to strengthen our balance sheet. Any future dividend will be at the discretion of our Board of Directors. The Board's decision will depend on several factors, including:

  • Our results of operations and cash flows.
  • Our financial condition.
  • Our leverage.
  • Restrictions in our debt agreements.
  • Other factors the Board considers relevant.

Property, Plant and Equipment and Depreciation

Property, plant and equipment, net, was $625,949 million at December 31, 2024. Depreciation and amortization was $22,780 million in fiscal 2024. We depreciate most of our assets using the straight-line method over their estimated useful lives. These estimates are based on the following:

  • Engineering studies.
  • Historical experience.
  • The expected period over which the assets will generate economic benefits.

The economic benefit period, in turn, depends on the remaining recoverable reserves in the basins we serve, the expected demand for the services and products the assets provide, and regulatory requirements. For our rate-regulated assets, depreciation rates are generally those approved by regulators.

Long-term changes in energy demand, including the transition to lower-carbon energy sources, could shorten the economic lives of certain assets. Such a change would increase our depreciation expense prospectively. We review useful lives periodically and adjust them when facts and circumstances warrant.

Impairment of Long-Lived Assets and Equity Method Investments

We evaluate long-lived assets for impairment when events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amount may not be recoverable. Examples of such indicators include:

  • A significant decline in throughput or producer activity.
  • An adverse change in commodity prices.
  • Loss of a significant customer.
  • A decision to dispose of an asset.

The recoverability test compares an asset group's carrying amount with its estimated undiscounted future cash flows. If the carrying amount is not recoverable, we measure the impairment as the excess of carrying amount over fair value, which we generally estimate using discounted cash flows. These estimates require assumptions about several matters, including:

  • Future volumes.
  • Contract renewals and rates.
  • Commodity prices.
  • Operating and capital costs.
  • Remaining asset lives.
  • Discount rates.

We also evaluate equity method investments for impairment when there are indications of a decline in value that is other than temporary. Impairment charges are included in other operating expense, which was $1,322 million in fiscal 2024 and $3,427 million in fiscal 2023.

Goodwill

Goodwill was $23,624 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level at least annually, and more frequently if events indicate that a reporting unit's fair value may be less than its carrying amount. We estimate fair value using a combination of discounted cash flow analyses and market-based approaches. The key assumptions include:

  • Long-term growth rates.
  • Projected margins and capital requirements.
  • Discount rates.
  • Market multiples for comparable midstream businesses.

Our most recent annual test did not result in an impairment. However, adverse changes in our assumptions could cause the estimated fair value of one or more reporting units to fall below its carrying amount. Such changes might include sustained declines in producer activity in a basin, higher discount rates or lower market valuations. That outcome could result in a material non-cash impairment charge.

Revenue Recognition and Gross Versus Net Presentation

We recognize revenue as we satisfy performance obligations under our contracts with customers. For our commodity purchase and sale arrangements, and for contracts in which we receive commodities as consideration for services, we must judge whether we act as principal or agent. This judgment determines whether we report revenue on a gross or net basis. We consider several factors in making it:

  • Whether we control the commodity before it is transferred to the customer.
  • Whether we bear inventory and price risk.
  • Whether we have discretion in establishing prices.

These conclusions significantly affect the reported amounts of revenue and cost of revenue, but they do not affect gross profit or operating income. We also estimate variable consideration under certain contracts, including minimum volume commitment deficiency payments and percentage-of-proceeds arrangements, and we recognize it to the extent a significant reversal is not probable.

Asset Retirement Obligations and Environmental Liabilities

We record asset retirement obligations for legal obligations to retire certain assets when we can reasonably estimate their fair value. These obligations are included within other non-current liabilities. Estimating them requires assumptions about the following:

  • Timing of retirement.
  • Retirement methods and costs.
  • Inflation rates.
  • Credit-adjusted risk-free discount rates.

For many of our pipelines and related facilities, the timing of retirement cannot be reasonably determined because of the indeterminate useful lives of the assets. In those cases, we do not record an obligation until sufficient information becomes available. We accrue environmental remediation costs when a loss is probable and reasonably estimable. These estimates may change as investigations progress, technologies evolve or regulations change.

Income Taxes

We record deferred tax assets and liabilities for the expected future tax consequences of temporary differences, net operating loss carryforwards and other tax attributes. We establish a valuation allowance when it is more likely than not that some portion of our deferred tax assets will not be realized. In assessing realizability, we consider the following:

  • Our history of pre-tax losses.
  • The scheduled reversal of deferred tax liabilities.
  • Projected future taxable income.
  • Available tax planning strategies.

Because we incurred losses before income taxes in fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023, we maintained a full valuation allowance against our net deferred tax assets. As a result, our effective tax rate was 0.0% in both years. Changes in our assessment of future profitability could result in a release of all or part of the valuation allowance, which would materially affect our reported income tax provision in the period of release. We also evaluate uncertain tax positions and recognize the benefit of a position only when it is more likely than not to be sustained upon examination.
