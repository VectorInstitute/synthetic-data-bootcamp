PART I

Item 1. Business

OVERVIEW

Orzenka Midstream, Inc. ("Orzenka," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") is a Delaware corporation headquartered in Phoenix, Arizona. We own, operate and develop energy infrastructure that connects hydrocarbon supply to demand. Our asset base includes:

  • natural gas gathering systems and processing plants;
  • natural gas liquids ("NGL") fractionation facilities;
  • crude oil, refined product and NGL transportation pipelines;
  • above-ground tankage and underground storage caverns; and
  • marine, rail and truck terminals.

Our customers include producers, refiners, petrochemical manufacturers, local distribution companies, power generators, marketers and exporters. Through our facilities, we move hydrocarbons from the wellhead and from the refinery gate to end-use markets in the United States and to export points.

Our common stock trades under the ticker symbol "ORZA." For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, total revenue was $52,746 million, an increase of 15.1% from the prior fiscal year. Revenue grew because volumes on our systems increased, assets placed in service during the year contributed to results, and our recently acquired assets contributed for a full year.

We manage our business through three reportable segments:

  • Gathering and Processing;
  • Transportation and Storage; and
  • Terminals and Marketing Services.

Each segment is organized around the part of the hydrocarbon value chain it supports. The segments are described below under "Our Reportable Segments." Additional segment financial information appears in Item 7, Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations, and in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8, Financial Statements and Supplementary Data.

Our business model rests on long-term commercial agreements. Many of these agreements contain fee-based rate structures, minimum volume commitments, acreage dedications or reservation charges. We structure our contracts this way to limit our direct exposure to commodity price changes and to support cash flows through the commodity cycle. Some of our activities, such as certain percent-of-proceeds processing arrangements and our marketing operations, do expose us to commodity prices. We manage that exposure through contract design, operating practices and, where appropriate, derivative instruments.

STRATEGY AND PRIORITIES

Safe and Reliable Operations

The safety of our workforce, the communities near our assets and the environment comes before every other operating objective. We maintain an integrity management program for our pipelines and facilities. The program includes:

  • inline inspection;
  • cathodic protection;
  • leak detection;
  • control room management; and
  • preventive maintenance.

We investigate incidents and near misses to identify root causes. We share the lessons learned across our operating regions so that corrective measures are applied consistently.

Disciplined Capital Allocation

We assess growth opportunities against expected returns, the strength and duration of the underlying commercial support, and how well the project fits our existing footprint. We favor projects that extend, debottleneck or connect assets we already own. These projects generally offer lower execution risk and better capital efficiency than greenfield development in new regions. We also pursue acquisitions when we believe a transaction will add to our system, broaden our customer base, or create opportunities to integrate the acquired assets with our own.

Cost Competitiveness and Operational Efficiency

We seek to lower our unit operating costs. Our main tools are:

  • standardized engineering and procurement practices;
  • centralized control operations;
  • predictive maintenance programs; and
  • consolidation of support functions.

We regard cost competitiveness as essential to winning new volumes in a market where customers can often choose among several service providers.

Financial Strength and Shareholder Returns

We intend to keep a balance sheet that supports access to capital markets throughout the commodity cycle. We aim to generate cash flow sufficient to fund maintenance capital, a significant share of our growth capital, and the return of capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases. Our board of directors reviews our capital allocation framework periodically. It may change dividend and repurchase levels in light of business conditions, investment opportunities and other factors.

Energy Transition Readiness

We evaluate opportunities that use our existing rights-of-way, engineering capabilities and customer relationships to serve lower-carbon energy markets. These include:

  • carbon dioxide transportation and sequestration;
  • renewable feedstock and renewable fuel logistics; and
  • the handling of hydrogen and hydrogen-derived products.

We pursue these opportunities only where commercial structures provide returns consistent with our investment criteria.

PRINCIPAL PRODUCTS AND SERVICES

We provide midstream services across the life of a hydrocarbon molecule, from the point of production or refining to the point of consumption or export.

  • Gathering. We provide low- and high-pressure gathering of natural gas and crude oil from wellheads and central delivery points. Related services include compression, dehydration, treating and condensate stabilization.
  • Processing and fractionation. We separate natural gas into residue gas and a mixed NGL stream. We then fractionate the mixed NGL stream into purity products: ethane, propane, normal butane, isobutane and natural gasoline.
  • Pipeline transportation. We provide interstate and intrastate transportation of natural gas, crude oil, refined products and NGLs. Service is offered under tariffs approved by regulators and under negotiated agreements.
  • Storage. We provide storage in above-ground tanks and in solution-mined salt caverns. Customers use our storage to manage operations, balance seasonal demand and take advantage of market opportunities.
  • Terminalling. We provide receipt, storage, blending, additive injection and redelivery of crude oil, refined products, NGLs and renewable fuels. These services are offered at inland and marine terminals, with connections to pipelines, rail, barges, ships and trucks.
  • Marketing and logistics services. We purchase and resell crude oil, NGLs, natural gas and refined products. These activities help optimize the use of our own assets and give customers market access, scheduling support and supply aggregation.

OUR REPORTABLE SEGMENTS

Gathering and Processing

The Gathering and Processing segment provides wellhead-to-market services to oil and natural gas producers in several onshore resource plays. Its assets include:

  • gathering pipelines;
  • field and central compression;
  • cryogenic and refrigeration processing plants;
  • treating facilities that remove carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide and other impurities; and
  • crude oil and produced water gathering systems in certain areas.

Volumes processed in this segment frequently flow onward into our Transportation and Storage segment. This linkage allows us to capture value at several points in the value chain.

Most contracts in this segment are long-term. Many include acreage dedications or minimum volume commitments. Fee types vary by agreement and include fixed fees per unit of throughput, cost-of-service structures, and percent-of-proceeds arrangements. Under a percent-of-proceeds arrangement, we keep a portion of the sales proceeds from residue gas and NGLs. Segment results depend mainly on producer drilling and completion activity. That activity is shaped by commodity prices, producer capital budgets, and the geology and economics of the basins we serve.

Transportation and Storage

The Transportation and Storage segment owns and operates long-haul and regional pipelines that carry natural gas, crude oil, refined products and NGLs. It also owns and operates the storage assets associated with those pipelines. Its assets include:

  • interstate and intrastate natural gas pipelines that serve utilities, industrial users, power generators and liquefied natural gas export facilities;
  • crude oil trunklines that connect producing regions with refining centers and export terminals;
  • refined product pipelines that serve metropolitan demand markets; and
  • NGL pipelines that deliver mixed and purity products to fractionation and petrochemical hubs.

The segment also includes above-ground tank farms and underground salt cavern storage for natural gas, NGLs and refined products.

Revenue in this segment comes mainly from fee-based services. Some services are priced under tariffs filed with federal or state regulators. Others are priced under negotiated rates in firm transportation and storage agreements. Many firm agreements include reservation or demand charges that are payable whether or not the customer ships or stores volumes. These charges add predictability to segment cash flows. Interruptible services and short-term storage arrangements supplement firm revenues and allow us to market available capacity.

Terminals and Marketing Services

The Terminals and Marketing Services segment operates our marine, inland, rail and truck-rack terminals. It also conducts our commodity marketing and logistics activities. The terminals offer:

  • storage, blending and handling services for refiners, marketers, wholesalers and exporters; and
  • dock capacity for loading and unloading vessels and barges serving domestic and international markets.

Our marketing activities purchase hydrocarbons at receipt points on or near our systems and resell them at downstream markets. In doing so, they raise utilization of our assets and give customers flexible supply and disposition options.

Revenue in this segment includes fees for terminalling throughput, storage and ancillary services. It also includes sales of commodities purchased in our marketing activities. Because marketing revenues reflect the gross value of the commodities sold, segment revenues can rise or fall sharply with commodity price levels, even when the margin we earn changes far less.

SALES AND MARKETING

We sell our services through a commercial organization arranged by segment and region. Commercial teams are responsible for:

  • originating new agreements;
  • renewing and extending existing agreements;
  • marketing available capacity; and
  • developing expansion projects with customer support.

The teams work closely with our engineering and operations groups to design services around customers' production profiles, refinery configurations and delivery needs.

On our interstate pipelines, we generally offer capacity through open season processes. We allocate capacity under terms and conditions set out in tariffs approved by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission ("FERC"). We often underwrite expansion projects through binding precedent agreements, in which shippers commit to long-term firm service before we make a final investment decision. In the Gathering and Processing segment, we compete for acreage dedications and new well connections by offering reliable service, competitive fees, and access to multiple downstream markets through our integrated system.

Our marketing personnel buy and sell commodities in physical markets. Most of these transactions settle in the month following delivery. We maintain risk management policies, approved by senior management and overseen by the audit committee of our board of directors, that establish:

  • position limits;
  • approved products and counterparties;
  • credit procedures; and
  • valuation and reporting requirements.

COMPETITION

The midstream industry is highly competitive. In every region where we operate, we compete with other midstream companies of various sizes, the midstream affiliates of integrated oil and gas companies, producer-owned gathering systems, and privately funded infrastructure developers. Competition rests mainly on:

  • rates and fees;
  • system reliability and available capacity;
  • the quality and flexibility of services;
  • connectivity to supply sources and end-use markets; and
  • the ability to develop new capacity on a timely and cost-effective basis.

In the Gathering and Processing segment, we compete for dedications of acreage and production. Our competitors include other third-party gatherers and processors, as well as producers that choose to build their own infrastructure. In the Transportation and Storage segment, we compete with other pipelines that serve the same supply regions and demand markets. We also compete with alternative modes of transportation, including rail, truck and marine carriers, and with storage operators in the same hubs. In the Terminals and Marketing Services segment, we compete with independent terminal operators, refiner-owned terminals, and other marketers and trading firms.

We believe our competitive strengths include:

  • the scale and geographic breadth of our asset base;
  • the integration of our systems across the value chain;
  • our access to multiple downstream markets, including export facilities;
  • our long-standing customer relationships; and
  • our record of executing expansion projects.

New pipeline and processing capacity built by competitors, changes in production patterns, or shifts in demand could reduce volumes on our systems or put pressure on the rates we receive.

SEASONALITY

Demand for natural gas and certain NGLs, particularly propane, generally peaks during the winter heating season. Demand for refined products, especially gasoline, generally rises in the summer driving season. As a result, volumes on parts of our transportation and terminalling systems vary from quarter to quarter. Firm reservation charges and minimum volume commitments, which are generally collected evenly through the year, reduce the effect of this variation on our results.

Our storage assets tend to benefit from seasonal price differences. Customers inject volumes in lower-demand periods and withdraw them when demand is high. Severe weather can disrupt our operations and those of our customers, temporarily reducing throughput or increasing operating costs. Examples include hurricanes along the Gulf Coast, winter storms, extreme heat and flooding. Taken as a whole, our results are not significantly seasonal. However, individual quarters may be affected by weather, maintenance turnarounds at customer facilities and planned outages on our own systems.

GOVERNMENT REGULATION

Our operations are subject to extensive federal, state, local and, in certain cases, tribal laws and regulations. These rules govern the rates and terms of our services, the safety of our pipelines and facilities, and the protection of the environment. Compliance requires significant expenditures and continuous attention. A failure to comply could result in fines, penalties, injunctive relief or the suspension of operations.

Interstate Natural Gas Pipeline Regulation

Our interstate natural gas pipelines are regulated by FERC under the Natural Gas Act and the Natural Gas Policy Act. FERC regulates many aspects of these pipelines, including:

  • the rates and terms and conditions of service;
  • the certification and construction of new facilities;
  • the extension or abandonment of service and facilities;
  • the acquisition and disposition of facilities;
  • accounting and record keeping; and
  • standards of conduct governing relationships with affiliates.

Rates charged for interstate natural gas transportation and storage must be just and reasonable. FERC may initiate proceedings to review existing rates, and shippers may file complaints challenging them.

Interstate Liquids Pipeline Regulation

Our interstate pipelines carrying crude oil, refined products and NGLs are subject to FERC regulation under the Interstate Commerce Act and the Energy Policy Act of 1992. These pipelines must publish tariffs that set out their rates and terms of service. Under FERC's indexing methodology, pipelines may generally adjust their rates each year within ceiling levels tied to an inflation-based index. Other ratemaking methods, such as cost-of-service filings, negotiated rates and market-based rates, are available in certain circumstances. Shippers may challenge new or changed rates, and in some cases existing rates, through protests and complaints.

Intrastate and Gathering Regulation

Our intrastate pipelines are generally regulated by the public utility or railroad commissions of the states in which they operate. That regulation varies from state to state. It may include rate regulation, nondiscriminatory access requirements, and complaint-based oversight. Our gathering facilities are generally exempt from FERC rate regulation. However, they may be subject to state requirements concerning ratable take, common purchaser rules, and nondiscriminatory access. Whether a facility performs gathering or transportation is a fact-based determination, and the classification of our facilities is subject to change.

Pipeline Safety

Our pipelines are subject to regulation by the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration ("PHMSA") of the U.S. Department of Transportation, and by state agencies acting under agreements with PHMSA. Applicable regulations govern:

  • design, construction, testing and operation;
  • maintenance and inspection;
  • integrity management in high-consequence areas;
  • control room management;
  • operator qualification;
  • public awareness; and
  • incident reporting.

Over time, PHMSA has extended its regulations to cover a broader set of gathering lines and has raised integrity verification requirements. We expect regulatory requirements in this area to keep evolving. Our storage facilities are also subject to federal and state safety and operational rules, including requirements specific to underground natural gas and hydrocarbon storage.

RISKS RELATED TO OUR BUSINESS AND INDUSTRY

Our results depend on the volumes of hydrocarbons that producers, refiners and shippers deliver to our systems, and those volumes are influenced by factors we do not control.

The throughput on our gathering systems, processing plants, pipelines, storage facilities and terminals depends on drilling and completion activity in the basins we serve, refinery utilization, petrochemical demand and export flows. Producers set their capital budgets based on commodity prices, access to financing, investor expectations about capital discipline, well productivity and the cost of oilfield services. A sustained decline in crude oil, natural gas or NGL prices, or a shift by producers toward returning capital rather than drilling, could reduce new well connections and allow natural production declines to outpace new supply. As a result, volumes on our systems could fall. Even where our contracts include minimum volume commitments or acreage dedications, these protections expire over time, may be renegotiated in a downturn and do not apply to all of our assets.

Lower volumes would reduce fee revenue in our Gathering and Processing segment. Because much of the gas we process flows onward into our own transportation, fractionation and storage assets, a volume shortfall at the wellhead can also lower utilization and revenue in our Transportation and Storage and Terminals and Marketing Services segments. Our cost of revenue and other operating expenses include a substantial fixed component, such as pipeline integrity, compression, labor and property taxes, that does not decline in proportion to throughput. Lower volumes could therefore reduce our margins by more than they reduce our revenue.

A portion of our earnings is directly exposed to commodity prices, and our hedging activities may not fully protect us.

Although we structure most of our agreements on a fee basis, certain percent-of-proceeds processing arrangements allow us to retain a share of the residue gas and NGLs we process. As a result, our earnings under those arrangements fall when commodity prices or processing spreads decline. Our marketing activities purchase and resell crude oil, NGLs, natural gas and refined products, and their results depend on location and time spreads that can narrow or reverse quickly. Because marketing revenues are recorded on a gross basis, changes in commodity prices can cause large swings in reported revenue and cost of revenue. Those swings can make our results harder to compare from period to period.

We use derivative instruments to reduce some of this exposure. However, our hedges cover only a portion of our expected volumes and periods, and they may be ineffective because of basis differences between the hedged index and our actual sales points. Hedges can also require us to post collateral when prices move against our positions, which could strain liquidity during periods of market volatility. In addition, our counterparties may fail to perform.

We depend on a limited number of large customers in certain segments, and their financial difficulty or loss of their business could materially affect our results.

Several large producers, refiners, utilities and exporters account for a meaningful share of revenue in individual segments. If one of these customers reduced its volumes, did not renew its contracts, sought to reject its agreements in a bankruptcy proceeding or failed to pay amounts owed, the affected segment's results could decline materially. Producer bankruptcies in prior industry downturns resulted in attempts to reject gathering and processing agreements, and courts have reached differing conclusions on whether acreage dedications survive such proceedings. Our credit support requirements, such as letters of credit, guarantees and prepayments, may be insufficient to cover our losses. Our accounts receivable have grown along with our revenue, which increases the amount at risk if a customer defaults.

We may be unable to renew or replace expiring contracts on comparable terms.

Our firm transportation, storage, gathering and terminalling agreements have finite terms. When they expire, customers may seek lower rates, shorter terms, reduced commitments or more flexible service, particularly where competing infrastructure has been built or where supply and demand patterns have shifted. If we cannot recontract our capacity at rates and terms similar to those we receive today, or if capacity remains unsubscribed, our revenue and cash flows could decline. Expiring reservation charges would also reduce the predictability of our cash flows that our contract structure is designed to provide.

We face substantial competition across our segments.

We compete with other midstream companies, integrated energy companies, producer-owned gathering systems, private equity-backed developers and, in some markets, rail and truck transportation. Competitors may have greater financial resources, lower costs of capital, more favorable asset locations or the willingness to accept lower returns to secure volumes. New pipeline or processing capacity built by competitors in basins or corridors we serve could divert volumes from our systems and put pressure on our rates when contracts come up for renewal. Customers increasingly can choose among several service providers. If we cannot keep our unit costs competitive, we may lose new business to those competitors.

Long-term changes in demand for hydrocarbons could reduce the use and value of our assets.

Our assets are long-lived and were sized and constructed in anticipation of decades of service. Policies that favor electrification, renewable power, energy efficiency and alternative fuels, together with technological advances and changing consumer preferences, could reduce long-term demand for crude oil, refined products, natural gas and NGLs in the markets we serve. Lower demand could lead to reduced throughput, shorter contract terms, lower rates or the early retirement of assets. It could also impair the carrying value of our property, plant and equipment and goodwill. Some investors and lenders have reduced their exposure to the fossil fuel sector, and further such actions could raise our cost of capital. The timing and extent of the energy transition are uncertain, and our planning assumptions may prove incorrect.

Our investments in lower-carbon businesses may not achieve expected returns.

We are evaluating and pursuing opportunities in carbon dioxide transportation and sequestration, renewable feedstock and fuel logistics, and hydrogen handling. These businesses depend heavily on government incentives, evolving regulatory frameworks, unproven commercial structures and technologies that have not been deployed at scale. Carbon dioxide pipelines in particular have faced significant public opposition and permitting challenges in several states. If incentives are reduced or repealed, if customer demand develops more slowly than expected, or if we cannot obtain permits and rights-of-way, we may be unable to recover our investments in these opportunities.

Acquisitions may not perform as expected and could result in goodwill or asset impairments.

We have grown in part through acquisitions, and goodwill represents a significant portion of our total assets. Acquired businesses may not achieve the volumes, cost savings or commercial integration we anticipated. We may assume liabilities, including environmental, safety, tax or litigation liabilities, that we did not identify during due diligence. Integrating acquired assets, systems and personnel also requires significant management attention and may disrupt our existing operations. If the performance of an acquired business or a reporting unit deteriorates, or if market conditions or discount rates change unfavorably, we may be required to record impairment charges. Such charges could be material to our results of operations and stockholders' equity.

Our growth projects are subject to construction, cost and schedule risks.

We invest substantial capital in expansions, new pipelines, processing plants, fractionators and terminals. These projects require federal, state and local permits, rights-of-way, specialized labor and long-lead equipment such as compressors, pipe and cryogenic components. Delays in permitting, litigation by project opponents, adverse weather, contractor performance problems, supply chain disruptions, tariffs on imported steel and inflation in labor and materials costs could cause cost overruns or delays. Such problems could also cause us to abandon projects after significant expenditures have been made. In many cases, our commercial agreements fix the rates we may charge before construction is complete. As a result, cost overruns may reduce our returns, and delays may defer revenue while our financing costs continue to accrue.

RISKS RELATED TO OPERATIONS

Our operations involve hazards that could result in injury, loss of life, environmental damage and significant liabilities, and our insurance may not cover all losses.

Gathering, processing, fractionating, transporting, storing and handling hydrocarbons involves inherent risks. These include pipeline ruptures, leaks, fires, explosions, releases of toxic gases such as hydrogen sulfide, cavern instability, equipment failure, vessel incidents at our docks and third-party damage from excavation. Any of these events could cause personal injury or death, property damage, environmental contamination, evacuation of nearby communities and the suspension of operations. They could also expose us to government investigations, fines, remediation obligations and litigation. Incidents involving our assets or the assets of other operators may also lead to more stringent regulation and damage our reputation with regulators, communities and customers.

We maintain insurance at levels we believe are customary for our industry. However, our policies contain deductibles, self-insured retentions, sublimits and exclusions, and certain risks, such as some environmental and cyber-related losses, may be uninsurable or available only at prohibitive cost. Conditions in the insurance market have at times reduced available capacity and raised premiums for energy infrastructure operators. An uninsured or underinsured loss could have a material adverse effect on our financial condition and results of operations.

Severe weather and changing climate conditions could damage our assets and disrupt our operations.

Many of our assets are located in regions exposed to hurricanes, flooding, extreme heat, winter storms, wildfires and drought, including Gulf Coast fractionation, storage and marine terminal facilities. Severe weather events can damage facilities and interrupt power supplies, and they can force shutdowns of producer, refinery and petrochemical operations that supply or receive our volumes. Extreme cold can cause wellhead freeze-offs that sharply reduce gathered volumes, while elevated temperatures can reduce the capacity of compression and processing equipment. Over time, more frequent or intense weather events could raise our repair, adaptation and insurance costs and reduce our revenue.

A cyberattack or failure of our technology systems could disrupt our operations and harm our business.

Our growing use of centralized operations and predictive maintenance increases our dependence on these systems. A successful attack, or a failure of systems maintained by us or our vendors, could disrupt pipeline operations, cause physical damage or safety incidents, compromise confidential information and delay invoicing and collections. Federal security directives applicable to pipeline operators impose additional compliance requirements, and our security measures may not prevent or detect every intrusion.

Aging infrastructure and expanding integrity requirements could increase our costs.

Portions of our pipeline and facility network were constructed decades ago. Maintaining the integrity of older assets requires ongoing inline inspection, repair, replacement and cathodic protection. Inspections may reveal conditions that require unplanned capital expenditures, pressure reductions or temporary shutdowns. If our maintenance costs rise faster than we can recover them through rates or contract escalators, our margins would decline.

We depend on third-party infrastructure, suppliers and service providers.

Our operations rely on interconnecting pipelines, fractionators, refineries, export terminals, rail carriers and electric utilities owned by others. Outages, capacity constraints or curtailments at these facilities could prevent us from receiving or delivering volumes. Our success depends on attracting and retaining a skilled workforce.

Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Risk Management and Strategy

Our operations depend on the secure and reliable functioning of the information technology ("IT") systems that support our business and the operational technology ("OT") systems that monitor and control our gathering systems, processing plants, fractionation facilities, pipelines, storage assets and terminals. These OT systems include supervisory control and data acquisition systems, our centralized control rooms and field automation equipment. A compromise of these systems could disrupt the flow of hydrocarbons to our customers, impair safe operations or expose sensitive commercial and personal information. We have therefore built a cybersecurity program intended to identify, assess and manage risks from cybersecurity threats across our IT and OT environments.

Our program draws on recognized industry frameworks and on the security requirements that federal authorities apply to pipeline owners and operators. Its principal elements include:

  • segmentation of OT networks from business networks, with restricted and monitored connections between them;
  • identity and access management controls, including multifactor authentication and periodic review of privileged accounts;
  • continuous security monitoring, with alerts reviewed by a dedicated security operations function;
  • vulnerability management and patching processes adapted to the operating constraints of control systems that run continuously;
  • endpoint protection, data backup and recovery capabilities designed to support restoration of critical systems;
  • annual awareness training for employees and contractors, supplemented by simulated phishing campaigns and role-specific training for control room and field personnel; and
  • a written incident response plan that sets out escalation paths, decision-making authority and coordination with operations, legal, communications and executive leadership.

We test the effectiveness of these measures through internal reviews, penetration testing and tabletop exercises. Exercise scenarios address events such as ransomware affecting business systems, unauthorized access to control systems and disruption at a third-party provider. Participants range from operations personnel to senior management. We use the results to refine our controls, response procedures and investment priorities.

Cybersecurity risk forms part of our enterprise risk management process, which our senior leadership uses to identify and prioritize risks across the Company. Through that process, cybersecurity risks are weighed alongside operational, safety, commercial, financial and regulatory risks. This lets us coordinate cybersecurity planning with our integrity management, emergency response and business continuity programs. We also consider cybersecurity when we design new facilities and when we integrate acquired businesses. Acquired systems are assessed and brought into our security architecture as part of integration planning.

We supplement our internal capabilities with outside expertise. Independent firms periodically assess the maturity of our cybersecurity program and test selected controls. We also retain outside specialists to support incident response and forensic investigation when needed. Our security team gathers threat intelligence from government agencies, industry information-sharing organizations, trade associations and commercial sources. It uses that intelligence to anticipate threats specific to energy infrastructure.

Third-Party Risk

We rely on vendors, software providers, cloud service providers, contractors and other third parties, some of whom have access to our systems or data. Before engaging a provider that presents meaningful cybersecurity risk, we conduct a security review scaled to the sensitivity of the service. Where appropriate, our contracts include security requirements, incident notification obligations and audit rights. We monitor higher-risk providers over the course of the relationship. These measures reduce, but cannot eliminate, the risk that an incident at a third party affects our operations.

Assessment of Cybersecurity Risks

As of the date of this Annual Report on Form 10-K, we are not aware of any risks from cybersecurity threats, including as a result of any previous cybersecurity incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect the Company, including our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. Cybersecurity threats against energy infrastructure continue to grow in frequency and sophistication, however, and our measures may not prevent or detect every incident. A future incident could have a material adverse effect on our operations, financial condition or reputation. For additional discussion, see Item 1A, Risk Factors.

Governance

Board Oversight

Our board of directors oversees our enterprise risks, including risks from cybersecurity threats. The board has assigned primary oversight of cybersecurity risk to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives reports from management on the cybersecurity program at regularly scheduled meetings. These reports address the threat landscape, program performance, results of assessments and exercises, significant incidents and planned investments. The Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full board, and the full board receives periodic briefings on cybersecurity as part of its review of enterprise risk. Directors may also consult directly with the Chief Information Security Officer between meetings.

Management's Role

Our Chief Information Security Officer ("CISO") leads the day-to-day management of cybersecurity risk. The CISO is responsible for security architecture, security operations, threat intelligence, vulnerability management, OT security, third-party risk and incident response. The CISO has extensive experience leading security programs in the energy and industrial sectors, including the protection of control systems for critical infrastructure. The CISO holds an advanced degree in a technical discipline and maintains recognized professional security certifications.

The CISO reports to our Chief Information Officer, who reports to our Chief Executive Officer and has broad experience leading technology functions at large industrial organizations. The CISO and Chief Information Officer participate in our enterprise risk management committee, made up of senior executives. Through that committee, they inform management of cybersecurity risks and the status of mitigation efforts.

Under our incident response plan, the CISO is informed promptly of significant cybersecurity events. The CISO then escalates such events to senior management, our legal function and, where warranted, the Audit Committee. A cross-functional team evaluates the materiality of incidents and the related disclosure obligations.

Overview

Orzenka Midstream, Inc. is a Delaware corporation headquartered in Phoenix, Arizona. We own, operate and develop energy infrastructure that moves crude oil, natural gas, NGLs and refined products from the wellhead and the refinery gate to end-use markets in the United States and to export points. Our assets include:

  • gathering systems;
  • processing plants;
  • fractionation facilities;
  • transportation pipelines;
  • above-ground and underground storage; and
  • marine, rail and truck terminals.

At the end of fiscal 2024 we employed about 177,300 people.

We manage our business through three reportable segments:

  • Gathering and Processing;
  • Transportation and Storage; and
  • Terminals and Marketing Services.

Our business model rests on long-term commercial agreements. Many of these agreements contain fixed fees per unit of throughput, minimum volume commitments, acreage dedications or reservation charges that are payable whether or not capacity is used. These features limit our direct exposure to commodity prices and add stability to our cash flows. Some activities, mainly percent-of-proceeds processing arrangements and our marketing operations, remain exposed to commodity prices and price spreads. Our marketing revenues are recorded on a gross basis. As a result, changes in commodity price levels can affect reported revenue and cost of revenue considerably more than they affect our margins.

Fiscal 2024 was a year of growth for the Company:

  • Revenue was $52,746 million, an increase of 15.1% from $45,826 million in fiscal 2023.
  • Operating income was $21,077 million, an increase of 15.9% from $18,179 million.
  • Net income was $13,156 million, an increase of 16.2% from $11,325 million.
  • Operating margin improved to 40.0% from 39.7%.
  • Net margin improved to 24.9% from 24.7%.

Growth came from three sources. Volumes on our existing systems increased. Expansion projects placed in service during the year contributed to results. Assets we acquired in prior periods contributed for a full year.

We generated $17,777 million of net cash from operating activities. We used these funds, together with net borrowings, to:

  • invest in our asset base and complete acquisitions;
  • pay dividends of $3,564 million; and
  • repurchase common stock, net of issuances, of $4,499 million.

Cash and cash equivalents rose to $10,049 million at December 31, 2024 from $8,785 million a year earlier.

Significant Developments in 2024

Strategic

  • We completed acquisitions of midstream businesses and assets that extend our footprint in basins and corridors adjacent to our existing systems. These transactions added gathering, transportation and storage capacity that we expect to integrate with our existing operations over time. They are the principal reason that goodwill increased to $15,677 million at December 31, 2024 from $13,577 million a year earlier.
  • We continued to evaluate opportunities that use our existing rights-of-way, engineering capabilities and customer relationships to serve lower-carbon markets. These include carbon dioxide transportation and sequestration, renewable fuel logistics and hydrogen handling. We pursue such opportunities only where commercial structures support returns consistent with our investment criteria.

Operational

  • We placed several expansion and debottlenecking projects in service across our gathering, pipeline and terminal systems. Each project was supported by long-term commercial agreements with producers, shippers and end users.
  • Throughput on our systems increased because producers in the basins we serve kept up drilling and completion activity and because demand from export, petrochemical and power generation customers remained firm.

Financial

  • We issued long-term debt to fund acquisitions and growth capital. Long-term debt was $85,152 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $73,377 million at December 31, 2023.
  • We returned capital to stockholders through dividends of $3,564 million and share repurchases, net of issuances, of $4,499 million.

Business Environment

Activity in the basins we serve stayed healthy during 2024. Producers continued to emphasize capital discipline and returns to their own investors. Even so, well productivity, efficiency gains in drilling and completion, and steady demand for crude oil, natural gas and NGLs supported continued growth in supply connected to our systems.

Demand-side conditions were also supportive:

  • Domestic demand for natural gas from power generators and industrial users remained strong.
  • Liquefied natural gas export capacity on the Gulf Coast continued to draw natural gas supply toward coastal markets.
  • Petrochemical demand for ethane and other NGL purity products, together with export demand for propane and butane, supported fractionation, storage and dock utilization.
  • Refined product demand in the metropolitan markets served by our product pipelines was generally stable.

Commodity prices were volatile during the year. Because most of our revenue is fee-based, price movements affected our margins far less than they affected the gross revenue reported by our marketing activities. Several conditions raised our costs:

  • inflation in labor, materials and contracted services;
  • higher interest rates; and
  • tariffs and supply chain constraints affecting steel pipe and long-lead equipment.

We expect these factors to remain relevant to our operating and capital costs in fiscal 2025.

Debt

  • fund acquisitions and growth capital expenditures;
  • refinance maturing obligations; and
  • for general corporate purposes.

We also use our revolving credit facilities and commercial paper program to manage short-term liquidity needs.

Our debt agreements contain customary covenants, including limits on our leverage ratio and restrictions on liens and certain transactions. We were in compliance with all covenants at December 31, 2024. We seek to stagger our debt maturities to limit refinancing risk in any single year. We monitor our leverage relative to our cash flows and the expectations of the credit rating agencies, and we intend to manage our capital structure to maintain our investment grade credit ratings. Higher interest rates on new and refinanced debt have increased our interest expense. A downgrade of our credit ratings could further increase our borrowing costs and reduce our access to capital.

Critical Accounting Estimates

We prepare our consolidated financial statements in accordance with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. This requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses, as well as related disclosures. We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions and assumptions we believe are reasonable. Actual results may differ from these estimates. The estimates described below involve a significant degree of judgment and could have a material effect on our financial condition or results of operations. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements in Item 8.

Property, Plant and Equipment and Depreciation

Property, plant and equipment, net was $129,641 million at December 31, 2024, the largest component of our total assets of $172,373 million. We depreciate these assets on a straight-line basis over their estimated useful lives. In estimating useful lives, we consider:

  • the physical condition of the assets;
  • technological change;
  • expected supply of and demand for the hydrocarbons we handle; and
  • regulatory and contractual factors.

Changes in these assumptions, including assumptions about the long-term demand for hydrocarbons discussed in Item 1A, could shorten estimated useful lives and increase depreciation expense.

Impairment of Long-Lived Assets

We evaluate long-lived assets for impairment whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amount may not be recoverable. Examples include:

  • a significant decline in throughput;
  • the loss or bankruptcy of a significant customer;
  • adverse regulatory developments; or
  • a sustained decline in commodity prices affecting the basins we serve.

The recoverability test compares the carrying amount of an asset group with its estimated undiscounted future cash flows. If the carrying amount is not recoverable, we measure the impairment as the excess of the carrying amount over fair value, which we generally estimate using discounted cash flows. Our cash flow estimates require judgment about future volumes, rates, contract renewals, operating costs, capital requirements and the remaining life of the assets. No material impairments of long-lived assets were recorded in fiscal 2024.

Goodwill

Goodwill was $15,677 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment at the reporting unit level at least annually, and more often if events or circumstances indicate that the fair value of a reporting unit may be below its carrying amount. We may first assess qualitative factors. When we perform a quantitative test, we estimate the fair value of each reporting unit using a combination of income and market approaches. The key assumptions are:

  • forecasted volumes, rates and operating margins;
  • capital expenditures;
  • long-term growth rates;
  • discount rates reflecting our weighted average cost of capital and the risks of each reporting unit; and
  • valuation multiples of comparable companies.

Based on our most recent annual test, the estimated fair value of each reporting unit exceeded its carrying amount. Adverse changes could result in impairment charges in future periods. Such changes could include lower producer activity in a basin, higher discount rates or underperformance of recently acquired businesses.

Business Combinations

We account for acquisitions of businesses using the acquisition method. We allocate the purchase price to the assets acquired and liabilities assumed based on their estimated fair values at the acquisition date. Any excess of the purchase price over these amounts is recorded as goodwill. Estimating the fair value of property, plant and equipment and of intangible assets such as customer relationships requires significant judgment about:

  • replacement costs;
  • expected cash flows;
  • customer attrition rates;
  • contract renewal probabilities; and
  • discount rates.

These estimates affect the amount of goodwill we recognize, future depreciation and amortization expense, and the carrying amounts tested for impairment. We may adjust provisional amounts during the measurement period, which does not exceed one year from the acquisition date.

Revenue Recognition

We recognize revenue as we satisfy our performance obligations, which is generally as we provide gathering, processing, transportation, storage and terminalling services, or when we deliver commodities to customers. Judgment is required in several areas:

  • identifying performance obligations in contracts with multiple services;
  • determining whether we act as principal or agent in commodity transactions, which determines whether revenue is reported on a gross or net basis;
  • estimating variable consideration; and
  • accounting for minimum volume commitments.

Where customers fall short of their minimum volume commitments, we estimate whether the customer will make up the shortfall within the allowed period. We recognize deficiency payments as revenue when the likelihood of make-up becomes remote or when the make-up period expires.

Income Taxes

Our provision for income taxes was $4,089 million in fiscal 2024. Determining our income tax provision, deferred tax assets and liabilities, and liabilities for uncertain tax positions requires judgment in interpreting complex federal and state tax laws. We recognize the benefit of a tax position only when it is more likely than not to be sustained on examination. We estimate the timing of the reversal of temporary differences. Changes in tax laws, in the mix of earnings among jurisdictions, or in the outcome of examinations could affect our effective tax rate in future periods.

Asset Retirement and Environmental Obligations

We record asset retirement obligations when we have a legal obligation to retire an asset and can reasonably estimate the fair value of that obligation. Many of our pipelines and facilities have indeterminate lives, because we expect to operate and maintain them indefinitely. For those assets, we cannot reasonably estimate the settlement date, and no obligation is recorded. We accrue environmental remediation costs when a loss is probable and can be reasonably estimated. These estimates depend on assumptions about remediation methods, regulatory requirements, cost inflation and the timing of expenditures.
