Item 1A. Risk Factors

An investment in our securities involves risk. The risks and uncertainties described below are those we currently consider material to our business, financial condition, results of operations and cash flows. Any of them could cause our actual results to differ materially from our historical results or from the expectations expressed in forward-looking statements in this Annual Report on Form 10-K. They are not the only risks we face. Additional risks that we do not presently know of, or that we currently believe are immaterial, could also adversely affect us. You should read these risk factors together with the other information in this report, including Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations and our Consolidated Financial Statements and related notes.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Our results depend heavily on the commercial aerospace industry, which is cyclical and sensitive to conditions we cannot control.

A large portion of our revenue comes from original equipment content and aftermarket services for commercial aircraft. Demand for new aircraft and for maintenance services depends on airline profitability, passenger and cargo traffic, fuel prices, the availability and cost of aircraft financing, fleet retirement decisions, and general economic conditions in the regions our customers serve. Historically, the industry has experienced sharp downturns triggered by recessions, terrorist attacks, public health emergencies, armed conflicts and fuel price shocks. These downturns have often been followed by order cancellations, deferred deliveries and reduced flight activity.

Because our cost structure includes a substantial base of fixed manufacturing and engineering costs, a decline in volume can reduce our margins more than proportionally. A prolonged downturn in commercial aerospace could materially reduce our revenue, profitability and operating cash flow. It could also require us to restructure operations or record impairments of long-lived assets.

Changes in the production rates of a small number of airframe and engine programs could significantly affect our revenue and profitability.

Our Aerostructures and Propulsion Systems segments supply content to a limited number of high-volume commercial programs. Revenue in these segments tracks our customers' build rates closely. Our customers may reduce, pause or delay planned rate increases because of their own supply chain constraints, quality or certification issues, regulatory actions, labor disputes or weaker airline demand. Production pauses or rate reductions on a major program can leave us carrying excess inventory, underutilized capacity and workforce costs that we cannot quickly adjust.

Conversely, rapid rate increases require us to add labor, tooling and supplier capacity ahead of revenue. If we are unable to execute these ramps efficiently, we may incur premium freight, overtime and expediting costs. We could also face contractual penalties or harm to customer relationships.

We depend on a concentrated group of customers, and the loss of or a significant reduction in business from any of them could materially harm us.

The commercial airframe and engine industries are dominated by a small number of manufacturers. As a result, a relatively small group of customers accounts for a significant share of our revenue. These customers have substantial negotiating leverage over pricing, payment terms, warranty provisions and the allocation of development costs. They may pursue vertical integration, dual sourcing or supplier consolidation strategies, any of which could reduce our share of future work.

A customer's financial difficulties, a dispute with a customer, or the loss of a position on a major program could materially reduce our revenue and earnings. Such events could also result in impairment of the related tooling, capitalized costs or other program assets.

Many of our long-term agreements are fixed-price, and cost overruns could cause losses.

We perform a substantial portion of our work under multi-year agreements that fix the price per unit for a defined quantity or period. Our profitability under these arrangements depends on our ability to estimate accurately, and then control, labor hours, material costs, supplier pricing, learning-curve improvements and overhead absorption over the life of the agreement. Several developments could cause our actual costs to exceed our estimates:

- Inflation in wages, energy and raw materials
- Engineering changes or technical difficulties
- Supplier failures
- Lower-than-expected production volumes

We recognize revenue on many of these contracts over time using estimates of total contract costs. When our estimates change, we may need to record cumulative adjustments that reduce previously recognized profit. When estimated costs exceed estimated revenue, we must record a forward loss provision for the full amount of the anticipated loss in the period it becomes evident. Such adjustments could be material to our results in any given period.

We face significant competition, and pricing pressure could reduce our margins.

We compete with large, diversified aerospace suppliers, specialized component manufacturers, low-cost producers in emerging markets, and the in-house capabilities of our customers. In the aftermarket, we also compete with independent repair providers and with suppliers of non-original equipment replacement parts approved by aviation regulators. These alternatives can be offered at lower prices than parts produced under original equipment design authority.

Competitors may develop superior technologies, achieve lower costs or offer more favorable commercial terms. Customers routinely seek annual price reductions and greater supplier participation in development costs. If we cannot maintain our technical differentiation, cost competitiveness and delivery performance, we may lose program awards and aftermarket share or be forced to accept lower prices.

Demand for our aftermarket services depends on fleet utilization and maintenance practices that may change.

Our Aftermarket Services segment, and the spare parts portion of our Propulsion Systems segment, depend on aircraft flight hours, cycles and the timing of scheduled maintenance events. Reduced flight activity, early retirement of older aircraft that carry our content, or the deferral of shop visits by airlines seeking to conserve cash would reduce demand for our services and parts. The increasing use of used serviceable material from retired aircraft could also reduce that demand.

Operators and lessors may also shift maintenance work to engine and airframe manufacturers under long-term service agreements that we do not participate in. In addition, newer aircraft and engine designs may require less frequent maintenance or fewer replacement parts. Any of these trends could reduce the recurring revenue that we expect our installed base to generate.

Our defense and space business depends on government spending priorities that can change.

A meaningful portion of our revenue comes from military and space programs funded by the United States government and allied governments, either directly or through prime contractors. Government budgets are subject to political negotiation, competing priorities, continuing resolutions, potential shutdowns and statutory spending limits. Specific programs on which we have content may be reduced, delayed or cancelled. Funding may also shift toward platforms or technologies in which we do not participate.

The space launch market includes new entrants and programs with uncertain technical and financial prospects. The failure or delay of a launch program on which we supply hardware could reduce our revenue. It could also leave us with unrecovered investment.

Our investments in new programs and technologies may not generate adequate returns.

We invest significant amounts in research and development, design, qualification, tooling and production capacity, often years before a new platform enters service. Our recovery of these investments depends on several factors outside our control:

- The platform's commercial success and production volumes over its life
- The timing of certification and entry into service
- Our continued status as a supplier on the program

Development programs frequently encounter technical challenges, schedule delays and changes in customer requirements. These can increase our costs and defer revenue. If a program is cancelled, achieves lower volumes than expected, or experiences prolonged delays, we may not recover our investment and may be required to record impairments or losses.

The aerospace industry's transition toward lower-emission aircraft and propulsion could change demand for our products.

Airframe and engine manufacturers, airlines and regulators are pursuing reductions in aviation emissions. They are doing so through more efficient engines, new airframe configurations, sustainable aviation fuels, and hybrid-electric and hydrogen propulsion concepts. These developments may require materials, manufacturing processes and system architectures that differ from those in which we have historically specialized. They may also alter the mix and volume of components on future aircraft.

The pace and direction of this transition are uncertain. If we fail to anticipate technological change, or invest in technologies that do not achieve market acceptance, our competitive position on next-generation platforms could weaken. In addition, regulations or taxes intended to curb aviation emissions could reduce air travel demand and, in turn, demand for our products and services.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Disruptions in our supply chain could prevent us from meeting customer commitments.

We rely on suppliers for raw materials, forgings, castings, fasteners, electronic components and specialized processing services. Many of these suppliers are sole or limited sources that have been qualified to stringent aerospace standards. Qualifying replacements can take months or years. The aerospace supply base has experienced capacity constraints, labor shortages and financial stress in recent years, and certain suppliers have struggled to meet delivery schedules.

A supplier's failure to deliver conforming material on time could interrupt our production and delay deliveries to our customers. Such a failure could also cause us to incur additional costs or contractual penalties. A significant supplier disruption could have a material adverse effect on our results of operations and customer relationships.

Increases in the cost or reduced availability of specialty metals and other materials could adversely affect our margins.

Our products use significant quantities of titanium, nickel-based superalloys, aluminum, specialty steels, carbon fiber and resins. The markets for several of these materials are concentrated among a small number of producers. Some materials are sourced from regions affected by geopolitical tension, sanctions or export restrictions. Prices and availability can change rapidly.

Our long-term customer agreements do not always include escalation clauses that fully pass through material cost increases. Where such clauses exist, they may operate with a lag. Rising material costs, or the need to secure alternative sources at higher prices, could reduce our gross margin and operating cash flow. Because inventories represent a significant portion of our current assets, we also face the risk of obsolescence or valuation adjustments if program requirements or demand change.

Inflation and higher operating costs could reduce our profitability.

We have experienced inflationary pressure on wages, benefits, energy, freight, purchased services and materials. In addition, our selling, general and administrative expenses grew faster than revenue during fiscal 2024. Our ability to offset these increases through productivity improvements, pricing actions or contract escalation provisions is limited by the fixed-price nature of many of our agreements and by our customers' bargaining power. Persistent inflation could compress our margins and reduce the cash available to invest in our business or return to stockholders.

We may be unable to attract, train and retain the skilled workforce our operations require.

Our operations depend on approximately 27,000 employees, including engineers, certified machinists, composite technicians, inspectors and licensed maintenance personnel. Competition for these skills is intense throughout the aerospace and defense industry, and the time needed to train new employees to required proficiency is substantial. High turnover or difficulty in hiring could reduce our productivity, increase our labor costs and impair our ability to support customer rate increases.

A portion of our workforce is represented by labor unions or works councils. Work stoppages or strikes at our facilities, at our customers or at our suppliers could disrupt production. So could difficult collective bargaining negotiations. In addition, certain programs require employees to hold security clearances, which further limits the available labor pool.

Product defects, quality escapes or aviation accidents could result in significant costs and reputational harm.

Many of our products are flight-critical, and a failure could contribute to an aircraft accident involving loss of life. A defect or nonconformance in our products or repairs could lead to recalls, retrofit campaigns, warranty claims and customer charge-backs. It could also lead to the grounding of aircraft, regulatory investigations or product liability litigation. Regulators and customers have heightened their scrutiny of manufacturing quality across the aerospace supply chain. Quality findings at our facilities could result in production holds or the loss of certifications.

Our insurance may not cover all potential liabilities, and coverage may become more expensive or unavailable. A significant quality failure could materially affect our financial results. It could also damage our reputation with customers and regulators in ways that impair future program awards.

Our capital-intensive operations expose us to risks of facility disruption and asset impairment.

Property, plant and equipment represents the largest component of our total assets, reflecting the specialized machinery, autoclaves, tooling and assembly facilities our products require. Many of our production processes are concentrated at single sites, and qualified alternative capacity may not be readily available. Disruption at a key facility could halt production of certain products for an extended period. Possible causes include fire, equipment failure, severe weather, earthquakes, flooding, utility outages or other events. The frequency and severity of extreme weather events may increase as a result of climate change.

If demand for the products made with these assets declines, or a program is cancelled, we may be required to record impairment charges. Significant reductions in volume could also leave us with underutilized fixed assets whose costs we cannot readily reduce.

Cybersecurity incidents and information technology failures could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.

We rely on information technology systems, some operated by third parties, to support engineering, manufacturing, supply chain management, financial reporting and communications. We hold sensitive technical data, including controlled defense information and customer intellectual property. As an aerospace and defense supplier, we are a target for sophisticated threat actors, including state-sponsored groups. We also face risks from ransomware, insider threats, and vulnerabilities in third-party software and supplier networks.

A successful attack or a significant system failure could interrupt production. It could also result in the loss or theft of proprietary or classified information and subject us to regulatory penalties or contractual liability. A security breach could also jeopardize our eligibility for certain government contracts, which require compliance with defined cybersecurity standards. The costs of maintaining and enhancing our security programs continue to increase.

Our international operations expose us to political, economic and currency risks.

We operate facilities in North America, Europe and the Asia-Pacific region and sell to customers in many countries. These activities expose us to several risks:

- Changes in foreign laws and tax regimes
- Political instability and armed conflict
- Expropriation
- Difficulties in staffing and managing foreign operations
- Varying labor practices
- Fluctuations in currency exchange rates

Much of our commercial aerospace revenue is denominated in U.S. dollars, while a portion of our costs at non-U.S. facilities is incurred in local currencies. Currency movements could therefore affect our margins. Geopolitical events could disrupt our operations or our customers' and suppliers' operations in affected regions.

Acquisitions and divestitures may not achieve their intended benefits, and we may be required to impair goodwill.

As part of our strategy, we evaluate acquisitions that broaden our capabilities or aftermarket reach. Acquisitions involve risks that could prevent us from realizing the anticipated benefits:

- Overpaying for acquired businesses
- Difficulty integrating operations, systems and personnel
- Undisclosed liabilities
- Loss of key customers or employees

Goodwill represents a meaningful portion of our total assets, and it increased during fiscal 2024. We test goodwill for impairment at least annually. If the operating performance or market outlook of a reporting unit deteriorates, we could be required to record non-cash impairment charges that reduce our earnings and stockholders' equity. Divestitures may also result in losses, ongoing indemnification obligations or stranded costs.

Risks Related to Financing and Capital Structure

Our liquidity depends on continued generation of operating cash flow, and our cash balance is modest relative to our obligations.

We operate with a relatively modest cash balance compared with the scale of our revenue and our short-term obligations. Our current liabilities exceed our current assets. We rely primarily on cash generated from operations, supplemented by access to credit facilities and capital markets, to fund working capital, capital expenditures, debt service, dividends and share repurchases.

Our operating cash flow can fluctuate significantly from period to period, for several reasons:

- The timing of customer payments, advances and milestone billings
- Inventory build-ups ahead of production rate increases
- Changes in customer payment terms

If our operating cash flow declines, or if our access to external financing is impaired, we may need to reduce investments, curtail capital returns or seek financing on unfavorable terms.

Our indebtedness and the terms of our financing arrangements could limit our flexibility.

We have a significant amount of long-term debt outstanding. Our debt agreements contain customary covenants that may restrict our ability to incur additional indebtedness, grant liens, complete certain transactions and make certain payments. A breach of these covenants could result in an event of default and acceleration of our obligations.

Our credit ratings affect our cost of borrowing and our access to capital. A downgrade could increase our interest expense and limit our ability to refinance maturing debt on acceptable terms. Rising interest rates could also increase the cost of any new or variable-rate borrowings.

Our substantial non-current liabilities, including employee benefit obligations, could require cash contributions and affect our results.

We carry significant non-current liabilities that include defined benefit pension and postretirement obligations, contract liabilities, environmental reserves and other long-term commitments. The funded status of our pension plans depends on discount rates, asset returns, mortality assumptions and regulatory funding requirements. Unfavorable changes in these factors could increase our required contributions, reduce our stockholders' equity and increase benefit expense. Satisfying these obligations would reduce the cash available for other purposes.

Our capital return program reduces our financial flexibility and may be changed or discontinued.

In fiscal 2024, we returned a substantial amount of capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases. Combined, these returns represented a large portion of our operating cash flow. Our capital return decisions reduce the cash and liquidity available to absorb unexpected costs, fund growth or weather an industry downturn. Our board of directors may change or suspend dividends or share repurchases at any time based on our financial condition, capital requirements, contractual restrictions and other factors. Any reduction could adversely affect the market price of our common stock.

Risks Related to Legal and Regulatory Matters

Our government contracting activities subject us to extensive regulation, audits and the risk of termination.

Contracts with the United States government and its prime contractors are subject to procurement regulations, cost accounting standards and other contract requirements. Government agencies routinely audit our costs, pricing, business systems and compliance. Audits could result in adjustments to contract prices, the withholding of payments, or the disallowance of costs.

Violations of applicable regulations could result in civil or criminal penalties, contract termination, or suspension or debarment from government contracting. Government customers may also terminate contracts for convenience. In that case, we would generally recover only costs incurred and a portion of the associated profit.

We must maintain aviation regulatory approvals to manufacture and repair our products.

Our manufacturing and repair operations are subject to oversight by the Federal Aviation Administration and by comparable aviation authorities in other countries. We must maintain production approvals, repair station certificates and other authorizations to conduct our business. Regulators may change requirements, increase inspections, or issue airworthiness directives that affect our products. The suspension or revocation of an approval, or a finding of noncompliance, could halt operations at an affected facility. It could also require costly corrective actions.

Export controls, sanctions, tariffs and trade restrictions could limit our sales and increase our costs.

Many of our products and much of our technical data are subject to U.S. export control regulations and to economic sanctions programs. These rules restrict the sale and transfer of controlled items and technology to certain countries, entities and individuals, and they require licenses for many transactions. Violations could result in significant fines, loss of export privileges and reputational damage.

Changes in trade policy could increase our costs or reduce demand for our products, including any of the following:

- New tariffs on imported materials and components
- Retaliatory measures against U.S. aerospace exports
- Restrictions on trade with particular countries

Environmental, health and safety laws could impose significant costs and liabilities.

Our manufacturing processes involve hazardous materials, including chemicals used in plating, coating, cleaning and composite fabrication. We are subject to environmental laws governing air emissions, wastewater discharges, waste handling and the remediation of contaminated sites. Some of these laws impose liability regardless of fault or the legality of the original conduct.

We are currently involved in remediation activities at certain current and former sites. Additional contamination could be discovered, and the regulations governing certain chemicals could become more stringent. Either development could require additional expenditures. Climate-related disclosure and emissions regulations could also increase our compliance costs.

Changes in tax laws or disputes with taxing authorities could adversely affect our results.

We are subject to income and other taxes in the United States and in numerous foreign jurisdictions. Our effective tax rate depends on the mix of earnings across jurisdictions, the availability of research credits and other incentives, and the application of complex tax rules. Changes in tax legislation could increase our tax expense and cash tax payments, including the implementation of global minimum tax rules or changes to the treatment of research expenditures. We are also subject to examinations by tax authorities, and their outcomes may differ from the amounts we have recorded.

Litigation and our inability to protect intellectual property could harm our business.

From time to time, we are party to legal proceedings involving commercial disputes, product liability, employment, environmental and other matters. The outcomes of these proceedings are inherently uncertain and could result in significant judgments, settlements or injunctions.

Our competitive position also depends on proprietary processes, designs and repair procedures. Many of these are protected as trade secrets rather than through patents. Unauthorized use or disclosure of our intellectual property, or claims that we infringe the rights of others, could weaken our competitive position. Such matters could also require costly litigation.

General Risks

Adverse economic, geopolitical and public health events could reduce demand and disrupt our operations.

Recessions, financial market instability, armed conflicts, terrorism, pandemics and other large-scale disruptions can reduce air travel, delay aircraft deliveries, and impair the financial condition of our customers and suppliers. These events may also interrupt transportation and logistics networks and restrict the movement of personnel. Their effects on the aerospace industry have historically been severe and, at times, prolonged.

Our results depend on estimates and judgments that may prove inaccurate.

The preparation of our financial statements requires significant estimates. These include estimates of total contract costs and revenues, inventory valuation, the recoverability of long-lived assets and goodwill, pension assumptions, warranty reserves, and income taxes. Actual outcomes may differ materially from these estimates. Revisions could have a material effect on our reported results in the period in which they occur.

We depend on key leadership and technical personnel.

Our success depends on the continued service of our senior management team and of individuals with specialized program, engineering and customer relationship expertise. The loss of key personnel, or our inability to plan effectively for leadership succession, could disrupt our operations and impair execution of our strategy.

The market price of our common stock may be volatile.

The trading price of our common stock may fluctuate significantly in response to several factors:

- Variations in our operating results
- Announcements by our customers regarding production rates or program changes
- Industry developments
- Changes in analysts' expectations
- Broader market conditions

Some of these factors are unrelated to our operating performance. Volatility in our stock price could also affect the cost and effectiveness of our share repurchase program and our equity-based compensation arrangements.
