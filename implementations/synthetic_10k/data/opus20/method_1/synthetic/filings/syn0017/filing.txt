PART I


Item 1. Business

Overview

Orris Aerospace, Inc. ("Orris," the "Company," "we," "us" or "our") designs, engineers and manufactures structural assemblies, propulsion components and integrated systems for commercial aircraft, business and regional jets, military platforms and space launch vehicles. We also provide maintenance, repair and overhaul services, spare parts and engineering support for the fleets that carry our products. Our products are found on most of the major commercial airframe families in service today. Our components are designed into many of the defense and space programs operated by the United States government and allied nations.

Orris was incorporated in the State of Delaware, and our corporate headquarters is located in Columbus, Ohio. We operate engineering centers, manufacturing plants and service facilities in North America, Europe and the Asia-Pacific region. For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, we reported total revenue of $10,928 million.

Our business model rests on long-term participation in aircraft and engine programs. When we are selected as a supplier on a new platform, we typically invest in design, qualification, tooling and production capacity well before the platform enters service. In return, we often become the sole or primary source for the content we supply over a production life that can extend for decades. After an aircraft enters service, it requires periodic inspection, repair and replacement of parts. This generates aftermarket demand that continues long after original equipment production has ended. We believe the combination of original equipment content and recurring aftermarket activity provides a durable revenue base and visibility into future demand.

Our Strategy

Our objective is to be the partner of choice for airframe and engine manufacturers, operators and government customers that require complex, flight-critical hardware delivered on schedule and to exacting quality standards. We pursue this objective through the following priorities:

- Operational excellence. We seek to improve safety, quality, delivery and cost performance across our factories through standardized production systems, lean manufacturing practices, automation and disciplined capital investment. Consistent execution supports our customers' production schedules and earns us opportunities on future programs.

- Content growth on new and existing platforms. We work to expand the value of our content on each aircraft by offering larger integrated assemblies, additional systems and design-and-build capability, rather than build-to-print components alone.

- Aftermarket expansion. We seek to capture a greater share of the service, repair and spare parts demand generated by our installed base. We do this by developing proprietary repair processes, obtaining regulatory approvals and building direct relationships with airline and military operators.

- Technology leadership. We invest in advanced materials, composite and metallic manufacturing processes, thermal management and digital engineering tools that we believe will be required on next-generation aircraft and propulsion systems, including platforms designed to improve fuel efficiency and reduce emissions.

- Balanced portfolio. We maintain a mix of commercial, defense and space business. Different demand cycles across these end markets help moderate the effect of a downturn in any single market on our overall results.

- Disciplined capital allocation. We prioritize investments in our existing operations and programs. We also evaluate targeted acquisitions that broaden our capabilities or aftermarket reach, and we return capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases.

Principal Products and Services

We supply a broad range of products and services that fall into the following general categories:

- Aerostructures. Fuselage sections, wing components, nacelles, thrust reverser structures, pylons, flight control surfaces, empennage assemblies and related subassemblies, produced in both metallic and composite materials.

- Propulsion components. Precision-machined and fabricated engine components, including fan and compressor cases, combustor and turbine hardware, structural castings, exhaust systems and rotating and static parts made from high-temperature nickel, titanium and specialty alloys.

- Engineered systems. Thermal management products, fluid conveyance assemblies, actuation components and environmental control subsystems for commercial and military aircraft.

- Space and missile hardware. Structures, propulsion hardware and thermal protection components for launch vehicles, satellites and missile programs.

- Aftermarket services. Component maintenance, repair and overhaul; spare parts; engineering and technical support; exchange and rotable pool programs; and long-term service agreements for commercial operators, lessors and military customers.

Reportable Segments

We manage our business through three reportable segments: Aerostructures, Propulsion Systems, and Aftermarket Services. Each segment has its own leadership team that is responsible for its operations, engineering and customer relationships. Segment results are reviewed regularly by our chief operating decision maker to assess performance and allocate resources. Financial information about our segments is presented in the Notes to the Consolidated Financial Statements included in Item 8 of this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

Aerostructures

The Aerostructures segment designs and manufactures large structural assemblies for commercial transports, business jets, regional aircraft and military platforms. Its principal products include fuselage sections, wing and empennage components, nacelle and thrust reverser assemblies, pylons and flight control surfaces. The segment holds positions on single-aisle and wide-body commercial aircraft programs as well as on rotorcraft and military transport platforms.

Aerostructures operates under long-term supply agreements with airframe manufacturers. These agreements generally set pricing for a defined number of units or a defined period and are tied to the customer's production rate. Revenue in this segment therefore tracks commercial aircraft build rates closely. The segment's competitive position depends on its ability to manage large, capital-intensive assembly operations, to maintain tight dimensional and quality tolerances, and to support production rate changes by its customers. The segment has made significant investments in automated drilling and fastening, composite layup and curing, and large-scale assembly tooling.

Propulsion Systems

The Propulsion Systems segment produces engineered components and subsystems for commercial and military gas turbine engines, auxiliary power units and space propulsion systems. Products include engine cases, combustor and turbine components, structural castings, exhaust hardware, thermal management products and fluid conveyance assemblies. Many of these parts operate at extreme temperatures and pressures and are classified as flight-critical. Their production therefore requires extensive process qualification and certification.

Engine manufacturers typically award propulsion content under life-of-program or multi-year agreements. Because certain components are consumed and replaced during engine shop visits, much of the segment's product portfolio also generates spare parts demand. The segment's capabilities include precision machining, investment casting, advanced welding and joining, specialty coatings and nondestructive inspection. Propulsion Systems also supplies hardware for military engine programs and for launch vehicle and in-space propulsion applications. This provides exposure to defense and space spending in addition to commercial air traffic.

Aftermarket Services

The Aftermarket Services segment supports the in-service fleet through component maintenance, repair and overhaul, the sale of spare parts, engineering and technical services, and long-term service agreements. Its customers include commercial airlines, aircraft lessors, cargo operators, business aviation operators, military services and other maintenance providers.

Demand in this segment is driven principally by aircraft utilization, flight hours and cycles, the size and age of the installed fleet, and the timing of scheduled maintenance events. The segment benefits from proprietary repair procedures, regulatory repair station certifications and original equipment design authority on a substantial portion of the parts it services. Aftermarket Services operates repair stations and distribution facilities located near major airline hubs and military installations so that it can offer short turnaround times to its customers. We believe this segment provides a recurring revenue stream that is less dependent on new aircraft production than our original equipment businesses.

Customers

Our customers include large commercial airframe manufacturers, commercial and military engine manufacturers, business jet and rotorcraft manufacturers, airlines and lessors, defense prime contractors, space launch providers, and agencies of the United States government and allied governments.

The commercial aerospace industry is highly concentrated. As a result, a relatively small number of airframe and engine manufacturers accounts for a significant portion of our revenue. The loss of, or a significant reduction in purchases by, any of these customers could have a material adverse effect on our business. Our relationships with our principal customers generally span many years and multiple programs. These relationships are typically governed by long-term agreements that define pricing, delivery and quality requirements and allocate responsibility for nonrecurring engineering and tooling costs.

Sales to the United States government are made either directly or as a subcontractor to defense prime contractors. These sales are subject to the procurement regulations and audit rights described below under "Government Regulation." Government contracts may be terminated at the government's convenience. Funding for multi-year programs is subject to annual congressional appropriations.

We sell to customers located in the United States and in many other countries. International sales are made primarily to airframe and engine manufacturers and airlines in Europe and the Asia-Pacific region, and to allied governments through direct commercial sales and foreign military sales arrangements.

Sales and Marketing

We market our products and services primarily through direct sales organizations that are aligned with our segments and with our largest customers. For our original equipment businesses, customer relationships are managed by dedicated program and account teams. These teams include business development, engineering, program management and contracts personnel who engage with customers throughout the life of a program, from early design studies and competitive proposals through production and sustainment.

New program awards are typically pursued through competitive requests for proposal issued by airframe and engine manufacturers or by government agencies. The selection process often begins years before a new aircraft or engine enters service. Award decisions generally weigh technical capability, past performance, quality and delivery record, price, and willingness to share development investment and risk.

Our Aftermarket Services segment markets directly to airlines, lessors, maintenance providers and military operators through regional sales teams, customer support representatives and digital ordering platforms. We also participate in industry trade shows, technical conferences and customer forums. In certain international markets, we use independent sales representatives and distributors. We remain responsible for product quality and regulatory compliance in those markets.

Competition

We operate in highly competitive markets. Competitors range from large diversified aerospace and defense companies with substantial financial, technical and manufacturing resources to smaller specialized suppliers that focus on particular components, processes or regions. In some cases, our customers also have in-house capabilities to produce the parts and assemblies we supply, and they may choose to make rather than buy.

Within aerostructures, we compete with independent aerostructures manufacturers and with the internal fabrication operations of airframe manufacturers. Within propulsion, we compete with specialized engine component suppliers, casting and forging houses and precision machining companies. In the aftermarket, we compete with original equipment manufacturers, independent maintenance providers, airline-affiliated repair shops, and suppliers of parts manufacturer approval parts and used serviceable material.

We believe the principal competitive factors in our markets include:

- product quality, safety and reliability;
- on-time delivery performance and the ability to support customer production rate changes;
- engineering and design capability, including expertise in advanced materials and processes;
- price and total cost of ownership;
- regulatory certifications and approvals; and
- financial strength and the capacity to invest in program development and production capacity.

We believe that our engineering depth, broad manufacturing capabilities, established positions on long-lived programs and extensive aftermarket network allow us to compete effectively. Qualification requirements and long product life cycles make it costly and time-consuming for customers to change suppliers once a program is in production. However, customers periodically re-compete existing work, and pricing pressure remains a persistent feature of our industry.

Raw Materials and Suppliers

Our operations require a variety of raw materials, including aluminum, titanium, nickel-based and cobalt-based superalloys, steel, carbon fiber, resins and specialty chemicals. We also purchase castings, forgings, fasteners, electronic components and other parts from third-party suppliers. Some of these materials and components are available from only a limited number of qualified sources, and some suppliers are located outside the United States.

We manage supply risk through long-term purchase agreements, dual sourcing where practical, safety stock for critical materials, and active supplier development and monitoring programs. Many of our long-term customer contracts include provisions that allow us to pass through a portion of raw material price changes. These provisions may not fully offset cost increases or may operate with a time lag. Disruptions in the supply of materials or components, shortages of qualified labor at our suppliers, or significant price increases could adversely affect our ability to meet customer delivery schedules and our results of operations.

Seasonality

Our business is not significantly seasonal. Revenue in our original equipment segments depends principally on customer production schedules, which are generally set well in advance and are not tied to the calendar. Quarterly results may vary, however, because of the timing of customer deliveries, planned plant shutdowns for maintenance and holidays (which typically occur during the summer and at year-end), and the timing of government contract awards and funding. Demand in our Aftermarket Services segment is influenced by airline flying patterns and maintenance planning cycles. Operators often schedule heavier maintenance outside peak travel periods. These factors can cause modest variations in quarterly aftermarket activity.

Backlog

Our backlog consists of firm orders from customers for products and services that have not yet been delivered or performed. Because many of our long-term agreements with airframe and engine manufacturers specify pricing and allocate content without firm delivery quantities, we recognize only firm purchase orders in backlog. Customers may adjust, defer or cancel orders in response to changes in their own production plans. For this reason, our backlog at any date is not necessarily indicative of future revenue. Information regarding our remaining performance obligations is included in the Notes to the Consolidated Financial Statements.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Our results depend heavily on the commercial aerospace industry, which is cyclical and sensitive to conditions we cannot control.

A large portion of our revenue comes from original equipment content and aftermarket services for commercial aircraft. Demand for new aircraft and for maintenance services depends on airline profitability, passenger and cargo traffic, fuel prices, the availability and cost of aircraft financing, fleet retirement decisions, and general economic conditions in the regions our customers serve. Historically, the industry has experienced sharp downturns triggered by recessions, terrorist attacks, public health emergencies, armed conflicts and fuel price shocks. These downturns have often been followed by order cancellations, deferred deliveries and reduced flight activity.

Because our cost structure includes a substantial base of fixed manufacturing and engineering costs, a decline in volume can reduce our margins more than proportionally. A prolonged downturn in commercial aerospace could materially reduce our revenue, profitability and operating cash flow. It could also require us to restructure operations or record impairments of long-lived assets.

Changes in the production rates of a small number of airframe and engine programs could significantly affect our revenue and profitability.

Our Aerostructures and Propulsion Systems segments supply content to a limited number of high-volume commercial programs. Revenue in these segments tracks our customers' build rates closely. Our customers may reduce, pause or delay planned rate increases because of their own supply chain constraints, quality or certification issues, regulatory actions, labor disputes or weaker airline demand. Production pauses or rate reductions on a major program can leave us carrying excess inventory, underutilized capacity and workforce costs that we cannot quickly adjust.

Conversely, rapid rate increases require us to add labor, tooling and supplier capacity ahead of revenue. If we are unable to execute these ramps efficiently, we may incur premium freight, overtime and expediting costs. We could also face contractual penalties or harm to customer relationships.

We depend on a concentrated group of customers, and the loss of or a significant reduction in business from any of them could materially harm us.

The commercial airframe and engine industries are dominated by a small number of manufacturers. As a result, a relatively small group of customers accounts for a significant share of our revenue. These customers have substantial negotiating leverage over pricing, payment terms, warranty provisions and the allocation of development costs. They may pursue vertical integration, dual sourcing or supplier consolidation strategies, any of which could reduce our share of future work.

A customer's financial difficulties, a dispute with a customer, or the loss of a position on a major program could materially reduce our revenue and earnings. Such events could also result in impairment of the related tooling, capitalized costs or other program assets.

Many of our long-term agreements are fixed-price, and cost overruns could cause losses.

We perform a substantial portion of our work under multi-year agreements that fix the price per unit for a defined quantity or period. Our profitability under these arrangements depends on our ability to estimate accurately, and then control, labor hours, material costs, supplier pricing, learning-curve improvements and overhead absorption over the life of the agreement. Several developments could cause our actual costs to exceed our estimates:

- Inflation in wages, energy and raw materials
- Engineering changes or technical difficulties
- Supplier failures
- Lower-than-expected production volumes

We recognize revenue on many of these contracts over time using estimates of total contract costs. When our estimates change, we may need to record cumulative adjustments that reduce previously recognized profit. When estimated costs exceed estimated revenue, we must record a forward loss provision for the full amount of the anticipated loss in the period it becomes evident. Such adjustments could be material to our results in any given period.

We face significant competition, and pricing pressure could reduce our margins.

We compete with large, diversified aerospace suppliers, specialized component manufacturers, low-cost producers in emerging markets, and the in-house capabilities of our customers. In the aftermarket, we also compete with independent repair providers and with suppliers of non-original equipment replacement parts approved by aviation regulators. These alternatives can be offered at lower prices than parts produced under original equipment design authority.

Competitors may develop superior technologies, achieve lower costs or offer more favorable commercial terms. Customers routinely seek annual price reductions and greater supplier participation in development costs. If we cannot maintain our technical differentiation, cost competitiveness and delivery performance, we may lose program awards and aftermarket share or be forced to accept lower prices.

Demand for our aftermarket services depends on fleet utilization and maintenance practices that may change.

Our Aftermarket Services segment, and the spare parts portion of our Propulsion Systems segment, depend on aircraft flight hours, cycles and the timing of scheduled maintenance events. Reduced flight activity, early retirement of older aircraft that carry our content, or the deferral of shop visits by airlines seeking to conserve cash would reduce demand for our services and parts. The increasing use of used serviceable material from retired aircraft could also reduce that demand.

Operators and lessors may also shift maintenance work to engine and airframe manufacturers under long-term service agreements that we do not participate in. In addition, newer aircraft and engine designs may require less frequent maintenance or fewer replacement parts. Any of these trends could reduce the recurring revenue that we expect our installed base to generate.

Our defense and space business depends on government spending priorities that can change.

A meaningful portion of our revenue comes from military and space programs funded by the United States government and allied governments, either directly or through prime contractors. Government budgets are subject to political negotiation, competing priorities, continuing resolutions, potential shutdowns and statutory spending limits. Specific programs on which we have content may be reduced, delayed or cancelled. Funding may also shift toward platforms or technologies in which we do not participate.

The space launch market includes new entrants and programs with uncertain technical and financial prospects. The failure or delay of a launch program on which we supply hardware could reduce our revenue. It could also leave us with unrecovered investment.

Our investments in new programs and technologies may not generate adequate returns.

We invest significant amounts in research and development, design, qualification, tooling and production capacity, often years before a new platform enters service. Our recovery of these investments depends on several factors outside our control:

- The platform's commercial success and production volumes over its life
- The timing of certification and entry into service
- Our continued status as a supplier on the program

Development programs frequently encounter technical challenges, schedule delays and changes in customer requirements. These can increase our costs and defer revenue. If a program is cancelled, achieves lower volumes than expected, or experiences prolonged delays, we may not recover our investment and may be required to record impairments or losses.

The aerospace industry's transition toward lower-emission aircraft and propulsion could change demand for our products.

Airframe and engine manufacturers, airlines and regulators are pursuing reductions in aviation emissions. They are doing so through more efficient engines, new airframe configurations, sustainable aviation fuels, and hybrid-electric and hydrogen propulsion concepts. These developments may require materials, manufacturing processes and system architectures that differ from those in which we have historically specialized. They may also alter the mix and volume of components on future aircraft.

The pace and direction of this transition are uncertain. If we fail to anticipate technological change, or invest in technologies that do not achieve market acceptance, our competitive position on next-generation platforms could weaken. In addition, regulations or taxes intended to curb aviation emissions could reduce air travel demand and, in turn, demand for our products and services.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Disruptions in our supply chain could prevent us from meeting customer commitments.

We rely on suppliers for raw materials, forgings, castings, fasteners, electronic components and specialized processing services. Many of these suppliers are sole or limited sources that have been qualified to stringent aerospace standards. Qualifying replacements can take months or years. The aerospace supply base has experienced capacity constraints, labor shortages and financial stress in recent years, and certain suppliers have struggled to meet delivery schedules.

A supplier's failure to deliver conforming material on time could interrupt our production and delay deliveries to our customers. Such a failure could also cause us to incur additional costs or contractual penalties. A significant supplier disruption could have a material adverse effect on our results of operations and customer relationships.

Increases in the cost or reduced availability of specialty metals and other materials could adversely affect our margins.

Our products use significant quantities of titanium, nickel-based superalloys, aluminum, specialty steels, carbon fiber and resins. The markets for several of these materials are concentrated among a small number of producers. Some materials are sourced from regions affected by geopolitical tension, sanctions or export restrictions. Prices and availability can change rapidly.

Our long-term customer agreements do not always include escalation clauses that fully pass through material cost increases. Where such clauses exist, they may operate with a lag. Rising material costs, or the need to secure alternative sources at higher prices, could reduce our gross margin and operating cash flow. Because inventories represent a significant portion of our current assets, we also face the risk of obsolescence or valuation adjustments if program requirements or demand change.

Inflation and higher operating costs could reduce our profitability.

We have experienced inflationary pressure on wages, benefits, energy, freight, purchased services and materials. In addition, our selling, general and administrative expenses grew faster than revenue during fiscal 2024. Our ability to offset these increases through productivity improvements, pricing actions or contract escalation provisions is limited by the fixed-price nature of many of our agreements and by our customers' bargaining power. Persistent inflation could compress our margins and reduce the cash available to invest in our business or return to stockholders.

We may be unable to attract, train and retain the skilled workforce our operations require.

Our operations depend on approximately 27,000 employees, including engineers, certified machinists, composite technicians, inspectors and licensed maintenance personnel. Competition for these skills is intense throughout the aerospace and defense industry, and the time needed to train new employees to required proficiency is substantial. High turnover or difficulty in hiring could reduce our productivity, increase our labor costs and impair our ability to support customer rate increases.

A portion of our workforce is represented by labor unions or works councils. Work stoppages or strikes at our facilities, at our customers or at our suppliers could disrupt production. So could difficult collective bargaining negotiations. In addition, certain programs require employees to hold security clearances, which further limits the available labor pool.

Product defects, quality escapes or aviation accidents could result in significant costs and reputational harm.

Many of our products are flight-critical, and a failure could contribute to an aircraft accident involving loss of life. A defect or nonconformance in our products or repairs could lead to recalls, retrofit campaigns, warranty claims and customer charge-backs. It could also lead to the grounding of aircraft, regulatory investigations or product liability litigation. Regulators and customers have heightened their scrutiny of manufacturing quality across the aerospace supply chain. Quality findings at our facilities could result in production holds or the loss of certifications.

Our insurance may not cover all potential liabilities, and coverage may become more expensive or unavailable. A significant quality failure could materially affect our financial results. It could also damage our reputation with customers and regulators in ways that impair future program awards.

Our capital-intensive operations expose us to risks of facility disruption and asset impairment.

Property, plant and equipment represents the largest component of our total assets, reflecting the specialized machinery, autoclaves, tooling and assembly facilities our products require. Many of our production processes are concentrated at single sites, and qualified alternative capacity may not be readily available. Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Our business depends on information systems, networks and data. We rely on them to design and qualify flight-critical hardware, run our factories and repair stations, coordinate with suppliers, and support customers across the commercial, defense and space markets. Much of the information we create or receive is proprietary to us or our customers. Some of it is export-controlled technical data, and some is controlled unclassified information that defense prime contractors and government agencies require us to protect. Weakness in these systems could disrupt production, delay deliveries, expose sensitive data, impair our eligibility for government work and damage customer trust. We therefore treat cybersecurity as an enterprise risk rather than solely a technology matter.

Cybersecurity Risk Management and Strategy

We maintain a cybersecurity program intended to identify, assess, prioritize and mitigate risks from cybersecurity threats to our corporate information technology environment and to the operational technology that controls our manufacturing and test equipment. The program is integrated into our enterprise risk management process. Cybersecurity risks are evaluated alongside operational, financial, regulatory and strategic risks, and our information security, enterprise risk, legal, compliance and internal audit functions coordinate in reviewing them.

The program is aligned with recognized industry frameworks and standards, including the National Institute of Standards and Technology Cybersecurity Framework. It also reflects the cybersecurity requirements that apply to contractors and subcontractors handling United States government information. Its principal components include:

- Asset management and network segmentation designed to separate engineering, business and factory-floor systems and limit the spread of malicious activity.
- Identity and access controls, including multifactor authentication and privileged access management for systems that hold sensitive or export-controlled data.
- Continuous security monitoring, threat detection and vulnerability management, supported by a security operations function and external threat intelligence sources.
- Data protection measures, including encryption and data loss prevention tools.
- Secure configuration, patching and change management practices for both information technology and operational technology assets.

We provide cybersecurity awareness training to employees and to contractors with access to our systems. Training is tailored to job function, with additional instruction for engineers, program personnel and others who handle controlled information. We supplement this training with simulated phishing campaigns and periodic communications on emerging threats.

Third-Party Risk Management

Our operations depend on an extensive network of suppliers, technology vendors, cloud service providers and customers with whom we exchange data electronically. We maintain processes to evaluate the cybersecurity practices of third parties before engagement and during the relationship. Third parties that access our networks or handle sensitive information receive heightened scrutiny. These processes include security questionnaires, review of independent attestations where available, and contractual provisions addressing data protection, incident notification and, where applicable, flow-down of government cybersecurity requirements. Because many of our suppliers are smaller specialized manufacturers, we also share guidance and expectations with our supply base to strengthen cybersecurity practices across it. We periodically engage independent assessors, consultants and auditors to evaluate elements of our program, test our defenses and recommend improvements. Our internal audit function also reviews selected information security controls.

Incident Response

We maintain a documented cybersecurity incident response plan that defines roles, escalation thresholds and procedures for detection, containment, eradication, recovery and post-incident review. The plan addresses incidents affecting manufacturing operations and covers coordination with customers, government agencies and law enforcement. It provides for cross-functional participation by information security, legal, communications, operations and finance personnel, and it includes a process for assessing the materiality of an incident and evaluating disclosure obligations. We conduct tabletop exercises involving management and technical teams to test the plan, and we incorporate lessons learned into our procedures. We also maintain business continuity and disaster recovery plans for critical systems.

Assessment of Risks from Cybersecurity Threats

Like other aerospace and defense companies, we are targeted by a range of threat actors, including criminal groups seeking financial gain through ransomware or fraud and sophisticated state-sponsored actors seeking access to defense and advanced technology information. We and our third-party providers have experienced cybersecurity incidents in the past. To date, we have not identified any risks from cybersecurity threats, including as a result of any previous cybersecurity incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect us, including our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. However, no security program can eliminate all risk. A future incident could have a material adverse effect on our business. For further discussion, see "Risk Factors" in this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

Cybersecurity Governance

Board Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees risks from cybersecurity threats as part of its broader oversight of enterprise risk. The Board has delegated primary oversight responsibility to its Audit Committee. The committee's responsibilities include reviewing our cybersecurity and data protection program, the policies and controls used to manage cybersecurity risk, compliance with applicable government cybersecurity requirements, and our incident response and recovery readiness. The Audit Committee receives regular reports from our Chief Information Security Officer ("CISO") and other members of management. These reports cover the threat environment, the results of internal and external assessments, key risk indicators, significant incidents and the status of initiatives to strengthen our defenses. The Chair of the Audit Committee reports on these matters to the full Board, and the full Board receives periodic cybersecurity briefings directly from management.

Management's Role

Our CISO has day-to-day responsibility for assessing and managing material risks from cybersecurity threats and leads our information security organization. The CISO reports to our Chief Information Officer, who reports to our Chief Executive Officer. The CISO also has direct access to the Audit Committee. Our CISO has extensive experience leading information security programs at aerospace and defense manufacturers, including programs subject to government cybersecurity requirements, and holds industry-recognized information security certifications. Members of the information security leadership team have backgrounds in security operations, operational technology security, risk management and compliance.

A cross-functional cybersecurity risk council, chaired by the CISO, includes senior leaders from our segments and from legal, compliance, finance and internal audit. The council reviews risk assessments, sets remediation priorities and monitors progress. Our executive leadership team reviews cybersecurity risk as part of its regular risk discussions. Under our incident response plan, significant incidents are escalated promptly to senior management and, as appropriate, to the Audit Committee and the full Board, along with an evaluation of external reporting and disclosure requirements.

Orris designs, engineers and manufactures structural assemblies, propulsion components and integrated systems for commercial aircraft, business and regional jets, military platforms and space launch vehicles. We also provide maintenance, repair and overhaul services, spare parts and engineering support for the fleets that carry our products. We manage our business through three reportable segments:

- Aerostructures, which designs and manufactures large structural assemblies, including fuselage sections, wing and empennage components, nacelle and thrust reverser assemblies, pylons and flight control surfaces.

- Propulsion Systems, which produces engineered components and subsystems for commercial and military gas turbine engines, auxiliary power units and space propulsion systems.

- Aftermarket Services, which supports the in-service fleet through component maintenance, repair and overhaul, spare parts sales, engineering and technical services, and long-term service agreements.

We are incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Columbus, Ohio. As of December 31, 2024, we employed about 27,000 people at engineering centers, manufacturing plants and service facilities in North America, Europe and the Asia-Pacific region.

Our revenue is generated primarily under long-term agreements with airframe and engine manufacturers, defense prime contractors, government agencies, airlines and lessors. Original equipment revenue in our Aerostructures and Propulsion Systems segments is closely tied to our customers' production rates on the platforms where we hold content. Aftermarket revenue, whether in our Aftermarket Services segment or in the spare parts portion of Propulsion Systems, is driven principally by aircraft utilization, flight hours and cycles, fleet size and age, and the timing of scheduled maintenance events. Our defense and space business depends on government budget priorities and program funding.

Business Environment in Fiscal 2024

Commercial air travel demand remained healthy throughout fiscal 2024. Airline customers continued to operate their fleets at high utilization levels, and many operators extended the service lives of older aircraft while new aircraft deliveries remained below the levels originally planned by the airframe manufacturers. These conditions supported demand for spare parts and repair services across our installed base.

Original equipment production across the industry continued to recover, although not uniformly. Supply chain constraints, labor availability and quality and certification matters affected the pace at which certain of our customers were able to raise production rates on major programs. As a result, our original equipment businesses operated under production schedules that changed during the year. In some cases we carried higher inventory to protect customer deliveries and to prepare for anticipated rate increases.

Defense demand was stable to improving, supported by sustained procurement and sustainment activity on military aircraft and engine programs. Activity in launch vehicle and missile programs also continued. Inflation in wages, energy and certain raw materials moderated compared with the prior year but remained elevated relative to historical levels. This continued to pressure costs on fixed-price agreements, which we worked to offset through productivity initiatives, supplier negotiations and contractual pricing provisions where available.

Revenue increased 4.1% to $10,928 million in fiscal 2024 from $10,499 million in fiscal 2023. All three segments contributed to the increase. Growth was strongest in our aftermarket activities and more moderate in original equipment.

- Improved absorption of fixed manufacturing costs at higher volumes in our Propulsion Systems facilities.

Critical Accounting Estimates

Our Consolidated Financial Statements are prepared in accordance with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. Preparing them requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses, and the related disclosures. We base our estimates on historical experience, current contract terms, customer production forecasts and other assumptions we believe to be reasonable under the circumstances. Actual results may differ from these estimates.

We consider the following accounting estimates to be critical. Each involves a significant level of estimation uncertainty and has had, or is reasonably likely to have, a material effect on our financial condition or results of operations. Our significant accounting policies are described in the Notes to the Consolidated Financial Statements.

Revenue Recognition on Long-Term Contracts

A substantial portion of our revenue is recognized over time as we perform under long-term agreements with airframe and engine manufacturers and government customers. For these contracts, we generally measure progress toward completion using the ratio of costs incurred to date to estimated total costs at completion. Under this method, revenue and profit are recognized as costs are incurred, based on our estimates of total contract revenue and total contract cost.

Estimating total contract costs requires significant judgment. Our estimates include assumptions about the following:

- Labor hours and labor rates.

- Material and supplier pricing.

- Learning-curve improvements.

- Overhead absorption.

- Production volumes.

- The cost of engineering changes and technical requirements.

Estimating total contract revenue also requires judgment. Examples include the amounts expected from contract modifications, claims, price escalation provisions and variable consideration such as incentive fees or penalties. Variable consideration is included in the transaction price only to the extent that a significant reversal of cumulative revenue is not probable.

We review our contract estimates regularly. Changes in estimates are recognized on a cumulative catch-up basis in the period in which the change is identified. When estimated total costs on a contract are expected to exceed estimated total revenue, we record a provision for the entire anticipated loss in the period in which the loss becomes evident.

Because many of our contracts are fixed-price and extend over multiple years, changes in customer production rates, supplier performance, inflation or productivity can cause significant revisions to our estimates. Such revisions could materially affect our results in the period in which they are recorded. They are a principal source of the risks described in Item 1A under the risk factor relating to fixed-price long-term agreements.

Goodwill

Goodwill was $1,183 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment annually at the reporting unit level, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. When we perform a quantitative test, we estimate the fair value of each reporting unit using a combination of income and market approaches.

The income approach relies on discounted projected cash flows. The most significant assumptions are revenue growth rates, which depend on customer production rates and aftermarket demand, projected operating margins, terminal growth rates and the discount rate applied to future cash flows. The market approach relies on valuation multiples of comparable companies. Changes in these assumptions, or a significant downturn in the commercial aerospace market, could result in an impairment charge. Based on our most recent annual test, the estimated fair value of each reporting unit exceeded its carrying value, and no impairment was recorded.

Long-Lived Assets and Program-Related Assets

We hold substantial investments in property, plant and equipment, specialized tooling and other program-related assets, many of which are dedicated to specific aircraft or engine programs. We evaluate these assets for impairment whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that their carrying amounts may not be recoverable. Examples of such events include a program cancellation, a significant reduction in expected production volumes or the loss of a supplier position.

Recoverability is assessed by comparing the carrying amount of an asset group with the undiscounted cash flows we expect it to generate. If the carrying amount is not recoverable, we recognize an impairment equal to the amount by which the carrying amount exceeds fair value. These assessments require estimates of program volumes, pricing, costs and remaining useful lives, which are inherently uncertain over long program horizons. We also estimate useful lives for depreciation and amortization purposes. Changes in those estimates could affect future depreciation and amortization expense.

Inventories

Inventories were $2,194 million at December 31, 2024. Inventories are stated at the lower of cost or net realizable value. We record adjustments for excess, obsolete and slow-moving inventory based on estimates of future demand, which consider customer production forecasts, the expected remaining life of the programs supported, aftermarket usage trends and the potential for alternative uses. Changes in customer production schedules, program cancellations or shifts in aftermarket demand could require additional write-downs.

Income Taxes

We operate in many tax jurisdictions and are subject to income taxes in the United States and in other countries. Determining our provision for income taxes requires significant judgment. This includes evaluating uncertain tax positions, assessing the realizability of deferred tax assets and estimating research and development tax credits.

We recognize the benefit of an uncertain tax position only when it is more likely than not that the position will be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. We record valuation allowances to reduce deferred tax assets to the amount we believe is more likely than not to be realized, considering projected future taxable income and available tax planning strategies. Tax audits, changes in tax laws and differences between our estimates and final tax outcomes could affect our effective tax rate and results of operations in the period in which they occur.

Contingencies

We are subject to various legal proceedings, government investigations, contract disputes, warranty claims and environmental matters arising in the ordinary course of business. We record a liability when a loss is probable and the amount can be reasonably estimated. These assessments require judgment about the likely outcome of each matter and the range of possible losses. They are based on the advice of legal counsel, the status of negotiations and our historical experience. The ultimate resolution of these matters could differ from our estimates.
