PART I

Item 1. Business

Overview

Kalondra Machinery, Inc. is a Delaware corporation with its principal executive offices in San Jose, California. In this Annual Report on Form 10-K, "Kalondra," "the Company," "we," "us" and "our" refer to Kalondra Machinery, Inc. and its consolidated subsidiaries unless the context indicates otherwise. Our common stock trades under the ticker symbol KOLR.

Kalondra designs, engineers, manufactures, sells and supports heavy machinery, power generation and energy equipment, and industrial automation systems. Our products are used to build and maintain infrastructure, extract and process natural resources, generate and distribute electric power, and operate automated manufacturing and logistics facilities. Our customers include construction contractors, mining and quarrying operators, utilities, energy producers, governmental bodies, original equipment manufacturers and industrial companies of many sizes. We sell through a combination of independent dealers, distributors and direct sales channels, and we support the installed base of our equipment through replacement parts, service, remanufacturing, digital monitoring and related offerings throughout each product's operating life.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, our consolidated revenue was $82,769 million, an increase of 6.7% compared with fiscal 2023. We believe that the breadth of our product portfolio, the durability and productivity of our equipment, the reach of our distribution network and our ongoing investment in engineering and technology are the foundation of our competitive position.

Our Strategy

Our strategy focuses on durable, profitable growth across economic cycles. Its principal elements are:

  • Product leadership. We design equipment that lowers total cost of ownership for our customers through fuel efficiency, uptime, operator productivity and serviceability. We continue to invest in alternative powertrains, electrification, autonomy and connectivity across our product lines.
  • Services and aftermarket growth. Our installed base of machines, engines and systems creates recurring demand for parts, service, overhauls, remanufactured components and digital subscriptions. Expanding these offerings is a priority because they deepen customer relationships and tend to be less cyclical than new equipment sales.
  • Operational excellence. We pursue continuous improvement in manufacturing, procurement and logistics through lean production practices, supplier development, and disciplined management of inventory and capital investment.
  • Disciplined capital allocation. We aim to fund organic investment in our businesses, maintain a strong balance sheet, pursue selective acquisitions that complement our capabilities, and return capital to stockholders through dividends and share repurchases.

Principal Products and Services

Our product and service offerings fall into the following broad categories.

Construction and earthmoving equipment. We manufacture a broad line of machines used in road building, residential and nonresidential construction, utility work and site preparation. The line includes excavators, wheel loaders, track-type tractors, motor graders, backhoe loaders, compact track and skid steer loaders, articulated trucks and compaction equipment. Many models offer integrated grade control, payload measurement and telematics capabilities that help customers improve precision and reduce rework.

Mining and quarrying equipment. We produce large-scale equipment for surface and underground mining and aggregates production, including large wheel loaders, hydraulic mining shovels, off-highway haul trucks, large dozers, underground loaders and drilling equipment. We also offer fleet management software, autonomous haulage technology and site optimization services that help mine operators improve safety and productivity.

Engines, power generation and energy systems. We design and manufacture reciprocating engines, gas turbines, generator sets, compression packages and related controls. These products serve standby and prime power applications for data centers, hospitals, commercial buildings and industrial facilities, as well as oil and gas production, natural gas transmission, marine propulsion, rail and other industrial uses. We also offer microgrid solutions, battery energy storage integration and hybrid power systems.

Industrial automation and precision systems. We supply robotic material handling systems, automated guided vehicles, motion control components, industrial drives, sensors and integrated control software for manufacturers, distribution centers and process industries. Our engineering teams also provide system design, integration and commissioning services for complex automation projects.

Aftermarket parts and services. Across all of our businesses, we and our dealers sell replacement and wear parts, perform scheduled and unscheduled maintenance, rebuild major components and provide extended service agreements. Our remanufacturing operations restore used engines, transmissions, hydraulic components and other assemblies to like-new specifications. These operations offer customers lower-cost alternatives while reducing raw material use and waste.

Digital and connected solutions. Most of our new machines and power systems are equipped with telematics hardware that transmits operating data to our cloud-based platforms. These platforms give customers and dealers equipment health monitoring, predictive maintenance alerts, fuel and utilization analytics, and remote diagnostics. We offer them through standard packages and paid subscriptions.

Financing support. Through arrangements with our dealers and third-party financial institutions, we help customers obtain retail financing, leasing and rental options for our equipment. These programs are designed to support equipment sales and are not a separate reportable segment.

Reportable Segments

We manage our operations through three reportable segments: Construction and Resource Equipment, Power and Energy Systems, and Industrial Automation. Our chief operating decision maker evaluates segment performance and allocates resources based on segment profit, as described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements included in Item 8 of this report. Certain corporate costs, centrally managed research activities and other items are not allocated to the segments.

Construction and Resource Equipment

The Construction and Resource Equipment segment designs, manufactures and markets machines for construction, infrastructure, forestry, mining, quarrying and aggregates applications, together with related parts, services and technology offerings. Customers range from small owner-operated contractors and rental companies to large multinational construction firms and global mining companies. The segment sells mainly through our independent dealer network, which provides sales, rental, financing coordination, parts distribution and field service in its territories. Demand for the segment's products is influenced by public infrastructure spending, residential and nonresidential construction activity, commodity prices, mining capital expenditure programs, interest rates and the age and utilization of the equipment fleets in our markets. The segment's growing autonomy and fleet management offerings reflect customer demand for safer, more productive operations at remote and large-scale sites.

Power and Energy Systems

The Power and Energy Systems segment designs, manufactures and supports engines, turbines, generator sets, compression equipment, energy storage integration and associated controls for power generation, oil and gas, marine, rail and industrial customers. The segment also supplies engines and power modules to other equipment manufacturers for use in their products. It reaches customers through our dealer network, specialized distributors and direct sales teams for large and engineered-to-order projects. Demand is driven by investment in data center and commercial construction, the need for reliable backup and distributed power, natural gas production and transmission activity, fleet replacement cycles in marine and rail markets, and the transition toward lower-emission and hybrid energy solutions. Engineered projects in this segment can have long lead times and may involve multi-year delivery and service obligations.

Industrial Automation

The Industrial Automation segment develops and sells robotic systems, automated material handling equipment, motion control and drive components, sensing technologies and industrial software, together with integration and lifecycle services. Customers include manufacturers in the automotive, electronics, consumer goods, food and beverage, and pharmaceutical industries, as well as operators of warehouses and distribution centers. The segment sells through direct sales teams, systems integrators and distributors. Demand reflects manufacturing capital spending, labor availability and cost, the expansion of electronic commerce and logistics networks, and the adoption of advanced manufacturing technologies. The segment benefits from our engineering presence in the San Jose area and our ability to apply software, controls and data analytics capabilities developed across the Company.

Customers

Our customer base is broad and diverse by industry, size and geography. It includes construction contractors, equipment rental companies, mining and aggregates producers, oil and gas operators, pipeline companies, electric utilities, data center developers and operators, governmental agencies, shipbuilders and vessel operators, railroads, original equipment manufacturers, manufacturers across many industries, and logistics and distribution companies. No single customer accounted for a material portion of our consolidated revenue in fiscal 2024. Because a substantial portion of our sales flows through independent dealers and distributors, the end users of our products are more numerous than our direct customer relationships suggest.

We sell our products and services in North America, Latin America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Asia-Pacific region. Our international operations expose us to foreign currency fluctuations, trade policies, tariffs, export controls, and political and economic conditions in the countries where we operate. These risks are discussed further in Item 1A, Risk Factors.

Order Backlog

Many of our products, particularly large mining equipment, engineered power systems and complex automation projects, are built to customer specifications and ordered well in advance of delivery. We monitor order backlog as one indicator of near-term demand. Backlog can vary significantly from period to period depending on order timing, dealer inventory decisions, delivery schedules and customer project timelines, and orders may be subject to cancellation or deferral. For these reasons, backlog may not reliably indicate revenue for any future period.

Sales and Marketing

Independent dealers are central to our go-to-market model. Our dealers are independently owned businesses that generally operate under agreements granting them the right to sell and service Kalondra products within defined territories. Dealers invest in facilities, parts inventories, service technicians, rental fleets and digital capabilities to support customers in their territories. We work closely with dealers on sales planning, product training, marketing programs, service standards and technology deployment, and we measure dealer performance against agreed objectives. Many of our dealer relationships span multiple generations of ownership, and we consider the strength of the network to be one of our most important competitive advantages.

We complement the dealer network with direct sales organizations that serve large global accounts, governmental customers, original equipment manufacturers and customers buying engineered-to-order systems. In our Industrial Automation segment, we also sell through systems integrators and specialized distributors that combine our products with other components to deliver complete solutions.

Our marketing activities include trade shows, customer demonstration centers, digital marketing, application engineering support, and customer and dealer training programs. We increasingly use data from connected equipment to identify service opportunities, recommend parts replacement before failure and tailor product offerings to how customers actually use our machines. We also offer customer loyalty and parts programs through our dealers and e-commerce platforms, which allow customers to identify, order and track parts and service online.

Manufacturing, Supply Chain and Raw Materials

We operate manufacturing, assembly, remanufacturing and distribution facilities in the United States and internationally, and we supplement our own production with components sourced from a global supplier base. Our principal raw materials and purchased components include steel, iron castings and forgings, aluminum, copper, rubber, hydraulic components, electronic components, semiconductors, batteries, tires and engine components. We generally purchase these materials and components from multiple suppliers, although certain specialized components come from a limited number of suppliers or a single source.

Supply disruptions, logistics constraints, labor shortages at suppliers and price volatility for commodities have affected our industry in recent years and may continue to do so. We seek to reduce these risks through long-term supply agreements, dual sourcing where practicable, supplier development and quality programs, regional sourcing strategies, and inventory planning. We use pricing actions, cost reduction initiatives and, in certain cases, contractual price adjustment mechanisms to help offset changes in material and component costs.

Research and Development

We invest continuously in research, development and engineering to improve existing products and to develop new products, technologies and services. Our engineering teams operate in technical centers in the United States and other regions, including a significant presence in the San Jose area focused on software, controls, autonomy, sensing and data analytics. Current development priorities include electrified and hybrid powertrains, alternative fuel capability for engines, battery and energy storage integration, autonomous and semi-autonomous machine operation, operator assistance and safety systems, connected equipment platforms, and advanced robotics and motion control. Research and development expense represented 2.4% of revenue in fiscal 2024, in line with fiscal 2023. We also collaborate with universities, research institutions, suppliers and customers on selected development programs.

Competition

We operate in highly competitive markets. Our competitors include large multinational equipment manufacturers with broad product lines and global distribution networks, regional manufacturers with strong positions in particular geographies or product categories, specialized producers of engines, turbines and power systems, and automation and robotics companies ranging from diversified industrial conglomerates to technology-focused firms. In certain markets, particularly in Asia, we face growing competition from manufacturers that offer lower-priced equipment and are expanding internationally. In aftermarket parts, we also compete with independent suppliers of non-genuine replacement parts and independent service providers.

The principal competitive factors in our markets include product performance, quality, reliability and durability; total cost of ownership; price; availability and lead times; breadth of product offering; strength and responsiveness of dealer and service support; availability of financing; technology and digital capabilities; emissions performance and fuel efficiency; and brand reputation. We believe we compete effectively on the basis of our broad product range, the productivity and reliability of our equipment, the reach and capability of our dealer network, our aftermarket and remanufacturing capabilities, and our investment in technology. However, our competitive position varies by product line and region, and competitors may introduce new technologies, pursue aggressive pricing, or consolidate in ways that affect our market share and margins.

Seasonality and Cyclicality

Demand for several of our products follows seasonal patterns. Sales of construction equipment and related parts and services are generally stronger in the second and third quarters, when weather in many of our principal markets favors construction activity, and weaker in the winter months. Dealers in our construction markets often build inventory ahead of the spring selling season and adjust inventory levels toward the end of the year, which can affect the timing of our sales to dealers relative to retail demand. Sales in our Power and Energy Systems and Industrial Automation segments are less seasonal but can fluctuate significantly depending on the timing of large project shipments and customer capital spending decisions.

More broadly, our businesses are cyclical and sensitive to general economic conditions, including gross domestic product growth, construction activity, infrastructure investment, commodity prices, energy markets, manufacturing activity, interest rates and credit availability. Changes in these conditions can significantly affect demand for our products and services.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

Demand for our products is cyclical, and a downturn in the end markets we serve could significantly reduce our revenue and profitability.
Purchases of construction and mining machinery, engines, power generation equipment and automation systems are capital investments. Customers often defer them when economic conditions weaken, credit tightens or their own revenue declines. Our sales depend on residential and nonresidential construction activity, public infrastructure investment, mining and quarrying capital programs, energy production and transmission, data center and commercial construction, and manufacturing and logistics capital spending. Each of these is sensitive to interest rates, business confidence, commodity prices and overall economic growth.

During a downturn, customers and dealers may postpone new equipment purchases, extend the operating lives of existing fleets, reduce rental fleet investment and draw down existing inventories before placing new orders. These responses can amplify the decline in our shipments relative to the decline in end-user activity. Because a meaningful portion of our cost base consists of manufacturing capacity, engineering resources and selling and administrative functions that cannot be reduced quickly, lower volumes would likely reduce our gross and operating margins more than proportionately. Our recent revenue and operating income growth should not be taken as an indication of future results.

Volatility in commodity prices could reduce demand from our mining, quarrying and energy customers.
A significant portion of the demand for our large mining equipment, off-highway trucks, compression packages and engines comes from customers whose investment decisions depend on the prices of metals, minerals, coal, aggregates, crude oil and natural gas. When commodity prices fall or are expected to remain depressed, mining companies and energy producers typically reduce exploration, delay or cancel new projects, idle equipment and cut maintenance budgets. Those actions reduce demand for both new equipment and aftermarket parts and services. Commodity price movements can be abrupt and are driven by global supply and demand, geopolitical developments, currency movements and investor sentiment, none of which we control. Over the longer term, shifts in energy policy and in the global energy mix could also structurally reduce demand for equipment used in the extraction and transport of certain fossil fuels.

Changes in government infrastructure spending and public policy could adversely affect demand for our construction equipment.
Governmental bodies at the federal, state, local and foreign levels fund a substantial share of road, bridge, water, transit and utility projects, and therefore drive a substantial share of demand in our Construction and Resource Equipment segment. Infrastructure funding is subject to legislative appropriation, budget constraints, political priorities and administrative delays. Funds authorized under multi-year programs may be disbursed more slowly than expected, redirected to other purposes or reduced in future budget cycles. Delays in project approvals, environmental permitting or procurement can also defer equipment purchases by the contractors who perform public works. A reduction in public infrastructure investment, or a slower pace of spending than our customers and dealers anticipate, could reduce our sales and lead to excess inventory in our distribution channel.

Growth in our Power and Energy Systems segment depends in part on continued investment in data centers and distributed power, which may not be sustained.
In recent periods, demand for our generator sets, gas turbines, energy storage integration and related controls has benefited from the construction of data centers and from customers' increased focus on reliable backup and prime power. This demand is concentrated among a relatively small number of large developers and operators, and their investment plans can change quickly because of technological developments, financing conditions, power grid availability, permitting constraints or shifts in their own business strategies. If data center construction slows, if customers adopt alternative power architectures that rely less on our products, or if competitors expand capacity faster than demand grows, we could face order cancellations, pricing pressure and underutilized manufacturing capacity in this segment. Capacity investments we make in anticipation of continued demand may not produce the returns we expect.

We operate in highly competitive markets, and competitive pressures could reduce our market share, pricing and margins.
We compete with large multinational equipment manufacturers, regional and specialized producers, and a growing number of competitors based in lower-cost countries. Some of these competitors may have greater resources in particular product lines or geographies, lower manufacturing costs, more favorable access to government support or financing, or more aggressive pricing strategies. In industrial automation, we also compete with technology companies and software providers that may move more quickly in robotics, artificial intelligence and controls. Competitors may introduce products with better performance, lower total cost of ownership or more advanced digital features, or may use pricing and financing incentives to gain share during weak demand periods. Because cost of revenue represents the large majority of our revenue, even modest price reductions can have a significant effect on our profitability. If we cannot differentiate our products, maintain our dealer network's service advantage and manage our cost structure, our results could be adversely affected.

If we fail to keep pace with technological change, including electrification, autonomy and digital connectivity, our products could become less competitive.
Our industry is undergoing significant technological change. Customers increasingly expect alternative and lower-emission powertrains, battery-electric and hybrid machines, autonomous and semi-autonomous operation, advanced operator assistance, and integrated data platforms that support predictive maintenance and fleet optimization. Developing these technologies requires sustained investment in research and development, software talent and testing infrastructure. The outcome of those investments is uncertain, and they may take longer, cost more or achieve less market acceptance than we anticipate. Competing technologies, industry standards or customer preferences may develop in ways that make our chosen approaches less attractive. New entrants, including well-funded technology companies, may disrupt traditional equipment business models. If we underinvest, invest in the wrong technologies or are slow to commercialize new offerings, we could lose market share. If we invest heavily in technologies that do not achieve broad adoption, we may be unable to recover our costs.

We depend on our independent dealers and distributors, and their performance and financial condition significantly affect our results.
A substantial portion of our sales, and most of our aftermarket parts and service activity, flows through independently owned dealers and distributors. We do not control their operations, and their decisions regarding inventory levels, rental fleet investment, pricing, staffing and capital expenditures directly affect our sales. Dealers may increase or reduce inventory based on their expectations of end-user demand, which can cause our shipments to diverge from retail activity and make our results more volatile. A dealer's financial difficulty, change of ownership, loss of key personnel or failure to meet our service standards could harm customer relationships and our reputation in that territory. Replacing a dealer can be costly, time-consuming and disruptive. Consolidation among dealers could also increase their bargaining power. In addition, competitors may seek to attract our dealers or to build competing distribution in our dealers' territories.

Our international operations subject us to economic, political, currency and trade risks.
We manufacture, source and sell products in many countries across North America, Latin America, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Asia-Pacific region. Our international business is subject to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates, which affect the translated value of our foreign revenue and costs and the relative competitiveness of products manufactured in different countries. It is also subject to changes in tariffs, quotas, export controls, sanctions and other trade restrictions; to local content and investment requirements; to restrictions on the repatriation of earnings; to differing labor regulations; to political instability, armed conflict and terrorism; and to the difficulty of enforcing contracts and protecting intellectual property in certain jurisdictions. Escalating trade tensions between major economies could raise our input costs, disrupt supply chains that cross national borders, provoke retaliatory measures against our products or reduce demand in affected markets. Our efforts to hedge currency exposures or to mitigate trade-related costs may not be effective.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Increases in the cost or reduced availability of raw materials, components and energy could reduce our margins.
Our products require large quantities of steel, cast iron, aluminum, copper and other metals, as well as engines and drivetrain components, hydraulics, electronic controls, semiconductors, batteries, tires and other purchased parts. Because cost of revenue accounts for most of our revenue, our profitability is highly sensitive to changes in material, component, freight and energy costs. Prices for these inputs can rise sharply because of global demand, supply disruptions, trade measures, inflation and geopolitical events. We may be unable to raise our prices quickly enough, or by enough, to offset higher costs, particularly where competitive conditions are intense or where we have committed to fixed prices under existing orders and dealer programs. Some components, including certain electronic and engineered parts, come from a limited number of suppliers or from single sources. Shortages of these components could halt production of finished equipment even when other inputs are available.

Disruptions in our supply chain or manufacturing operations could prevent us from meeting customer demand.
Our manufacturing network relies on a complex, multi-tier supply base and on the timely movement of materials, components and finished goods across regions. Supplier financial distress, quality failures, capacity constraints, labor disputes, natural disasters, pandemics, transportation bottlenecks, cyberattacks or geopolitical events could interrupt the flow of parts to our factories. Many of our products are built at a limited number of facilities that have specialized tooling and skilled workforces, so a significant disruption at any one of them, whether caused by fire, severe weather, equipment failure, a utility outage or another event, could be difficult to remedy quickly. Production interruptions can lead to late deliveries, contractual penalties, lost sales, higher expedited freight and overtime costs, and damage to our relationships with dealers and customers. Our insurance may not fully cover the resulting losses.

Our significant investment in inventories exposes us to the risk of obsolescence, write-downs and reduced cash flow.
Inventories represent the largest component of our current assets. We carry substantial inventories of raw materials, work in process, finished machines and replacement parts to support long production cycles, global distribution and the aftermarket needs of our installed base. If demand weakens unexpectedly, if product transitions or regulatory changes make existing models less marketable, or if we misjudge the mix of products customers want, we could be left with excess or obsolete inventory. In that case we may need to reduce production, offer discounts or record write-downs, any of which would reduce our margins. Building inventory in anticipation of demand also consumes operating cash, which could constrain other uses of capital. Conversely, holding too little inventory could cause us to miss sales opportunities when demand strengthens.

Engineered-to-order projects and long-term service commitments expose us to cost overruns and execution risk.
Certain products in our Power and Energy Systems and Industrial Automation segments, as well as large mining equipment and autonomy deployments, are designed to customer specifications. They often involve long lead times, multi-year delivery schedules, integration with customer facilities, and performance or availability guarantees. Pricing for these arrangements is generally established before all costs are known. If our estimates of material, labor, engineering or commissioning costs prove inaccurate, if inflation rises faster than anticipated, or if technical difficulties or customer-caused delays occur, our margins on these projects could fall or the projects could become loss-making. Failure to meet contractual delivery dates or performance specifications may result in liquidated damages, warranty claims, contract termination and reputational harm. Long-term service agreements likewise depend on assumptions about equipment reliability and maintenance costs that may prove incorrect.

Product quality issues, warranty claims and product liability claims could result in significant costs and damage our reputation.
Our products are complex, operate in demanding environments and are often critical to our customers' operations and safety. Design or manufacturing defects, failures of purchased components or software errors in connected and autonomous systems could cause equipment failures, property damage, personal injury or death. Such events could lead to warranty costs, product recalls, field retrofit campaigns, regulatory investigations and product liability litigation. Cybersecurity incidents affecting our information systems or connected products could disrupt our operations and expose us to liability.
We rely on information technology systems to design and manufacture products, manage our supply chain, process orders and transactions, and communicate with dealers, suppliers and customers. Most of our new machines and power systems also transmit operating data to our cloud-based platforms, and some of our products can be monitored, updated or controlled remotely. These systems and products are potential targets for cyberattacks, ransomware, malicious insiders and other threats, and the threats continue to grow in sophistication. A successful attack could interrupt our manufacturing operations, compromise confidential and proprietary information or personal data, impair the functioning or safety of customer equipment, or allow unauthorized access to customer sites. Any of these could result in significant remediation costs, legal claims, regulatory penalties and loss of customer trust. Our security measures, and those of the third-party service providers on which we depend, may not prevent every incident, and our insurance may not cover all resulting losses.

Our success depends on attracting and retaining skilled employees and on maintaining constructive relations with our workforce.
We employ approximately 126,100 people worldwide. Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Risk Management and Strategy

Overview

Kalondra relies on information technology to design and manufacture its products, operate its supply chain, support dealers and customers, and run its connected equipment platforms. Unauthorized access to, or disruption of, these systems could harm our operations, our customers and our reputation. We therefore maintain an enterprise-wide cybersecurity program designed to identify, assess and manage material risks from cybersecurity threats. The program is aligned with the cybersecurity framework published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology and with other recognized industry standards. It is integrated into our enterprise risk management process, so cybersecurity risks are evaluated alongside the operational, financial, legal and strategic risks that management and the Board of Directors oversee.

Scope of the Program

The program covers our corporate information systems, our engineering and product development environments, and the operational technology that controls production lines, test cells and distribution facilities across our global manufacturing footprint. It also covers the telematics hardware, cloud-based platforms and software that support equipment health monitoring, predictive maintenance and remote diagnostics for our machines and power systems. Because connected products and automation systems can create risks for customers as well as for Kalondra, our product security function participates in product development. It conducts threat modeling, secure coding reviews and vulnerability testing before new hardware and software are released, and it maintains a process for receiving and addressing vulnerability reports after release.

Identifying and Assessing Threats

We identify and assess cybersecurity risks through several complementary activities:

  • continuous monitoring of our networks, endpoints and cloud environments by a security operations function, supported by detection, logging and analytics tools;
  • regular vulnerability scanning and patch management across information technology and operational technology assets;
  • periodic penetration testing and adversary simulation exercises, some performed by our internal teams and some by independent firms;
  • threat intelligence obtained from commercial providers, government agencies and industry information-sharing organizations; and
  • risk assessments of significant business initiatives, including acquisitions, new facilities and new digital offerings.

The results of these activities are used to rank risks according to likelihood and potential impact, to prioritize remediation and to guide investment decisions. We also engage outside advisors to review the maturity of our program against industry practices, and our internal audit function periodically tests selected cybersecurity controls.

Third-Party Risk

We rely on suppliers, service providers, systems integrators, cloud hosting providers and independent dealers, any of which could be a source of cybersecurity risk. Before we engage vendors that will access our systems or handle sensitive data, we evaluate their security practices through questionnaires, documentation reviews and, where appropriate, technical assessments. Our contracts generally require such vendors to maintain appropriate safeguards and to notify us promptly of security incidents. We reassess higher-risk vendors periodically. We also work with our dealer network on security expectations for systems that connect to our dealer portals and parts ordering platforms. We cannot eliminate all risks associated with third parties, but we seek to reduce them to a level consistent with our risk tolerance.

Incident Response and Resilience

We maintain a documented cybersecurity incident response plan. It defines roles, escalation procedures, communication protocols and criteria for assessing the severity of an incident. A cross-functional team that includes information security, information technology, legal, compliance, communications and affected business units manages the response to significant incidents. Each incident is evaluated for potential materiality and disclosure obligations. We conduct tabletop exercises with members of senior management to test the plan, and we update it to reflect lessons learned and changes in the threat environment. Business continuity and disaster recovery plans for critical systems and manufacturing sites are designed to support timely restoration of operations. We also maintain cybersecurity insurance, although that coverage may not be sufficient to cover all losses from an incident.

Training and Awareness

Employees with network access receive mandatory security awareness training on topics such as phishing, data handling and reporting suspicious activity. Employees in engineering, manufacturing, finance and other higher-risk roles receive supplemental training. We run simulated phishing campaigns throughout the year and use the results to target further education.

Governance

Board Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees risks from cybersecurity threats and has delegated primary responsibility for this oversight to its Audit Committee. The Audit Committee receives regular reports from our Chief Information Security Officer on the threat landscape, the status of key security initiatives, results of assessments and testing, significant incidents and the progress of remediation efforts. The full Board receives periodic updates on cybersecurity as part of its review of enterprise risk and technology strategy, including investments in digital and information technology systems. Directors also take part in educational sessions on emerging cybersecurity matters.

Management's Role

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads the cybersecurity program and reports to our Chief Information Officer, who reports to our Chief Executive Officer. The Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience leading information security, risk management and incident response functions at large global industrial and technology organizations. That experience includes securing manufacturing and operational technology environments, and the Chief Information Security Officer holds recognized professional security certifications. The Chief Information Security Officer is supported by a team of security professionals with expertise in security operations, architecture, product security, identity management and governance, risk and compliance.

A management-level Cybersecurity Steering Committee meets regularly to review cybersecurity risks, approve policies and prioritize resources. It includes the Chief Information Officer, the Chief Information Security Officer, the General Counsel, the Chief Financial Officer and leaders of our reportable segments. Under our escalation procedures, significant incidents are reported promptly to the Steering Committee and, as appropriate, to the Audit Committee and the full Board.

Material Effects of Cybersecurity Threats

As of the date of this Annual Report, we are not aware of any cybersecurity incidents that have materially affected, or are reasonably likely to materially affect, our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. Like other industrial companies, we and some of our suppliers and dealers have experienced cybersecurity attacks, and we expect such attempts to continue and to become more sophisticated. For additional discussion of the cybersecurity risks we face, see the risk factors under the heading Risk Factors in this Annual Report.

Overview

Our Business

Kalondra Machinery, Inc. is a Delaware corporation headquartered in San Jose, California. We design, manufacture, sell and support heavy machinery, engines and power generation and energy equipment, and industrial automation systems. We had approximately 126,100 employees at the end of fiscal 2024. We manage our operations through three reportable segments: Construction and Resource Equipment, Power and Energy Systems, and Industrial Automation. We reach customers through independent dealers, distributors, systems integrators and direct sales teams. A large and growing portion of our activity comes from replacement parts, service, remanufacturing and digital subscriptions that support our installed base of equipment.

Our results are influenced by conditions in construction and infrastructure, mining and quarrying, energy production and transmission, data center and commercial construction, and manufacturing and logistics capital spending. They are also affected by the prices and availability of steel, metals, electronic components and other inputs, by dealer inventory decisions, and by foreign currency exchange rates, tariffs and trade policy in the regions where we operate.

Fiscal 2024 Highlights

Fiscal 2024 was a year of higher revenue, expanded margins and strong cash generation. Pricing actions, a favorable mix of products and services, and continued cost discipline more than offset inflation in labor and certain purchased components. Key financial results for fiscal 2024 were:

  • Revenue of $82,769 million, an increase of 6.7% from $77,572 million in fiscal 2023.
  • Gross profit of $24,780 million. Gross margin was 29.9%, compared with 28.9% in fiscal 2023.

  • Net income of $8,951 million, an increase of 13.4% from $7,892 million in fiscal 2023. Net margin was 10.8%, compared with 10.2% in the prior year.
  • Net cash provided by operating activities of $13,653 million.
  • Capital expenditures for property and equipment of $2,210 million.
  • Cash returned to stockholders through dividends of $567 million and share repurchases, net of issuances, of $7,803 million.
  • Cash and cash equivalents of $897 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $866 million at the end of fiscal 2023. Long-term debt was $2,278 million.

Business Environment and Outlook

Demand across our end markets was generally constructive in fiscal 2024, although conditions varied by market. In the Power and Energy Systems segment, investment in data centers and the growing need for reliable backup and prime power supported strong demand for generator sets, gas turbines and related controls. Activity in natural gas compression and transmission was also solid. In the Construction and Resource Equipment segment, public infrastructure programs and nonresidential construction supported demand. Residential construction activity and some dealer inventory adjustments partially offset that support. Mining customers continued to invest in fleet replacement and productivity technologies, including autonomy and fleet management offerings. However, capital programs in some commodity markets became more selective during the year. In the Industrial Automation segment, warehouse and logistics automation remained an area of growth, while spending by certain discrete manufacturers was more cautious because of higher interest rates and uncertain production schedules.

For fiscal 2025, we expect continued demand for power generation and energy equipment and for aftermarket parts and services. We also expect infrastructure-related construction activity to remain supportive. We remain attentive to risks that could affect our results. These include the pace of public infrastructure disbursements, interest rates and their effect on construction and capital equipment purchases, commodity price volatility, changes in tariffs and trade policy, competitive pricing pressure, and the timing of large engineered orders. We intend to keep investing in capacity for high-demand products, in electrification, autonomy and connectivity, and in our services capabilities, while maintaining disciplined control of costs and inventories.

Results of Operations

The following discussion compares our consolidated results for fiscal 2024 with those for fiscal 2023.

Revenue

Revenue for fiscal 2024 was $82,769 million, compared with $77,572 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 6.7%. The increase reflected three principal factors.

First, we realized favorable price on our equipment and aftermarket offerings. Price increases implemented across our product lines, particularly in power generation and large mining equipment, were broadly sustained during the year.

Second, sales volume increased in the Power and Energy Systems segment. Demand from data center developers and operators, commercial and industrial standby power customers, and gas compression applications drove higher shipments of generator sets, reciprocating engines and turbines. Deliveries on engineered-to-order projects also contributed as production capacity expanded during the year.

Third, aftermarket parts, service and remanufacturing revenue grew across our businesses. High utilization of the installed base, increased rebuild and overhaul activity, and greater adoption of extended service agreements and digital subscriptions supported this growth.

Construction and Resource Equipment revenue increased modestly. Price realization and solid demand related to infrastructure and mining fleet replacement were partially offset by lower volumes in certain compact equipment categories and by dealers' efforts to manage inventory levels in some regions. Industrial Automation revenue benefited from warehouse and distribution center automation projects. Softer orders from certain manufacturing customers partially offset that benefit. Foreign currency translation did not materially affect the year-over-year change in consolidated revenue.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $57,989 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $55,129 million in fiscal 2023. Gross profit increased to $24,780 million from $22,443 million, and gross margin improved to 29.9% from 28.9%.

The increase in cost of revenue reflected higher sales volume, particularly in Power and Energy Systems and aftermarket parts, along with continued inflation in wages, freight and certain purchased components such as electronic controls and engine subassemblies. The improvement in gross margin was driven primarily by four factors:

  • price realization that exceeded the increase in manufacturing costs;
  • a favorable mix shift toward higher-margin aftermarket parts, services and power generation products;
  • improved absorption of fixed manufacturing costs at higher production levels; and
  • benefits from lean manufacturing, procurement and logistics initiatives, including improved supplier performance.

Several items partially offset these benefits. Start-up costs associated with capacity expansion in our power generation facilities increased. Material costs for certain commodities, including copper and specialty alloys, were higher. Production inefficiencies arose in some product lines during the first half of the year as we responded to intermittent component shortages. Steel costs were generally more favorable than in the prior year, which partially mitigated other inflationary pressures.

Research and Development

Research and development expense was $1,986 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $1,899 million in fiscal 2023. Research and development expense represented 2.4% of revenue in both years. The higher spending reflected continued investment in alternative and lower-emission powertrains, battery-electric and hybrid machines, autonomous and semi-autonomous operation, and connected data platforms. It also reflected development of higher-output power generation products and next-generation robotics and motion control technologies for the Industrial Automation segment. We expect to continue investing in these technology areas, which we consider important to our long-term competitive position.

Selling, General and Administrative

Selling, general and administrative ("SG&A") expense was $6,406 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $5,876 million in fiscal 2023. SG&A expense represented 7.7% of revenue, compared with 7.6% in the prior year. The increase reflected the following:

  • higher incentive compensation associated with improved financial performance;
  • wage and benefit inflation;
  • increased investment in digital and information technology systems, including cybersecurity and customer-facing platforms;
  • expanded sales and marketing activities supporting our services and power generation businesses; and
  • the inclusion of SG&A expense of businesses acquired during the year.

Management continues to pursue efficiencies in administrative functions while funding initiatives that support growth in services and technology offerings.

Other Operating Expense

Other operating expense was $4,966 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $4,654 million in fiscal 2023. Other operating expense consists primarily of amortization of acquired intangible assets, restructuring and facility rationalization costs, certain product support and dealer program costs not included in cost of revenue, and other operating items. The increase reflected higher amortization of intangible assets associated with acquisitions, costs related to the consolidation of certain manufacturing and distribution facilities, and higher costs related to dealer support programs. Lower costs in other operating categories partially offset these increases.

Operating Income

Operating income was $11,422 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $10,014 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 14.1%. Operating margin expanded to 13.8% from 12.9%. Operating income grew faster than revenue, which increased 6.7%, primarily because gross margin improved. That improvement more than offset higher spending on research and development, SG&A and other operating expense. The improvement reflected price realization, a favorable mix of products and services, and the operating leverage associated with higher volumes, particularly in Power and Energy Systems and aftermarket parts and services.

Interest Expense

Interest expense was $103 million in both fiscal 2024 and fiscal 2023. Interest expense was unchanged year over year because our long-term debt remained relatively stable, at $2,278 million at December 31, 2024 compared with $2,288 million at December 31, 2023, and because a substantial portion of our borrowings carries fixed interest rates.

Income Before Income Taxes

Income before income taxes was $11,319 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $9,911 million in fiscal 2023. The increase reflected the higher operating income described above.

Income Taxes

The provision for income taxes was $2,368 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $2,019 million in fiscal 2023. Our effective tax rate was 20.9% in fiscal 2024, compared with 20.4% in fiscal 2023. The higher effective tax rate was primarily due to a greater proportion of earnings in jurisdictions with higher statutory tax rates. It also reflected the effect of global minimum tax rules that became effective in certain foreign jurisdictions and a smaller benefit from discrete items, including the resolution of tax audits and share-based compensation, than in the prior year. Research and development tax credits and other incentives partially offset these factors.

Our effective tax rate may fluctuate in future periods. Potential causes include changes in the geographic mix of earnings, tax legislation in the United States and other jurisdictions, the implementation of global minimum tax rules, and the outcome of tax examinations.

Net Income

Net income was $8,951 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $7,892 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 13.4%. Net margin improved to 10.8% from 10.2%. Higher operating income drove the increase in net income, partially offset by the higher provision for income taxes and the increase in the effective tax rate.

Critical Accounting Estimates

We prepare our consolidated financial statements in accordance with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. Doing so requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses and the related disclosures. We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions and other assumptions that we believe are reasonable. Actual results could differ from these estimates. We consider the following estimates critical because they require significant judgment and changes in them could materially affect our financial statements. The Audit Committee of our Board of Directors has reviewed these critical accounting estimates.

Revenue Recognition

We recognize most revenue from equipment, engines, parts and components at a point in time, generally when control transfers to dealers, distributors or end customers upon shipment or delivery. For certain engineered-to-order power generation systems and complex automation projects, we recognize revenue over time as we perform. We generally measure progress by the ratio of costs incurred to total estimated contract costs. This method requires us to estimate total contract revenue and costs, including the effects of change orders, liquidated damages, material and labor cost inflation, and the risk of schedule delays. We review these estimates regularly and record changes in the period in which they become known. Anticipated losses on contracts are recognized in full when they become probable.

We also offer dealers and customers sales incentives, including volume rebates, price support and promotional financing programs. We record these incentives as reductions of revenue based on estimates of expected payments. These estimates consider historical experience, program terms, dealer inventory levels and anticipated retail sales. If actual incentive payments differ from our estimates, we adjust revenue in the period in which the difference is identified.

Inventory Valuation

Inventories are stated at the lower of cost or net realizable value. We record reserves for excess, obsolete and slow-moving inventory based on estimates of future demand, product life cycles, planned product transitions and expected selling prices. Given the size of our inventories, which were $15,863 million at December 31, 2024, changes in demand forecasts, technological obsolescence or market conditions could require additional reserves that would increase cost of revenue in the period in which they are recorded.

Goodwill and Intangible Assets

Goodwill was $7,727 million at December 31, 2024. We test goodwill for impairment annually at the reporting unit level, and more frequently if events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying value of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. We estimate the fair value of reporting units primarily with discounted cash flow models, supplemented by market-based approaches. These models require significant assumptions, including revenue growth rates, operating margins, capital expenditures, terminal growth rates and discount rates that reflect the risks of each reporting unit. The fair values of our reporting units exceeded their carrying values in our most recent annual test. However, adverse changes in end-market conditions, technological developments or our operating performance could result in future impairment charges.

We amortize acquired intangible assets with finite lives over their estimated useful lives. We review them for impairment when indicators of impairment are present. The valuation of intangible assets acquired in business combinations requires estimates of future cash flows, customer attrition rates, royalty rates and discount rates.

Product Warranty

We provide standard warranties on our equipment, engines and systems and offer extended warranty and service agreements. We accrue estimated standard warranty costs at the time of sale based on historical claim rates, cost per claim, product mix and known product issues. New products and technologies, including electrified and autonomous equipment, may have limited claims history, which increases the uncertainty of our estimates. If actual claim frequency or repair costs differ from our assumptions, or if we identify a significant product issue requiring a field campaign, we may need to adjust our warranty accruals, and those adjustments could be material.

Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and in numerous foreign jurisdictions. Determining our worldwide provision for income taxes requires significant judgment in three principal areas.

First, we must assess the realizability of deferred tax assets. We record valuation allowances when it is more likely than not that some portion of a deferred tax asset will not be realized. In making this assessment, we consider the expected reversal of existing temporary differences, projected future taxable income, tax planning strategies and recent operating results.

Second, we must evaluate uncertain tax positions. We recognize the benefit of a position only when it is more likely than not to be sustained upon examination, based on its technical merits. The amount recognized is the largest benefit that is more likely than not to be realized upon settlement.

Third, we must interpret complex and evolving tax laws, including global minimum tax rules. Our effective tax rate was 20.9% in fiscal 2024. Changes in tax laws, in the geographic mix of earnings, or in the outcome of tax examinations could cause our effective tax rate and provision for income taxes to differ from our estimates.

Pension and Other Postretirement Benefits

We sponsor defined benefit pension and other postretirement benefit plans in the United States and certain other countries. Measuring the related obligations and expense requires assumptions about discount rates, expected long-term returns on plan assets, rates of compensation increase, mortality and healthcare cost trends. Changes in these assumptions, particularly discount rates and asset returns, could affect our benefit obligations, funded status and future benefit expense. We review these assumptions annually with the assistance of independent actuaries.
