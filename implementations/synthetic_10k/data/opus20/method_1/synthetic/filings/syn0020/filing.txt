PART I


Item 1. Business

Overview

Drazennov Medical, Inc. is a global health care technology company that designs, manufactures and sells medical devices, therapies and related services used by physicians, hospitals and patients to diagnose and treat chronic and acute conditions. Our products address cardiovascular disease, neurological disorders, surgical care, diabetes and patient monitoring. We sell them in developed and emerging markets around the world. Unless the context requires otherwise, references in this Annual Report on Form 10-K to "Drazennov," "the Company," "we," "us" and "our" refer to Drazennov Medical, Inc. and its consolidated subsidiaries.

For the fiscal year ended December 31, 2024, we generated total revenue of $60,579 million, an increase of 3.8% compared with the prior fiscal year. Growth reflected continued adoption of our established therapies, contributions from recently introduced products and expanded access to our technologies in markets outside the United States. We invested 11.4% of revenue in research and development during fiscal 2024. We consider this level of investment central to maintaining a broad and replenishing portfolio of clinically differentiated products.

Our mission is to improve and extend patients' lives by bringing to market technologies that address unmet clinical needs. We aim to deliver meaningful clinical outcomes and economic value to health care systems. Our scale, global manufacturing network and long-standing relationships with clinicians enable us to support patients across the continuum of care, from diagnosis and intervention through long-term disease management.

Drazennov Medical, Inc. is incorporated in the State of Delaware. Our principal executive offices are located in Minneapolis, Minnesota.

Our Strategy

Our strategy centers on four priorities.

First, we seek to lead in innovation. We direct research and development spending toward therapies that can change the standard of care in large and underpenetrated patient populations. We complement internal programs with targeted acquisitions, licensing arrangements and collaborations with academic and clinical institutions.

Second, we seek to expand access to our therapies. We work with health care providers, payers and governments to build the clinical and economic evidence that supports coverage and reimbursement. We also invest in physician training and referral pathways so that more eligible patients are identified and treated.

Third, we pursue operational excellence. We are working to simplify our manufacturing footprint, strengthen supply chain resilience, improve quality systems and apply digital tools to our operations. The goals are higher reliability and more efficient use of capital.

Fourth, we actively manage our portfolio. We periodically evaluate our businesses and product lines against their growth prospects, margin profile and strategic fit. We reallocate resources toward the areas we believe offer the most attractive long-term returns.

Principal Products and Services

Our product portfolio includes implantable and minimally invasive devices, capital equipment, single-use instruments and consumables, software, and services that support the effective use of our technologies. Principal product categories include:

- Cardiac rhythm management devices, including implantable pacemakers, defibrillators, leadless pacing systems and insertable cardiac monitors, together with remote monitoring platforms that transmit device data to clinicians.
- Structural heart and vascular therapies, including transcatheter heart valve systems, coronary and peripheral stents, balloon catheters, aortic stent grafts and devices used to treat venous disease.
- Cardiac ablation solutions, including mapping and navigation systems, ablation catheters and related consumables used to treat atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias.
- Neuromodulation therapies, including spinal cord stimulation systems for chronic pain, deep brain stimulation systems for movement disorders and sacral nerve stimulation systems for bladder and bowel dysfunction.
- Surgical technologies, including powered surgical instruments, advanced energy devices, surgical staplers, hernia repair products, robotic-assisted surgical platforms and surgical navigation and imaging systems.
- Spine and cranial products, including spinal implants, biologics, neurovascular devices used to treat stroke and aneurysms, and cerebrospinal fluid management products.
- Diabetes management products, including insulin pumps, continuous glucose monitoring sensors and transmitters, infusion sets and software that connects these devices into automated insulin delivery systems.
- Patient monitoring and respiratory products, including pulse oximetry sensors, capnography systems, ventilators, airway management devices and hospital monitoring software.

Beyond our devices, we provide clinical education, technical field support, equipment service contracts, data management platforms and outcomes analytics to hospitals and health systems. We are increasingly offering integrated solutions that combine devices with digital tools and services. We believe these solutions help providers improve procedural efficiency and patient outcomes.

Reportable Segments

We manage our business through three reportable segments, organized by therapeutic area and customer call point. Each segment has its own leadership team responsible for research and development, marketing and commercial strategy. Manufacturing, supply chain, quality, regulatory and other functions are shared across the enterprise. Financial information for our segments is in the notes to the consolidated financial statements included elsewhere in this Annual Report on Form 10-K.

Cardiovascular and Vascular Therapies

The Cardiovascular and Vascular Therapies segment develops and sells products that diagnose, treat and monitor heart rhythm disorders, heart failure, structural heart disease, and coronary, aortic and peripheral vascular disease. The segment's principal customers are electrophysiologists, interventional and general cardiologists, cardiac and vascular surgeons, and the hospitals and ambulatory centers where they practice.

The segment's strategy emphasizes less invasive approaches, such as transcatheter valve replacement and leadless pacing. It also seeks to expand our presence in cardiac ablation, which we consider one of the fastest-growing areas of cardiovascular medicine. Remote monitoring and data connectivity are integral to the segment's implantable device offerings and support ongoing patient management after implantation.

Neuroscience and Surgical Solutions

The Neuroscience and Surgical Solutions segment develops and sells therapies for chronic pain, movement disorders, pelvic health, stroke and spinal conditions, along with a broad range of instruments and platforms used in general, thoracic, bariatric, colorectal, gynecologic and orthopedic surgery. Principal customers include neurosurgeons, orthopedic and spine surgeons, neurologists, pain management specialists, general surgeons, and hospital operating room and supply chain leaders.

The segment's strategy focuses on four areas: expanding the use of minimally invasive and robotic-assisted surgery, enlarging the installed base of our surgical platforms, growing the use of consumable instruments that accompany them, and advancing closed-loop neuromodulation technologies that adapt therapy to individual patient signals.

Diabetes and Patient Monitoring

The Diabetes and Patient Monitoring segment develops and sells insulin delivery systems, continuous glucose monitoring products and related software for people with diabetes. It also sells monitoring, respiratory and airway products used in hospitals, ambulatory settings and the home. Customers include endocrinologists, primary care physicians, diabetes educators, anesthesiologists, critical care clinicians, hospitals, and, for diabetes products, patients themselves through pharmacy and durable medical equipment channels.

The segment's strategy centers on automated insulin delivery, interoperability with third-party devices and digital applications, and connected monitoring solutions. These solutions are intended to help clinicians identify patient deterioration earlier and manage care across settings. Because a significant portion of the segment's revenue comes from recurring purchases of sensors, infusion sets and other disposables, the segment places particular emphasis on patient retention and the user experience.

Customers

Our principal customers are hospitals, integrated delivery networks, group purchasing organizations, ambulatory surgery centers, physician practices, clinics, government health care agencies, distributors, pharmacies and, for certain diabetes products, individual patients. In many markets, purchasing decisions involve both the clinicians who select and use our products and the administrators and procurement organizations that negotiate pricing and contract terms.

In the United States, a substantial portion of our sales is made under contracts with group purchasing organizations and integrated delivery networks. These contracts typically set pricing and terms for member facilities but do not obligate members to purchase specified volumes. Outside the United States, many of our customers are public hospitals or government-funded health systems, and sales often occur through tender processes or national procurement arrangements.

Payment for our products ultimately depends in large part on reimbursement from third-party payers. These include government programs such as Medicare and Medicaid in the United States, national health systems in other countries, and private insurers. No single customer accounted for a material portion of our consolidated revenue in fiscal 2024. We do not believe the loss of any single customer would have a material adverse effect on our consolidated results of operations.

We market and sell our products primarily through a direct sales force, supplemented by independent distributors and sales agents in certain geographies and product lines. Our sales representatives and clinical specialists are typically organized by segment and therapy area. Many of them are trained to provide technical support in the procedure room, assist with device programming and follow-up, and educate clinicians on the appropriate use of our products.

Our marketing activities include:

- Physician education and training programs delivered through our training centers, mobile labs, simulation tools and digital learning platforms.
- Participation in medical congresses and scientific meetings.
- Publication of clinical trial results in peer-reviewed journals.
- Patient awareness programs designed to help people recognize symptoms and seek appropriate care.

For diabetes products, we also maintain direct relationships with patients through customer service, product training and digital support channels.

We work with payers and health technology assessment bodies to demonstrate the clinical and economic value of our therapies. Our health economics and market access teams develop evidence on outcomes, cost-effectiveness and total cost of care to support coverage decisions, reimbursement coding and favorable payment rates.

Our marketing and promotional activities are subject to extensive regulation. We maintain compliance programs, policies and training designed to ensure that our interactions with health care professionals are consistent with applicable laws and industry codes of conduct.

Competition

The medical technology industry is highly competitive and characterized by rapid technological change, frequent product introductions and evolving clinical practice. We compete across our segments with large multinational medical device and diversified health care companies, as well as with smaller, specialized companies, many of which focus on a single therapy area or technology. Some competitors have greater resources, broader portfolios or stronger positions than we do in particular product categories or geographies. New entrants, including technology companies and venture-backed start-ups, are increasingly participating in areas such as digital health, diabetes management and robotic surgery.

Competition is based primarily on:

- Clinical outcomes, product performance and safety
- Technological innovation and the strength of clinical evidence
- Ease of use and reliability
- Quality of physician training and technical support
- Breadth of product offering and the ability to provide integrated solutions
- Price, contracting terms and total cost of care
- Reimbursement status and the timing of regulatory approvals

Pricing pressure has intensified as health care systems consolidate, governments implement cost-containment measures and procurement processes increasingly emphasize value. In certain markets, centralized volume-based procurement programs have significantly affected pricing for established product categories. We expect competitive and pricing pressures to continue. We believe our ability to compete depends on continued innovation, the generation of robust clinical and economic evidence, and efficient operations.

Research and Development

Research and development is fundamental to our competitive position. Our research and development organization includes scientists, engineers, clinicians, software developers, data scientists and regulatory professionals located at innovation centers in North America, Europe and Asia. Our activities include basic and applied research, product design and engineering, software and algorithm development, preclinical testing, clinical trials, and the post-market studies required by regulators or undertaken to expand product indications.

We conduct clinical trials around the world, often in collaboration with leading academic medical centers. These trials support regulatory submissions, label expansions and reimbursement decisions. We also pursue external innovation through acquisitions, equity investments, licensing and development partnerships. We continuously evaluate our pipeline and prioritize programs based on unmet clinical need, technical feasibility, regulatory pathway, expected reimbursement and commercial potential.

Manufacturing and Supply Chain

We manufacture most of our products at Company-owned or leased facilities in the United States, Mexico, Puerto Rico, Europe and Asia, and supplement internal capacity with contract manufacturers for certain components and finished goods. Our manufacturing processes include precision machining, electronics assembly, polymer extrusion and molding, sterilization, and cleanroom assembly of implantable and single-use devices.

We purchase raw materials and components from numerous suppliers, including specialized providers of batteries, semiconductors, medical-grade polymers, metals and sterilization services. Some components come from a single source or a limited number of sources, often because of regulatory qualification requirements or technical specifications. We maintain supplier quality programs and safety stock for critical materials, and we pursue dual sourcing where practicable. Nonetheless, disruptions in the supply of certain components, including electronic components and sterilization capacity, could affect our ability to meet customer demand.

Seasonality

Our business is not significantly seasonal overall, although certain patterns recur. Procedure volumes in many markets tend to soften during the summer months in the Northern Hemisphere, particularly in Europe, as patients and clinicians take vacations. In the United States, demand for certain products tends to be stronger in the fourth quarter, when many patients have satisfied annual insurance deductibles and hospitals finalize capital equipment purchases before their budget years close. Sales of capital equipment, such as surgical platforms and monitoring systems, can vary from quarter to quarter depending on the timing of customer purchasing decisions and installations.

Government Regulation

United States Food and Drug Administration

In the United States, our medical devices are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration ("FDA") under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Devices are classified into one of three classes based on risk. Class III devices, which include many of our implantable cardiac and neuromodulation products, generally require premarket approval ("PMA"). A PMA requires valid scientific evidence, typically including clinical trial data, demonstrating reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness. Many of our Class II devices are cleared through the premarket notification process under Section 510(k), which requires a showing of substantial equivalence to a legally marketed predicate device. Novel low-to-moderate-risk devices without a predicate may be classified through the De Novo process. Clinical trials of investigational devices are generally conducted under an investigational device exemption.

After a product is marketed, we remain subject to continuing FDA requirements, including:

- The Quality System Regulation and its successor quality management system requirements
- Establishment registration and device listing
- Medical device reporting of adverse events
- Corrections and removals reporting
- Unique device identification
- Labeling and promotional restrictions

The FDA inspects our facilities periodically. Failure to comply can result in warning letters, product seizures, injunctions, recalls, civil penalties or criminal prosecution.

International Regulation

Outside the United States, our products are subject to regulatory regimes that vary by country. In the European Union, medical devices are regulated under the Medical Devices Regulation, and in vitro diagnostic devices under the In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Regulation. Under these regulations, notified bodies must certify conformity before products may bear the CE mark. These regulations have increased requirements for clinical evidence, post-market surveillance and technical documentation, and they have extended the time and cost of obtaining and maintaining certifications. Other major markets, including Japan, China, the United Kingdom, Canada, Brazil and Australia, maintain their own registration, quality and post-market requirements.

Risks Related to Our Business and Industry

We operate in highly competitive markets characterized by rapid technological change, and our products may be displaced by competing technologies or alternative treatments.
Each of our three reportable segments competes against large diversified medical technology companies, specialized device manufacturers, pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies, and a growing number of start-up and technology companies, some of which have significant financial and engineering resources. Competition in our markets centers on clinical outcomes, product performance, ease of use, breadth of portfolio, service, evidence and price. Competitors may introduce products with superior clinical data, lower cost or more convenient designs. In our cardiac ablation, transcatheter valve, robotic-assisted surgery and continuous glucose monitoring franchises, new product cycles are short and market share can shift quickly.

Advances in pharmaceutical therapies, gene and cell therapies, or digital health tools could also reduce the number of patients who require device-based intervention. For example, new drug classes that slow the progression of cardiovascular disease, obesity or diabetes could reduce demand for some of our implantable and procedural products over time. If we fail to anticipate these shifts, or if competitors bring disruptive technologies to market before we do, our revenue, margins and market position could decline.

Pricing pressure from hospitals, group purchasing organizations, integrated delivery networks and government purchasers could reduce our revenue and margins.
A substantial portion of our sales in the United States is made under contracts with group purchasing organizations and integrated delivery networks that negotiate pricing on behalf of many facilities. Continued consolidation among hospitals and health systems increases the bargaining power of these purchasers, and many are adopting value analysis committees, standardization programs and capitated purchasing models designed to reduce the cost of medical devices. Outside the United States, many of our customers are public hospitals or government-funded systems that purchase through competitive tenders and national procurement programs, including volume-based procurement initiatives that have significantly reduced prices for certain device categories.

Because our cost structure includes substantial fixed manufacturing, research and development, and selling expenses, price reductions that are not offset by volume growth, mix improvement or cost savings would reduce our gross and operating margins. Losing a major group purchasing or tender contract could also lower volumes in a given product line or geography.

Changes in coverage and reimbursement by government and private payers could reduce demand for our products.
Demand for our products depends in large part on whether hospitals, physicians and patients receive adequate reimbursement from third-party payers. These include Medicare and Medicaid in the United States, national health systems in other countries, and private insurers. Payers increasingly require evidence of clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness before granting coverage, and they may limit coverage to narrow patient populations, require prior authorization, or set payment rates that do not adequately compensate providers for using our technologies.

Annual updates to hospital inpatient, outpatient and ambulatory surgery center payment systems, physician fee schedules and durable medical equipment rules can change the economics of procedures that use our products. Competitive bidding for durable medical equipment, or changes in how continuous glucose monitors and insulin pumps are covered under pharmacy or medical benefits, could also affect our Diabetes and Patient Monitoring segment. Adverse changes in reimbursement, or delays in obtaining coverage for new products, could slow adoption and reduce our revenue.

Our growth depends on successfully developing and commercializing new products, and our substantial research and development investment may not produce commercially viable results.
We devote a significant portion of our revenue to research and development, and we expect to keep doing so. Developing medical devices is costly, takes many years and is inherently uncertain. Product candidates may fail to show safety or efficacy in clinical trials, may not receive regulatory approval, may receive approval for narrower indications than expected, or may face manufacturing, reimbursement or market acceptance challenges that limit their commercial potential. A meaningful share of our expected future growth depends on a limited number of pipeline programs in areas such as cardiac ablation, renal denervation, robotic-assisted surgery, closed-loop neuromodulation and automated insulin delivery.

If these programs are delayed, fail or underperform commercially, we may not recover our investment. We may also need to record impairment charges on related capitalized assets, and our revenue growth and profitability could fall below expectations.

Unfavorable clinical trial results, new scientific data or negative publicity about our products or therapy categories could harm our business.
Physician adoption of our products depends heavily on clinical evidence published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at medical congresses. Results from our own studies, competitor studies, registries, independent analyses or regulatory reviews may raise questions about the safety, durability or effectiveness of our products or of an entire therapy category. Such findings, even if later disputed or not confirmed, could lead to reduced use of our products, changes in clinical guidelines, label changes, additional regulatory requirements or product liability claims. Negative media coverage or social media commentary about device safety could also damage our reputation with clinicians, patients and payers.

Our diabetes business depends on patient retention and recurring purchases of disposable products, which exposes it to consumer-oriented competitive risks.
A significant portion of revenue in our Diabetes and Patient Monitoring segment comes from recurring purchases of sensors, infusion sets and other disposables by people with diabetes who use our insulin pumps and glucose monitoring systems. Patients and their physicians can switch to competing systems at the end of a pump warranty period or at any time for sensor-based products. Competition in this market increasingly turns on factors familiar from consumer technology markets, such as device form factor, software features, smartphone integration, interoperability and user experience.

Product performance issues, field actions, customer service problems or supply interruptions could cause patients to discontinue our products, and lost patients may be difficult to win back. Lower patient retention would reduce recurring revenue and could adversely affect the segment's results.

Acquisitions, divestitures and other portfolio actions involve significant risks and may not achieve their intended benefits.
As part of our strategy, we acquire businesses and technologies, enter into licensing and collaboration arrangements, and periodically divest or exit product lines. Acquisitions involve risks including overpaying for targets, failing to integrate operations, quality systems, information technology and personnel, losing key employees or customers of acquired businesses, and assuming unknown liabilities, including product liability and compliance matters. Acquired technologies may not achieve expected clinical or commercial milestones, and contingent consideration arrangements may require significant fair value adjustments.

Divestitures can reduce revenue and scale, create stranded costs, require transition services, and leave us with ongoing indemnification obligations. Any of these outcomes could adversely affect our results of operations and could lead to impairment of the goodwill and intangible assets recorded in connection with past acquisitions.

Risks Related to Our Operations

Quality problems, product defects, recalls or field actions could result in significant costs, regulatory action and loss of customer confidence.
Many of our products are life-sustaining or life-supporting devices that are implanted in the body or used in critical care settings. Design or manufacturing defects, software errors, component failures, labeling deficiencies or improper use could cause patient injury or death. If quality or safety issues arise, we may initiate voluntary recalls or field corrective actions, or regulators may require us to do so. Such actions can involve significant costs for product replacement, physician and patient notification, remediation and additional testing.

Quality issues may also lead to regulatory warning letters, import restrictions, shipment holds, consent decrees, product liability litigation and reputational harm. In the past, quality system observations at individual facilities have required substantial remediation effort across the industry. A serious quality event affecting a major product line could materially reduce our revenue and increase our costs for an extended period.

Disruptions in our manufacturing operations or supply chain could prevent us from meeting customer demand.
We manufacture products at numerous facilities around the world. Certain products or critical components are produced at a single site, or rely on specialized processes such as sterilization that are available from a limited number of internal or third-party facilities. We also obtain certain raw materials, electronic components, batteries, resins and specialty metals from single or limited sources, and qualifying alternative suppliers for regulated medical devices can take considerable time and require regulatory approval.

Natural disasters, extreme weather, fires, power outages, labor disputes, pandemics, geopolitical conflict, export controls, regulatory actions against suppliers or sterilization providers, and supplier financial distress could interrupt our supply. Shortages of semiconductors and other electronic components have affected the medical technology industry in recent years. Supply disruptions could cause backorders, lost sales and market share, and higher costs, and could harm our relationships with customers.

Inflation and increases in the cost of materials, labor, freight and energy could reduce our margins.
Cost of revenue represents the largest component of our cost structure. Increases in the cost of raw materials, components, labor, freight, logistics and energy have affected our operations and may continue to do so. Our ability to pass these increases on to customers is limited by multi-year pricing contracts with group purchasing organizations and health systems, competitive tenders, government-set reimbursement rates and fixed-price arrangements in many markets. Inflation in wages and benefits also affects our substantial research and development, selling, general and administrative expenses. If we cannot offset higher costs through pricing, productivity improvements or supply chain initiatives, our gross margin and operating income could decline.

Cybersecurity incidents affecting our connected devices, information technology systems or third-party service providers could harm patients, disrupt operations and expose us to liability.
Many of our products, including implantable cardiac devices, insulin pumps, glucose monitoring systems, hospital monitoring systems and surgical platforms, connect to networks, mobile applications or cloud-based platforms that store and transmit patient data. We also rely on complex information technology systems to run our manufacturing, supply chain, financial, commercial and research operations. These devices and systems, and those of our suppliers and service providers, face threats from malicious actors, including ransomware attacks, nation-state actors and insider threats, as well as from software vulnerabilities and human error.

A successful attack or vulnerability could compromise device functionality or patient safety, cause unauthorized disclosure of personal health information, disrupt production or order fulfillment, and require costly remediation or product updates. Regulators increasingly require cybersecurity controls as a condition of market authorization, and a significant incident could result in regulatory action, litigation, contractual penalties and lasting damage to our reputation.

Our international operations expose us to economic, political, currency and regulatory risks.
We generate a significant portion of our revenue outside the United States and have manufacturing, research and commercial operations in many countries. International operations expose us to fluctuations in foreign currency exchange rates, which affect reported revenue, costs and the value of our foreign assets. They also expose us to differing regulatory and reimbursement regimes, trade barriers, tariffs and export controls, changes in tax laws, economic instability, and difficulties in enforcing contracts and intellectual property rights.

Geopolitical tensions, sanctions, armed conflicts and shifting trade relationships between major economies could restrict our ability to sell in, source from or manufacture in certain countries. Local procurement policies that favor domestic manufacturers, and pricing initiatives in emerging markets, could further limit our growth opportunities in those regions.

Our success depends on attracting and retaining qualified personnel.
We employ approximately 78,900 people worldwide, and our success depends on our ability to attract, develop and retain skilled employees. These include engineers, software developers, scientists, clinical and regulatory specialists, quality professionals, manufacturing personnel, and sales representatives and clinical specialists who support procedures in the hospital setting. Competition for talent in medical technology, software and data science is intense, and labor market conditions may increase compensation costs. Losing key leaders or specialized employees, or failing to plan management succession effectively, could impair our ability to execute our strategy, maintain customer relationships and comply with regulatory requirements.

Our operational excellence and restructuring initiatives may not achieve their intended savings and could disrupt our business.
We are pursuing initiatives to simplify our manufacturing footprint, consolidate facilities, strengthen supply chain resilience and improve the efficiency of our support functions. These initiatives require significant management attention and upfront costs, including severance, facility closure and asset write-down costs. Transferring production between sites for regulated medical devices requires validation and, in some cases, regulatory approval, and errors in these transitions can cause supply interruptions or quality issues. If we fail to execute these initiatives effectively, or if the expected savings do not materialize or arrive later than planned, our margins and competitive position could suffer.

Item 1C. Cybersecurity

Risk Management and Strategy

Cybersecurity is a core element of our enterprise risk management program. Our business depends on information systems that support research and development, manufacturing, supply chain, commercial operations and financial reporting. Many of our products also contain software or connect to networks, including implantable cardiac devices with remote monitoring capabilities, insulin pumps and continuous glucose monitoring systems that communicate with mobile applications, robotic-assisted surgical platforms and hospital patient monitoring software. Our cybersecurity program therefore addresses both our enterprise information technology environment and the security of the products we deliver to clinicians and patients.

Our program is aligned with recognized industry frameworks, including the cybersecurity framework published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology. It is supported by documented policies, standards and procedures that apply across our segments and functions. We identify and assess cybersecurity risks through periodic enterprise risk assessments, threat intelligence gathering, vulnerability scanning, penetration testing and continuous monitoring of our networks, endpoints and cloud environments. A centralized security operations function monitors security events at all times and coordinates detection and response activities. Identified risks are prioritized based on their potential effect on patient safety, business operations, data confidentiality and regulatory compliance, and remediation is tracked to completion.

Product cybersecurity is integrated into our design control and quality management processes. Our product development teams follow a secure development lifecycle that includes threat modeling, security requirements, code review, software composition analysis and security testing before release. After launch, we monitor our products for emerging vulnerabilities as part of postmarket surveillance. We maintain a coordinated vulnerability disclosure program that allows external researchers and customers to report potential issues, and we communicate with regulators, health care providers and patients when security updates or other mitigations are warranted. Our product security activities are designed to meet the premarket and postmarket cybersecurity expectations of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and comparable authorities in other jurisdictions.

We maintain a cybersecurity incident response plan that defines roles, escalation paths and communication protocols for responding to suspected or confirmed incidents. The plan establishes a cross-functional response team drawn from information security, information technology, legal, privacy, quality, regulatory, communications and the affected business units. It also includes procedures for escalating significant incidents to senior management and the Board of Directors. Incidents are evaluated to determine whether they may be material, and that evaluation involves our legal, finance and disclosure personnel so that any required public disclosure is made in a timely manner. We conduct tabletop exercises with management and technical teams to test and refine the plan, and we incorporate lessons learned from exercises and actual events.

Third parties are a significant source of cybersecurity risk for a company of our scale. We rely on suppliers of components and materials, contract manufacturers, sterilization providers, logistics partners, distributors, software vendors and cloud service providers, many of which have access to our systems or data. Our third-party risk management program assesses the security practices of these parties before engagement and periodically throughout the relationship, with the level of review based on the sensitivity of the data or systems involved and the criticality of the service. We include security and data protection requirements in our contracts, require prompt notification of security incidents and monitor certain vendors for changes in their risk profile. When we acquire businesses, we assess their cybersecurity posture during due diligence and integrate them into our security program according to a risk-based plan.

All employees and contractors with access to our systems must complete cybersecurity and data privacy training when they join and on a recurring basis afterward. We run simulated phishing campaigns to reinforce awareness and provide targeted training to personnel in higher-risk roles, including software engineers, system administrators and employees who handle financial transactions or sensitive patient information.

We engage independent assessors, consultants and auditors to evaluate the maturity and effectiveness of our cybersecurity program, test our defenses and review our compliance with applicable standards. Their findings inform our program priorities and investment decisions.

As of the date of this Annual Report on Form 10-K, we are not aware of any cybersecurity threats, including those resulting from any previous cybersecurity incidents, that have materially affected or are reasonably likely to materially affect our business strategy, results of operations or financial condition. However, threat actors continue to develop more sophisticated techniques, and health care organizations and medical device manufacturers remain frequent targets. We cannot guarantee that our safeguards will prevent or detect every incident. For further discussion of these risks, see the risk factors in Part I, Item 1A, Risk Factors, that address information security, the cybersecurity of our products and the protection of personal and health information.

Governance

Management's Role

Our Chief Information Security Officer leads our cybersecurity program and is responsible for assessing and managing cybersecurity risk across the enterprise, including threat detection, incident response and recovery. The Chief Information Security Officer reports to our Chief Information Officer, who is a member of our executive leadership team. Our Chief Information Security Officer has extensive experience in information security, including leadership roles in health care, life sciences and other highly regulated industries, with responsibility for security operations, security architecture, risk management and regulatory compliance. The Chief Information Security Officer holds recognized professional certifications in information security and is supported by a team of security professionals with expertise in areas such as threat intelligence, identity and access management, cloud security and operational technology security.

Product cybersecurity is managed by a dedicated product security organization within our research and development and quality functions. It works closely with the Chief Information Security Officer under shared standards and coordinated incident response procedures. A cross-functional cybersecurity steering committee, which includes senior leaders from information technology, product security, legal, privacy, quality, regulatory affairs, operations and finance, meets regularly to review the threat environment, program performance, significant risks and remediation priorities.

Board Oversight

Our Board of Directors oversees our overall risk management process, including risks from cybersecurity threats, directly and through its committees. The Science, Technology and Quality Committee of the Board has primary responsibility for overseeing cybersecurity risk, including the security of our information systems and products. The Audit Committee oversees the effect of cybersecurity matters on our internal control over financial reporting and our disclosure controls and procedures.

The Chief Information Security Officer and Chief Information Officer brief the Science, Technology and Quality Committee regularly throughout the year. Their reports cover the threat landscape, the results of risk assessments and independent reviews, the status of key security initiatives, product security matters, relevant legal and regulatory developments, and significant incidents affecting us or our industry. The committee chair reports on these matters to the full Board, and the full Board receives a direct briefing on cybersecurity from management at least annually. Under our incident response plan, management notifies the committee and, as appropriate, the full Board of significant cybersecurity incidents between scheduled meetings.

Business Environment and Trends

Several factors shaped our performance in fiscal 2024 and are likely to remain relevant to our results.

Procedure volumes in most of our major markets remained healthy, supported by an aging population, a rising prevalence of chronic disease and continued clinical preference for less invasive treatment. Demand was particularly favorable in cardiac ablation, transcatheter structural heart therapies, neuromodulation and automated insulin delivery. In these areas, new product introductions and expanded indications broadened the population of eligible patients.

Pricing remained a headwind in several product categories. Hospital and health system consolidation in the United States continued to strengthen the negotiating position of group purchasing organizations and integrated delivery networks. Outside the United States, competitive tenders and volume-based procurement programs continued to put pressure on prices for certain mature device categories. Favorable product mix, volume growth and cost reduction initiatives largely offset these pressures during the year.

Inflation in labor, freight, electronic components and certain raw materials moderated compared with the prior year but stayed above historical levels in some categories. We continued to invest in supply chain resilience, including dual sourcing of critical components and additional sterilization capacity. These investments added cost in the near term, and we believe they reduce the risk of future disruptions.

We continued to manage our portfolio actively. During fiscal 2024 we completed acquisitions intended to strengthen our positions in higher-growth therapy areas, which is reflected in the increase in goodwill on our consolidated balance sheet and in investing cash outflows. Results of Operations

The following discussion compares our consolidated results of operations for fiscal 2024 with fiscal 2023. All amounts are in millions of U.S. dollars unless otherwise noted.

Revenue

Revenue was $60,579 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $58,356 million in fiscal 2023, an increase of 3.8%. Higher unit volumes across all three reportable segments drove the increase, together with favorable product mix from recently introduced products. Pricing pressure in certain mature categories and markets partially offset these gains.

In our Cardiovascular and Vascular Therapies segment, cardiac ablation solutions made the largest contribution to growth. Expanding physician adoption of our mapping and navigation systems and catheter technologies drove these results. Transcatheter valve systems and leadless pacing devices also grew as clinicians continued to shift toward less invasive procedures. Remote monitoring services added recurring revenue associated with our installed base of implantable devices. Pricing pressure in coronary stents and certain peripheral vascular products partially offset this growth.

In our Neuroscience and Surgical Solutions segment, growth reflected continued demand for spinal cord stimulation systems, including our closed-loop technologies, and increased use of neurovascular devices for the treatment of stroke. Placements of our robotic-assisted surgical and surgical navigation platforms enlarged the installed base. Higher utilization of related single-use instruments and consumables contributed recurring revenue. Revenue from surgical staplers, advanced energy devices and hernia repair products grew modestly, reflecting stable procedure volumes and competitive pricing in these established categories.

In our Diabetes and Patient Monitoring segment, revenue growth reflected expanded adoption of our automated insulin delivery systems and continuous glucose monitoring sensors. Recurring purchases of disposables by our growing installed base of users also contributed. Improvements in patient retention, supported by software updates and enhanced customer service, benefited the segment. Patient monitoring and respiratory product revenue grew modestly as hospital capital spending stabilized and demand for pulse oximetry sensors and capnography consumables remained steady.

Geographically, revenue grew in both the United States and international markets. Outside the United States, we expanded access to our therapies in several emerging markets, and new product approvals in developed markets supported adoption. Volume-based procurement programs and competitive tenders reduced selling prices for certain products in specific international markets. Changes in foreign currency exchange rates also affected reported revenue. Item 7A discusses our exposure to foreign currency.

No single customer accounted for a material portion of our consolidated revenue in fiscal 2024 or fiscal 2023.

Cost of Revenue and Gross Margin

Cost of revenue was $34,924 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $33,744 million in fiscal 2023. The increase primarily reflected higher unit volumes. Cost of revenue includes materials, direct labor, manufacturing overhead, freight, warranty costs, royalties and the amortization of certain manufacturing-related assets.

Gross profit was $25,655 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $24,612 million in fiscal 2023. Gross margin was 42.3% in fiscal 2024, compared with 42.2% in fiscal 2023. The modest improvement reflected:

- Favorable product mix, driven by growth in higher-margin therapies such as cardiac ablation, transcatheter valves, neuromodulation and automated insulin delivery.
- Manufacturing efficiencies and procurement savings from our operational excellence programs, including site consolidation and automation.
- Better absorption of fixed manufacturing costs on higher production volumes.

These benefits were largely offset by:

- Pricing pressure in certain mature product categories and international tender markets.
- Continued elevated costs for labor, electronic components and certain raw materials.
- Costs to strengthen supply chain resilience and quality systems, including qualification of alternative suppliers and investment in sterilization capacity.
- Lower margins on certain products from recently acquired businesses during their initial integration period.

Research and development expense was $6,882 million in fiscal 2024, compared with $6,687 million in fiscal 2023. As a share of revenue, research and development was 11.4% in fiscal 2024, compared with 11.5% in fiscal 2023.

Spending also increased because of a larger number of clinical trials, including studies designed to support expanded indications and to generate the clinical and economic evidence that payers require for coverage decisions. Research and development activities of recently acquired businesses also contributed to the increase. Spending declined slightly as a share of revenue because revenue grew faster than research and development expense. Prioritization of programs with the most attractive clinical and commercial prospects also contributed, along with the discontinuation of certain lower-priority projects.

We expect to continue investing a significant portion of our revenue in research and development, consistent with our strategy of leading in innovation.

- Expanded customer service and digital support capabilities for our diabetes patient base.

Our debt agreements contain customary covenants, and we were in compliance with all such covenants at December 31, 2024. We seek to maintain a capital structure and credit profile that preserve access to the debt markets on favorable terms. We manage the maturity profile of our debt to avoid concentrations of maturities in any single year. Item 7A discusses our exposure to changes in interest rates.

Critical Accounting Estimates

We prepare our consolidated financial statements in accordance with accounting principles generally accepted in the United States. This requires management to make estimates and assumptions that affect the reported amounts of assets, liabilities, revenue and expenses and the related disclosures. We base our estimates on historical experience, current conditions and other assumptions we believe are reasonable. Actual results could differ from these estimates. We consider an accounting estimate critical when it requires significant judgment about matters that are highly uncertain at the time the estimate is made, and when a reasonably likely change in the estimate could materially affect our financial condition or results of operations. Our significant accounting policies are described in the notes to our consolidated financial statements.

Revenue Recognition and Variable Consideration

We recognize revenue when control of our products or services transfers to the customer. For most products this occurs on shipment or delivery. For consigned inventory it occurs when the product is used in a procedure. For service contracts and remote monitoring arrangements, we recognize revenue over the service period. Certain arrangements include multiple performance obligations, such as capital equipment combined with consumables, software and service. For these arrangements, we allocate the transaction price based on relative standalone selling prices, which may require estimation when such prices are not directly observable.

Our gross sales are reduced by variable consideration, including rebates under group purchasing organization and integrated delivery network contracts, distributor chargebacks, volume and tiered pricing discounts, product returns and, for certain diabetes products, payer and pharmacy rebates. We estimate these amounts using contract terms, historical experience, expected sales volumes, channel inventory levels and payer mix. These estimates require judgment, particularly for new contracts or changes in payer arrangements. If actual results differ from our estimates, we adjust revenue in the period in which the difference becomes known.

Goodwill and Intangible Assets

Goodwill was $52,295 million at December 31, 2024, compared with $48,618 million at December 31, 2023, and represents a significant portion of our total assets of $146,044 million. We test goodwill for impairment annually and whenever events or changes in circumstances indicate that the carrying amount of a reporting unit may exceed its fair value. We generally estimate the fair value of reporting units using discounted cash flow models. These models require significant assumptions about future revenue growth, operating margins, capital requirements, terminal growth rates and discount rates.

We record acquired intangible assets, including developed technology, customer relationships, trade names and in-process research and development, at fair value on acquisition. We amortize finite-lived intangible assets over their estimated useful lives. We review these assets for impairment when indicators arise, and we test indefinite-lived intangible assets, including in-process research and development, at least annually. The following could result in impairment charges:

- Clinical trial failures or regulatory delays.
- Adverse changes in reimbursement.
- Competitive developments.
- Shortfalls against projected results.

Business Combinations and Contingent Consideration

When we acquire a business, we allocate the purchase price to the assets acquired and liabilities assumed based on their estimated fair values. Estimating the fair value of intangible assets requires significant judgment about projected cash flows, technology obsolescence, customer attrition and discount rates. Many of our acquisitions include contingent consideration payable on the achievement of clinical, regulatory or commercial milestones. We remeasure these liabilities at fair value each reporting period, using probability-weighted assessments of milestone achievement and discount rates. Changes in fair value are recognized in other operating expense and may be significant.

Income Taxes

We are subject to income taxes in the United States and numerous foreign jurisdictions. Determining our provision for income taxes and our deferred tax assets and liabilities requires significant judgment. This includes interpreting complex tax laws, assessing the realizability of deferred tax assets, evaluating transfer pricing arrangements and applying new rules such as global minimum tax regimes. We recognize the benefit of uncertain tax positions only when it is more likely than not that the position will be sustained on examination, based on its technical merits. We periodically reassess these positions, and the resolution of examinations or changes in tax law could materially affect our effective tax rate.

Product Liability, Litigation and Other Contingencies

In the ordinary course of business, we are involved in product liability claims, intellectual property disputes, commercial litigation and governmental investigations. We record a liability when a loss is probable and the amount can be reasonably estimated. Assessing the likelihood of loss and estimating its amount require significant judgment, particularly for matters involving large numbers of claimants, novel legal theories or early-stage proceedings. We reassess these estimates as facts develop, and the ultimate outcome of these matters may differ materially from the amounts we have accrued.

Inventory Valuation

We state inventories at the lower of cost or net realizable value. We estimate reserves for excess, obsolete and expiring inventory based on forecasted demand, product life cycles, shelf life and planned product transitions. Because our products are subject to rapid technological change and frequent new product introductions, demand forecasts require judgment. Unanticipated changes in demand, product recalls or accelerated product transitions could require additional inventory write-downs.
